You already know plain-call and put payoffs and the distinction between a payoff and its earlier model value. A **barrier option** adds a condition on a specified trigger event. The event includes an observation window, schedule, price source and equality rule; the ending price alone need not identify it.

For this introductory chapter, use **European settlement, continuous monitoring throughout the stated life, equality counting as a trigger and zero rebate**, unless a case says otherwise. Under those terms, matched knock-in and knock-out payoffs partition a vanilla. Their prices are at most its price under the same positive valuation assumptions; a strict discount needs additional positive-value paths. The later [monitoring and gap-risk lesson](/exotic-options-and-structured-products/barrier-monitoring-and-gap-risk) changes those terms explicitly.

## The four flavours (knock-in vs knock-out, up vs down)

**Analogy.** Two everyday switches, opposite wiring. A **knock-in** is the loyalty coupon that *only activates once you cross a spending threshold* — useless in your wallet until you've spent enough, then suddenly worth real money. A **knock-out** is the engine warranty that's *valid right up until the day you redline the tachometer* — fully alive, protecting you, until one reckless moment voids it for good. One needs an event to *wake up*; the other dies the moment an event *happens*.

The switch analogy concerns payoff rights, not the contract’s current market price. An unactivated knock-in can already have positive value because future activation remains possible.

**The definitions.**

- A **knock-out** has the vanilla settlement right while its declared event has not occurred. Once the event occurs, that vanilla payment is extinguished permanently under the stated zero-rebate terms.
- A **knock-in** pays the vanilla amount at expiry only if the declared event occurred. Before activation it remains a contingent contract and can have positive market value; if the observation window ends without activation, its zero-rebate settlement is zero.

Now cross each of those with the **direction** of the barrier relative to today's price. An **up-barrier** sits *above* the spot; a **down-barrier** sits *below* it. Two switch types × two directions × \{call, put\} gives the full family of **eight** named combos. The four switch-and-direction labels:

Stick a call or a put on each cell and you get the eight you'll see quoted: up-and-out call, up-and-in call, down-and-out put, down-and-in put, and the rest. The name reads in order — **direction, switch, right** — so a "down-and-in put" is a put that *wakes up* if the stock *falls* to the barrier, and an "up-and-out call" is a call that *dies* if the stock *rallies* to the barrier.

A **rebate** is a separately specified payment associated with a barrier outcome. Its trigger, amount and payment date belong to the contract. A knock-out rebate might be paid when the event occurs or deferred to another date; a knock-in can also have a payment if it never activates. Neither amount nor timing follows from the word “barrier”. This chapter keeps every rebate at zero unless explicitly stated.

## In–out parity (the elegant identity)

Take European knock-in and knock-out contracts with the same underlying, strike, barrier event, observation window, fixings, equality rule, prior activation history, expiry, currency and settlement terms. Keep rebates at zero. On each path, ask whether that common event occurred:

- If it **did**, the knock-out died but the knock-in woke up — so the **knock-in is alive** and the knock-out is worthless.
- If it **didn't**, the knock-out stayed alive and the knock-in never activated — so the **knock-out is alive** and the knock-in is worthless.

Either way, **exactly one of the pair is live**, and the live one behaves like a plain vanilla. So owning *both* at once means you always hold exactly one live vanilla, no matter what the path does. That's the identity:

> **Knock-in + Knock-out = Vanilla** (same European payoff, event and settlement; zero rebates).

Rearrange it and you get the pricing shortcut desks actually use: **Knock-in = Vanilla − Knock-out.** Price any two of the three and the third is free.

**Worked example.** Say the plain vanilla call is worth **\$8**, and the corresponding **up-and-out** call is quoted at **\$3**. Then the **up-and-in** call must be:

The matched cash-flow identity needs no volatility model. With illustrative frictionless quotes of 8 for vanilla, 3 for out and 6 for in, buying the vanilla and selling the pair receives 1 initially while matching terminal obligations. A practical trade also requires executable bid–ask, financing and counterparty terms; the arithmetic is not a promise of an available riskless profit.

Now make the logic physical. The island below is an **up-and-out / up-and-in call**, strike \$100, up-barrier \$120, with two toggles: knock-out vs knock-in, and a path that *touches* the barrier vs one that *doesn't*. Walk through all four combinations deliberately — each click is a teaching moment:

1. **Knock-out + "stays below barrier".** The stock drifts up to 115, never tags 120, finishes in the money — the knock-out is **alive** and pays its intrinsic value. Good, that's just a vanilla so far.
2. **Knock-out + "touches barrier".** The stock spikes through 120 early, then *settles back to 110, still in the money* — yet the readout says **knocked out, worthless**. That's the gut-punch: it was right about direction and collected nothing, killed by a touch it later "recovered" from.
3. **Knock-in + "stays below barrier".** Same gentle drift to 115, ends in the money — but the knock-in **never woke up**, so it pays **nothing**. Being in the money is irrelevant if the switch never flipped.
4. **Knock-in + "touches barrier".** The stock tags 120, activating the option, then settles at 110 — now it's **alive** and pays intrinsic.

Then notice the parity with your own eyes: on the **breaching** path the knock-in is the live one and the knock-out is dead; on the **non-breaching** path it's the reverse. The selected leg pays the vanilla amount; if that amount is zero, both cash payments are zero. Add them and you reconstruct the same payoff.

## Zero-rebate payoff bounds and a down-and-out example

With matching European zero-rebate terms, the in/out payments are nonnegative and sum to the vanilla. Each value is therefore at most the vanilla under a common positive valuation. It is strictly lower only when the other component owns positive-value payoff paths. A trigger with positive probability alone is not enough if its associated intrinsic payoff is always zero.

A down-and-out call gives up the call payoff on triggered downside paths, including paths that later recover. A knock-in instead requires activation before paying. Those are the rights being compared; a lower price is not a free improvement to the same protection.

## Payoff boundaries, Greeks and the reverse knock-out

A settlement diagram and an earlier option-value curve are different objects. For an up-and-out call with strike 100 and upper barrier 120, a final fixing at 119 gives payoff 19, while an inclusive fixing at 120 gives zero, assuming no earlier trigger. This terminal payoff has a jump. A barrier above the call strike is commonly called a **reverse knock-out** because positive intrinsic value can be removed by that upper trigger.

Before expiry, continuously monitored zero-rebate value under a nondegenerate continuous diffusion can instead approach zero continuously at the absorbing barrier. Positive remaining time and volatility matter. Delta and gamma can be large or change rapidly with price, time and monitoring terms; the terminal jump does not prove that every earlier value curve jumps or that every Greek is infinite.

The distinction also prevents a bad example: with continuous monitoring, an up-and-out call whose barrier is at or below its strike cannot survive and finish strictly in the money. The terminal payoff on surviving paths is then zero. Merely calling such a contract “cheap and gentle” would hide its payment condition.

<Callout variant="warning" title="A local sensitivity does not supply a fill">
Discrete observations, expiry, market jumps, trading costs and executable quotes affect a hedge. A contract can trigger when the market jumps across a level without trading there. Stocks or other options may change the hedge exposure, but neither a terminal payoff sketch nor the name of a hedging instrument guarantees replication. A complete hedge needs a signed option, share and cash ledger under explicit model and execution assumptions.
</Callout>

## When to use them

Barrier features allocate payoff rights by a path event. They can appear in directional exposures, contingent protection and structured notes. A down-and-out call, for example, surrenders the call payoff on triggered paths, including paths that later recover. Whether that trade-off fits a particular exposure requires the actual terms and a full risk/cost comparison.

## Putting it together

A barrier contract adds a specified event to a payoff. This chapter used continuous inclusive monitoring and zero rebates; other terms can change the event or cash. Matched European in–out legs partition the same payoff and their values sum under common valuation assumptions. Keep that identity separate from a terminal payoff jump, a pre-expiry value curve and a hedge’s executable cash flows.
