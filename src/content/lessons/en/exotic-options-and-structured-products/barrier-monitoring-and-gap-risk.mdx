A stock starts at 100, falls to 85 during the year and finishes at 110. A down-and-out call can pay 10 or zero on that same complete path. The difference can be the contract's observation schedule: a level that matters at every instant is a different condition from a level checked only at named fixings.

We will specify that condition before calculating a payoff or a hedge. All paths and cash amounts are invented. Options are European, with cash settlement at the stated expiry unless a rebate has a separately specified payment date. There are no fees, funding restrictions or credit losses in the payoff identities. Those exclusions do not make actual trading or issuer promises risk-free.

## Monitoring defines which observations count

A **fixing** is a specified observation used by the contract to determine an event or payment. Its date, time, price source and any disruption provisions matter. **Continuous monitoring** applies the barrier condition throughout its stated window; **discrete monitoring** applies it only at the specified fixings. A price crossing outside a discrete schedule is not automatically a contractual trigger.

Consider a call with strike $K=100$, down barrier $H=90$ and expiry $T=1$ year. Plain-call settlement is $F=\max(S_T-K,0)$. We initially count equality as a trigger, $S\le H$. The following is a **complete constructed path**, with linear interpolation between all its nodes:

The continuously monitored contract first touches 90 at time 0.50. Its knock-out is extinguished, so it pays zero. Its matching knock-in activates and pays $110-100=10$ at expiry.

The discrete version observes only the start and expiry: 100 and 110. Neither satisfies $S\le90$. Its knock-out pays 10; its knock-in never activates and pays zero. The minimum of the full path is 85, while the minimum of the contractual fixing prices is 100. These are different measurements.

<BarrierMonitoringPaths client:visible  />

A second path that never falls below 98 also ends at 110. It gives the same plain-call payoff, while the continuously monitored barrier outcomes differ. In real data, having only the two endpoints would not reveal which intermediate path occurred. Connecting observed endpoints with a straight line would be an additional assumption, not evidence that no crossing happened.

[Broadie, Glasserman and Kou](https://www.columbia.edu/~sk75/mfBGK.pdf) distinguish continuous from discrete monitoring under their pricing model. Our first example needs no stochastic model: all intermediate nodes and the interpolation rule have been stipulated.

### When to use it

Read the observation window and fixing source before interpreting a barrier level. Keep a numerical simulation grid separate from contractual dates: adding intermediate simulation points can improve a continuous-contract calculation, but adding observations to the contract changes its payoff rule.

## Equality can change activation without changing a call's cash payment

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A down-and-in call with strike 100 activates when the final price equals its inclusive barrier 90. Must activation produce a positive payment?" text="No. The event occurs, but call intrinsic value is max(90−100,0)=0. Activation and positive settlement are separate." />

“Below 90” and “at or below 90” are different rules. Write the comparison explicitly. Do not fill in a missing equality convention from a drawing or the word “touch”. An ideal diffusion model may assign zero probability to a particular discrete fixing price, but actual contracts and rounded price records still need a rule.

Take a different constructed path whose monitored expiry price is **exactly 90**, with no earlier observation below that level. Under $S\le90$, the down-and-in event occurs. Under $S<90$, it does not.

For the call with $K=100$, however, $F=\max(90-100,0)=0$. Activation changes, yet settlement is zero under either equality convention. An active option need not be in the money.

<BarrierMonitoringPaths client:visible path="equality" monitoring="fixings" />

To see the cash consequence separately, use a **put** with the same strike 100 and terminal price 90. Its plain payoff is $\max(100-90,0)=10$. With no earlier trigger, a down-and-in put pays 10 under the inclusive rule and zero under the strict rule. Both price and rule are needed to determine the result.

### When to use it

Record equality, initial observation, final observation and any prior activation history as contract inputs. Distinguish “the event occurred” from “this payoff is positive”. Numerical tolerances used by software must implement the declared convention, rather than silently redefining the legal observation rule.

## A rebate has an event, an amount and a payment date

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"A 2 USD rebate is paid at year 0.5 rather than year 1, with an assumed positive 5% effective annual discount rate. What changes?"}
  options={[{"text": "Its common-date present value, even though the nominal payment stays 2", "correct": true}, {"text": "Its nominal amount automatically doubles to 4 USD"}, {"text": "Its discounted value stays identical for every positive rate"}, {"text": "Its event probability becomes known merely from the payment date"}]}
  explanation={"The path PV is 2/1.05^0.5 rather than 2/1.05. Event probability and the market price of the contingent claim remain separate."}
/>

A **rebate** is a separately specified payment associated with a barrier outcome. Its trigger, amount and date are contractual terms. For example, software may implement a knock-out rebate at the trigger and an unactivated knock-in rebate at expiry; [MathWorks documents those choices for its instrument](https://www.mathworks.com/help/fininst/fininstrument.barrier.html). Those implementation conventions are not a rule for every contract.

Our invented knock-out pays a rebate of 2 USD if its continuously monitored barrier triggers. On the complete dip path, that happens at $\tau=0.50$ and expiry is $T=1$. Compare payment at the trigger with payment at expiry, using an explicitly assumed **5% effective annual discount rate**:

$$
\operatorname{PV}(R\text{ at }0.50)=\frac{2}{1.05^{0.50}}=1.95180015,
\qquad
\operatorname{PV}(R\text{ at }1)=\frac{2}{1.05}=1.90476190.
$$

The earlier payment has greater present value under this positive rate. That is a common-date comparison of cash on a specified path. It is not the current market price of a contingent rebate: a price also requires a pricing model for whether and when its event occurs.

<BarrierParityCashLedger client:visible rebate={2} />

Changing the rebate payment date changes the discounted cash, even when its nominal amount and trigger stay the same. If the fixing-only knock-out never triggers on the shown path, its trigger rebate is zero. Do not add a rebate to a surviving plain payoff unless the contract actually says to do so.

### When to use it

List rebate cash separately from the option's intrinsic payoff. State what happens if there is no trigger, and distinguish a payment at first trigger from one deferred to expiry or another settlement date. The chosen discount rate is a valuation assumption, not a promised investment return.

## In–out parity partitions one identical event

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"Two barrier options share the underlying, strike, barrier number and expiry. Is that enough to use plain in–out parity?"}
  options={[{"text": "No: they must also partition the same monitoring/equality event and matching cash terms", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes: every contract with a common barrier level has identical event timing"}, {"text": "Yes: rebates and payment dates cancel regardless of their amounts"}, {"text": "No: in–out parity needs independence of trigger and final payoff"}]}
  explanation={"Pathwise partition needs the same event and payoff terms, with rebates separately accounted for. It does not need independence."}
/>

Let $E$ be one fully specified trigger event, including its observation window, fixing schedule and equality rule. Let $F$ be the same nonnegative European payoff at the same settlement date. With zero rebates,

$$
F_{\mathrm{in}}=F\mathbf1_E,\qquad
F_{\mathrm{out}}=F(1-\mathbf1_E),\qquad
F_{\mathrm{in}}+F_{\mathrm{out}}=F.
$$

The indicator $\mathbf1_E$ is 1 when the event occurs and 0 otherwise. Exactly one event condition selects the payoff, but **both cash payments can be zero if F=0**. You do not need independence, a crossing probability or a particular volatility model for this pathwise identity.

### A shared level is not enough

On the dip path, continuous monitoring triggers and start/expiry monitoring does not. A continuous knock-in pays 10. A fixing-only knock-out also pays 10. Pairing them gives **20**, not the vanilla's 10, because their events are different.

Reverse the mismatch: a fixing-only knock-in pays zero and a continuous knock-out pays zero. The pair gives **zero**, again not 10. Match both contracts to the same event and the total returns to 10.

<BarrierParityCashLedger client:visible rate={0} />

If the matched knock-out also pays rebate $R$ on the trigger, the combined cash flows are the vanilla cash **plus the rebate on that event**, at its stated date. A plain in–out price identity must therefore account for those additional cash flows. Mixing a rebate paid at time 0.50 with expiry cash without discounting is another separate error.

### From pathwise payoff to price

When the cash flows really match, a common linear valuation gives the corresponding price identity. For example, zero-rebate European contracts with the same event, currency, underlying, strike, expiry and settlement terms satisfy $V_{\mathrm{in}}+V_{\mathrm{out}}=V_{\mathrm{plain}}$ under the same pricing and credit assumptions. An illustrative vanilla price 8 and knock-out price 3 imply knock-in price $8-3=5$.

With nonnegative payoffs and matching zero-rebate terms, each component's value is **at most** the vanilla value. Strict discounts require positive value on the paths excluded by that component; a barrier feature alone does not establish strict inequality in every parameter configuration. Executable trading comparisons also need bid–ask, financing and counterparty terms.

### When to use it

Use parity to check a matched decomposition, after checking the actual event and payment terms. A common barrier number printed on two term sheets is insufficient. Rebate and monitoring differences belong in the cash-flow comparison before any pricing shortcut.

## A terminal payoff jump is not a universal pre-expiry price jump

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A final-fixing up-and-out call pays 19 at S=119 and zero at S=120. Does that establish a jump in every earlier continuous-monitoring option-value curve?" text="No. The plotted function is terminal settlement. With continuous diffusion, positive remaining time and zero rebate, the pre-expiry value can approach zero continuously at the absorbing boundary." />

Consider an up-and-out European call with strike 100 and upper barrier 120. For a **final fixing with no prior trigger**, and equality counting as knock-out, its settlement function is

$$
F_{\mathrm{out}}(s)=\max(s-100,0)\,\mathbf1_{\{s<120\}}.
$$

At terminal price 119 it pays 19. At 119.99 it pays 19.99. At 120 it pays zero. This final-fixing payoff has a jump. Under a strict $s>120$ trigger instead, the contract pays 20 at exactly 120, then zero immediately above it. Equality decides which point belongs to which side.

<BarrierTerminalBoundary client:visible  />

An upper barrier above the call's strike creates positive vanilla intrinsic value just below the barrier; this configuration is often called a **reverse knock-out**. That observation describes the payoff feature. It does not prove that the option's value at every earlier time remains high until it suddenly jumps to zero.

For a continuously monitored, zero-rebate up-and-out call under a nondegenerate continuous diffusion such as constant-volatility GBM with positive volatility and positive time remaining, the boundary is absorbing. Value can approach zero continuously as the current price approaches the barrier from below. There is then no universal finite jump in the pre-expiry value at first touch, though sensitivities and discrete hedging can still be difficult.

A useful bound makes the distinction concrete. On surviving paths, $S_T<120$, so the call payoff is at most $120-100=20$. With zero discounting and a stipulated pricing-measure survival probability $p_{\mathrm{surv}}$,

$$
0\le V_{\mathrm{out}}\le20p_{\mathrm{surv}}.
$$

If that probability is 1%, the bound is 0.20 USD, not a claim that the option is worth 19 or exactly 0.20. In the continuous-diffusion boundary limit, survival probability tends to zero and so does the bound. The terminal-price slider and the separate assumed survival probability in the visual are different calculations; neither estimates the other.

### When to use it

Label every plotted curve as a terminal payoff, a pre-expiry model value, a sensitivity or a realised path. Monitoring dates, jumps, rebates and remaining time change the relevant boundary behaviour. Do not infer a universal hedge, infinite Greek or transaction price from the shape of a terminal payoff alone.

A contractual event can occur when the market jumps across a level without trading there. An instruction to adjust a hedge is also distinct from an executable transaction. Liquidity, delays and order terms can change the price obtained.

The sign of every later fill difference is not predetermined. If the market recovers and the later fill is 95, cash is 950 and the difference versus the plan is +50, while the share position still lost 50 relative to its initial 1000 value. A model of fill timing and prices is needed to assess their distribution.

This example is only the gross share leg. It does not state an optimal delta, an option premium or the P&L of an entire barrier-option book. A complete hedge ledger includes the option position, marks, cash financing, execution costs and any rebate; see the earlier [discrete hedging lesson](/greeks-and-hedging/delta-neutral-hedging).

### When to use it

Stress gaps, delayed observations and available execution separately from the contract's trigger rule. A finer numerical grid cannot create liquidity, and changing a model's monitoring convention does not change the order or settlement terms you can actually trade.

## A Brownian bridge supplies a conditional model probability

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"Under constant-volatility GBM, only two simulated endpoint prices 100 and 100 are known. A down barrier is 90. What can the straight segment between endpoints establish?"}
  options={[{"text": "Only the interpolated segment’s shape; the conditional model may still cross between them", "correct": true}, {"text": "That every compatible stochastic path must remain above 90"}, {"text": "That every contract must count all numerical grid points as fixings"}, {"text": "That the option price equals the conditional crossing probability"}]}
  explanation={"A Brownian bridge conditions on endpoints and still contains random between-endpoint paths. It does not rewrite the observation schedule."}
/>

Sometimes only simulated interval endpoints are available, while the contract is continuously monitored. A **Brownian bridge** describes the model's continuous path conditional on those endpoints. It can supply a crossing probability between them, without pretending that a straight line was the realised path.

Here assume geometric Brownian motion (GBM), positive constant annual volatility $\sigma$, a fixed down barrier $H$ and interval length $\Delta t>0$ in years. The dynamics and probability measure must be specified: physical probabilities serve a risk model; risk-neutral probabilities serve a pricing model. The formula below is conditional under the chosen GBM law. A constant drift cancels when conditioning on both endpoints; that does not validate the volatility model or turn pricing probabilities into physical forecasts.

For endpoints $S_0>H$ and $S_1>H$, define log distances $x=\ln(S_0/H)$ and $y=\ln(S_1/H)$, and variance $v=\sigma^2\Delta t$. Reflection of Brownian paths at their first barrier crossing gives the ratio of two Gaussian endpoint densities:

$$
P(\text{hit }H\mid S_0,S_1)
=\frac{\exp[-(x+y)^2/(2v)]}{\exp[-(y-x)^2/(2v)]}
=\exp\left(-\frac{2xy}{v}\right).
$$

The common density normalisation cancels, and $(x+y)^2-(y-x)^2=4xy$. If an endpoint is at or below $H$, the inclusive event already occurred. [Glasserman and Staum, section 2.4 and equation 19](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/4311/one-step_survival.pdf), give the conditional minimum calculation under this GBM model and distinguish a simulation grid from the contract's observation dates.

### Two endpoints above the barrier do not imply zero probability

Take both endpoints at 100, barrier 90, annual volatility 20% and $\Delta t=1/4$ year:

$$
x=y=\ln(100/90)=0.10536052,\qquad v=0.20^2(0.25)=0.01,
$$

$$
P(\text{hit}\mid100,100)
=\exp\left[-\frac{2\ln(100/90)^2}{0.01}\right]
=0.10859090\approx10.859090\%.
$$

The straight segment between the two endpoint prices never hits 90, but the conditional GBM model assigns crossing probability about 10.86%. Raise the barrier to 99 and use a model interval of $1/12$ year: the same formula gives **94.119442%**, even though both endpoints remain 100.

<BarrierBridgeProbability client:visible  />

For a separate call with strike 90, those fixed terminal endpoints imply plain payoff 10. Conditional on the two endpoints under this one-interval model, its zero-discount no-rebate down-and-out expected payoff is $10(1-0.1085909018)\approx8.91409098$. That is a **conditional contribution**, not the unconditional option price. Generally, the full price needs the joint payoff and survival law, $\mathbb E_Q[F\mathbf1_{\mathrm{survive}}]$, not an unsupported product of a vanilla price and an unconditional survival probability.

### When to use it

Use the bridge calculation to address between-grid crossing in a compatible continuous-path model. It does not prove that an unknown historical crossing occurred, change a discrete contractual schedule, handle jumps by itself or establish a market price from endpoints alone. Stochastic volatility, changing barriers and other dynamics need their own analysis; numerical precision is separate from whether the assumed model is appropriate.
