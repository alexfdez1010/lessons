An option dealer sells a call, buys some shares and announces that delta is zero. The next morning the book has lost money. That can happen with every calculation correct: zero delta describes one local slope, while the account also contains curvature, passing time and a financing bill.

We will keep those accounts together. Every number below is an invented teaching case, not a trading recommendation. The hedge is against one specified underlying and one model's delta.

## Zero delta describes the current slope

Delta is the option value's sensitivity to a small change in the stock price, holding time, implied volatility and the other valuation inputs fixed. Let $C$ be the value **per underlying share** of a European call, and let $m$ be the contract multiplier. One short call contract has position delta $-m\Delta_C$. If $h$ is the number of shares held, then

$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{book}}=h-m\Delta_C,
\qquad h=m\Delta_C \text{ makes the current delta zero.}
$$

Shares have delta one each. Positive $h$ means shares owned; a short position in shares would have negative $h$. In particular, a short call with delta $0.50$ and multiplier $100$ needs **50 shares bought**, because $50-100(0.50)=0$. Shorting 50 instead gives $-50-50=-100$ shares of exposure.

Think of placing a tangent against a curved road. They point the same way at the contact point; they do not follow the same route over a whole kilometre. The [CFA Institute text](https://www.cfainstitute.org/sites/default/files/-/media/documents/book/rf-publication/2013/rf-v2013-n3-1-pdf.pdf) defines the hedge through the combined delta and shows why the ratio changes with the underlying.

### A full initial position

For the rest of the lesson, set stock $S_0=100$ USD, strike $K=100$, remaining time $\tau_0=0.25$ years, implied volatility $\sigma_0=0.20$ and continuous financing rate $r=0.04$ per year. There are no dividends. This is one European call, multiplier $m=100$, with fractional shares allowed. Borrowing and lending use the same rate; no margin restrictions are modelled.

Black–Scholes–Merton gives $C_0=4.48523641$ and $\Delta_0=0.55961769$, so

$$
h_0=100(0.55961769)=55.961769,\qquad
B_0=100C_0-h_0S_0=-5147.653283.
$$

The sale receives 448.523641 USD; the shares cost 5596.176924 USD. The negative cash account $B_0$ is borrowing, not a free contribution. Initial marked net wealth is $B_0+h_0S_0-100C_0=0$ before execution costs. Zero initial net wealth does not remove funding, collateral or solvency constraints.

If only stock changes immediately from 100 to 101, the shares gain 55.961769 USD and the short call loses 57.916264 USD under full model repricing. Net: **−1.954494 USD**. The first-order delta prediction was zero; the curve was still curved.

### When to use it

Delta is useful for setting and reporting current directional exposure. Name the model, underlying, multiplier and units. A zero slope is a local risk statement, not a bound on a finite move's loss.

## A frozen hedge has a complete profit and loss ledger

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"A short call has positive theta P&L in our case. What sign should its initial long-call theta have in the expansion?"}
  options={[{"text": "Negative: short position P&L uses −m × ΘC × elapsed years", "correct": true}, {"text": "Positive: every theta input is an unsigned cash receipt"}, {"text": "Zero: delta neutrality also removes the passing of time"}, {"text": "Negative, then subtract the same time decay a second time"}]}
  explanation={"We define ΘC as the signed long-call derivative for calendar time advancing. The short position flips it once."}
/>

Freeze $h_0$ shares for $\delta t$ years, let the call change from $C_0$ to $C_1$, and grow the signed cash account at the stipulated rate. Before any new trade, exact accounting gives

$$
P=-m(C_1-C_0)+h_0(S_1-S_0)+B_0(e^{r\delta t}-1).
$$

“Exact” here means using both complete model valuations and the complete account identity. It does not mean that the model predicts the actual executable option price. Shares, cash and the short-call liability are marked at the same endpoint.

A Taylor expansion helps explain the result. Write $\Gamma_C=\partial^2C/\partial S^2$, $\Theta_C=\partial C/\partial t$ for **calendar time advancing**, and $\mathcal V_C=\partial C/\partial\sigma$. These are the initial **long-call, per-share** Greeks. With initial delta cancelled and rates fixed,

$$
P\approx -m\left[\tfrac12\Gamma_C(\delta S)^2+\Theta_C\delta t+\mathcal V_C\delta\sigma\right]
+B_0(e^{r\delta t}-1).
$$

The minus sign outside the brackets belongs to the short call. Theta is signed, not an unspecified “theta earned” amount to subtract again. Our vega is per **1.00** change in volatility, so five percentage points means $\delta\sigma=0.05$. A quote per one volatility point would be this vega divided by 100. The approximation omits cross terms and higher orders; they belong in the residual, alongside any extra real-world price or execution differences.

### Five days later

Let $\delta t=5/365$, $S_1=105$ and volatility remain 20%. Remaining option time is $0.25-5/365=0.23630137$ years. Full model repricing gives $C_1=7.61466694$.

Initial $\Gamma_C=0.0394479331$, $\Theta_C=-9.94864793$ USD per year and $\mathcal V_C=19.72396655$. ### When to use it

Use full repricing to calculate a specified scenario and the Greek expansion to explain the local drivers. Retain cash financing in both. For large moves, changing volatility or near-expiry payoffs, inspect the approximation error rather than assuming it is small.

## Rebalancing exchanges cash for shares

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="If buying 18.178537 shares restores delta zero, should net wealth also return to zero?" text="No. Buying at the same mark exchanges cash for shares. Net wealth stays −35.955612 USD before costs; the already incurred loss remains." />

At the five-day endpoint, the call's per-share delta is $0.74140306$. The existing book now has

$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{before}}=55.961769-100(0.74140306)=-18.178537.
$$

Buy $18.178537$ shares at 105 USD. They cost $18.178537(105)=1908.746385$ USD. Just before trading, cash is $B^-=B_0e^{r\delta t}=-5150.474688$ USD. With zero execution cost, the cash after trading is $B^+=B^- -1908.746385=-7059.221073$ USD and shares become $74.140306$.

The trade restores delta zero without restoring wealth to zero. It merely swaps one asset account for another. A **self-financing** strategy has no outside cash contribution after setup: purchases, sales and financing change the internal cash account. It can still need borrowing capacity. This distinction is central to [Columbia's derivation of the financed hedge](https://www.columbia.edu/~yao/bsnotes04b.pdf).

If the stipulated all-in execution charge is 0.02 USD per share traded, this rebalance costs $18.178537(0.02)=0.363571$ USD. Cash falls further to −7059.584644 USD and net wealth becomes **−36.319182 USD**. The charge already represents execution friction in this example; do not also subtract the same spread a second time.

### When to use it

Record the sign and cash effect of every trade. For a short vanilla call, an isolated rise in spot, with other inputs fixed and positive call gamma, raises the share target: buying after the rise is the correct hedge direction. Time or volatility can also change delta; “gamma causes every hedge change” is too narrow. Actual stock borrow, dividends, margin and unequal funding rates require additional accounts.

## Delta does not cover every market change

A stock position has no option vega or option gamma. Changing its size offsets one chosen delta; it does not cancel the other Greeks. Two stress cases reuse the original position, freeze its shares for five days and exclude rebalance costs.

For the first row, stock gain is zero. Short-call vega is approximately $-100(19.72396655)(0.05)=-98.619833$ USD, offset partly by +13.628285 theta and reduced by −2.821405 financing. Full repricing gives $C_1=5.30739417$ instead of treating the initial vega as constant across the whole shift.

The second row demonstrates why a large finite move deserves repricing. The option is worth 21.03750517 USD per share, so $-100(C_1-C_0)+55.961769(20)-2.821405=-538.812896$ USD. A five-day move from 100 to 120 can occur over a continuous unobserved path; those two marks do not prove an instantaneous jump. If a genuine jump occurs while trading is unavailable, a later hedge cannot capture intermediate prices either.

<DeltaHedgeLedger client:visible mode="attribution" endSpot={100} days={5} endVolatility={0.25} labels={{}} />

The simulator is conditional accounting for an endpoint, not a probability distribution of losses. It assigns no probability to either stress. In a complete frictionless Black–Scholes–Merton model, continuous self-financing replication at the correct model volatility, with the stipulated financing and no dividends, can match the European payoff. That theorem does not extend automatically to stochastic volatility, jumps, mismatched option models, discrete trading or unavailable liquidity. A financed zero-initial-wealth sale-plus-replication book has zero model profit in that ideal; an option alone does not.

### When to use it

Stress spot, volatility, time and financing separately, then jointly. Include jumps, costs and liquidity where relevant, and specify which positions or limits address the remaining risks. Faster stock trading cannot directly remove a volatility shock with stock unchanged.

## Choose a risk and cost objective before a frequency

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="Can you add 200 USD of P&L standard deviation to 100 USD of expected costs and call the result expected cash loss?" text="Only as an explicitly chosen score with a risk weight, not as expected cash loss. Standard deviation describes dispersion; an expected cash cost is a different quantity." />

Suppose a smooth diffusion model has bounded enough gamma and sufficiently regular rebalancing. A centred short-interval error dominated by squared price increments has variance of order $(\delta t)^2$. Summing approximately independent or appropriately weakly dependent centred errors over $N$ equal intervals across a fixed horizon gives variance of order $N(T/N)^2$, hence standard deviation of order $1/\sqrt N$. This is an asymptotic model argument, not a promise that every extra trade improves one realised path. Near payoff kinks, jumps or misspecification, the argument needs revisiting.

A cost formula also needs assumptions. With proportional spread costs and a diffusion-like delta change, expected total turnover can scale roughly as $\sqrt N$ over the same horizon. A fixed ticket fee instead adds a term proportional to the number of tickets; real netting, no-trade bands, impact and liquidity change the comparison.

### A chosen decision score

For illustration only, stipulate standard deviation $s(N)=1000/\sqrt N$ USD and expected cost $c(N)=60\sqrt N$ USD. Standard deviation is risk, **not expected loss**. To compare it with costs, explicitly choose a dimensionless risk weight $\lambda$ and score

$$
J(N)=\lambda s(N)+c(N)=\frac{1000\lambda}{\sqrt N}+60\sqrt N.
$$

With $\lambda=1$, differentiating the continuous proxy gives $J'(N)=-500N^{-3/2}+30N^{-1/2}=0$, so $N^*=1000/60=16.6667$. Check feasible neighbouring integers: $J(16)=490.0000$ and $J(17)=489.9220$ USD. The best integer in 1–250 is 17 under this chosen score.

At $\lambda=0$ with positive costs, the score ignores risk and selects the lowest allowed frequency. At zero cost with positive $\lambda$, it selects the highest allowed frequency. With both zero, all counts tie and the display chooses the first one. A U-shaped interior minimum is therefore conditional even within this toy family. A manager might instead minimise expected costs subject to a loss limit or cash-capacity constraint; that is a different optimisation problem.

### When to use it

Compare specified policies on the same portfolio, horizon and scenario model, with a stated risk criterion, costs and constraints. Check out-of-sample behaviour and stress cases. A frequency chosen from this illustration is not an execution instruction for a real book.
