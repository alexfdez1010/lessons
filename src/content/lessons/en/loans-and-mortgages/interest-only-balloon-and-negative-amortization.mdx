Three borrowers each receive €1,000 at the same monthly interest rate. Their first scheduled payments are €340.02, €10 and €5. The third offer wins the smallest-payment competition. That tells us almost nothing about the debt it leaves behind.

We will follow the balances, the cash paid and the dates on which the remaining debt becomes due. These are invented contracts for learning the mechanics, not offers available from a lender. The **monthly rate is given directly as 1%**; it is not obtained by dividing a regulatory APR by twelve. There are no fees, insurance, taxes, new borrowing or rate changes in the first example.

## A payment has to pass the interest test

**Principal** is the outstanding borrowed balance. **Interest** is the charge for keeping that balance outstanding during a period. If the contract adds unpaid interest to principal, that addition is **capitalization**: the interest becomes part of the balance on which later interest is calculated.

For the simple monthly contracts here, let $B_{t-1}$ be the opening balance, $r$ the monthly rate, $I_t$ that month's interest and $C_t$ the scheduled payment:

$$
I_t=\operatorname{round}_{\text{cent}}(rB_{t-1}),\qquad
B_t=B_{t-1}+I_t-C_t.
$$

The second identity assumes that any unpaid interest **is contractually capitalized**. If it is not, paying too little can instead create arrears, fees or default under the agreement; you cannot invent permission by changing a spreadsheet cell. Payments are capped at the amount actually due.

The sign of $C_t-I_t$ describes the change in principal:

| First payment on €1,000 at 1% monthly | Interest | Payment minus interest | Balance after payment |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| €340.02 | €10.00 | +€330.02 principal repaid | €669.98 |
| €10.00 | €10.00 | €0.00 principal repaid | €1,000.00 |
| €5.00, capitalization permitted | €10.00 | −€5.00 principal reduction | €1,005.00 |

Pay more than interest and the balance shrinks. Pay exactly interest and it stays level. Pay less and, **under the stated capitalization clause**, it grows. The last row's negative principal reduction means €5 was added to debt, not that the lender received a negative payment.

### When to use the interest test

Use it on each line of a schedule. It reveals whether a low instalment is reducing debt, merely servicing it or deferring some interest. Day-count conventions, payment timing and fees can change a real schedule, so reproduce the lender's stated calculation method before comparing cents.

## Fully amortizing means the schedule clears the balance

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A three-month loan quotes a level payment rounded to cents. Must the final payment equal the first two to the cent?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; a final adjustment can clear the rounded balance', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; the lender must leave any residual cent unpaid' },
    { text: 'Yes; monthly interest never needs rounding' },
    { text: 'No; the final payment always contains only interest' },
  ]}
  explanation="The mathematical level payment may contain fractions of a cent. With monthly rounding, the final payment is adjusted to settle the remaining principal and interest exactly."
/>

A **fully amortizing schedule** repays principal and interest by the agreed maturity, assuming the scheduled payments are made. Amortization itself means reducing principal through repayment; it does not require every possible loan to have identical instalments. The familiar level-payment formula, before rounding, is

$$
M=P\frac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-n}}.
$$

Here $P$ is the initial principal, $r$ the periodic rate and $n$ the number of payments. For $P=1{,}000$, $r=0.01$ and $n=3$:

$$
M=1{,}000\frac{0.01}{1-1.01^{-3}}\approx340.02211148.
$$

Use €340.02 for the first two payments. **Round each month's interest half up to the nearest cent**, keep the balance in cents and make the last payment equal to the remaining balance plus that month's rounded interest:

| Month | Opening balance | Interest | Payment | Principal repaid | Closing balance |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | €1,000.00 | €10.00 | €340.02 | €330.02 | €669.98 |
| 2 | €669.98 | €6.70 | €340.02 | €333.32 | €336.66 |
| 3 | €336.66 | €3.37 | €340.03 | €336.66 | €0.00 |

Month two interest is $669.98\times0.01=6.6998$, rounded to €6.70. Month three interest is $336.66\times0.01=3.3666$, rounded to €3.37. The final due amount is $336.66+3.37=340.03$ euros. Total interest is $10+6.70+3.37=20.07$ euros, and total cash paid is €1,020.07.

This is why multiplying the rounded regular payment by three gives a one-cent error: $340.02\times3=1{,}020.06$, but the last payment is €340.03. With a zero rate, the unrounded payment becomes $P/n$; the final cent adjustment still matters.

### When this structure helps

It supplies a scheduled route to zero debt without relying on refinancing or a sale at maturity. It can require more cash each month than a deferral structure. It does not make the full cost of owning a home fit into the loan payment: taxes, insurance and maintenance remain separate.

## Interest only services the debt without reducing it

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="During an agreed interest-only phase, all interest is paid on time and there is no new borrowing. Which balance path fits?"
  options={[

    { text: 'Principal growing by each interest charge', correct: false },
    { text: 'Principal automatically forgiven at the phase end', correct: false },
  ]}
  explanation="Interest-only pays the charge for using the money. With no extra principal payment or new borrowing, it leaves the borrowed balance unchanged."
/>

An **interest-only phase** requires payments of interest while scheduled principal repayment is deferred. It can occupy part of a loan or its whole term. The [CFPB's interest-only explanation](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-an-interest-only-loan-en-101/) distinguishes a later lump-sum repayment from a transition to higher principal-and-interest instalments; the agreement determines which occurs. The [Banco de España's explanation of grace periods](https://clientebancario.bde.es/pcb/es/menu-horizontal/productosservici/financiacion/prestamopersonal/guia-textual/conceptocaracter/Plazo_y_periodo_de_carencia.html) likewise notes that paying only interest does not reduce principal.

In our first invented contract, the loan **matures at the end of month three**. Interest is $1{,}000\times1\%=10$ euros each month:

| Date | Interest payment | Separate principal repayment | Total cash due that date | Balance after all due payments |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| End of month 1 | €10.00 | €0.00 | €10.00 | €1,000.00 |
| End of month 2 | €10.00 | €0.00 | €10.00 | €1,000.00 |
| End of month 3, maturity | €10.00 | €1,000.00 | €1,010.00 | €0.00 |

The instalment before maturity is €10, but the final dated obligation is €1,010. Total interest is €30, versus €20.07 on the three-month fully amortizing loan. Both use exactly the same 1% monthly rate. Keeping more principal outstanding for longer produces more interest.

There is no negative amortization here: **all** accrued interest is paid. A flat principal balance is not a growing balance. Nor does the phrase “interest only” by itself prove there is a balloon; a longer agreement can instead start amortizing later.

### When interest only can be useful

It can match an agreed temporary cash-flow need or a known principal-repayment plan. Evaluate the deferred balance and the next payment phase alongside the initial cash relief. Expected asset appreciation, a future salary increase and refinancing approval are uncertain sources of repayment.

## Negative amortization adds unpaid interest to debt

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The contract expressly allows unpaid interest to be added monthly. On €1,000 at 1% monthly, a €5 payment leaves what balance after month one?"
  options={[
    { text: '€1,005', correct: true },
    { text: '€995' },
    { text: '€1,000' },
    { text: '€1,010' },
  ]}
  explanation="Interest is €10. Paying €5 leaves €5 unpaid, and the stated capitalization clause adds that €5 to principal: 1,000 + 10 − 5 = 1,005."
/>

**Negative amortization** occurs when the balance grows because payments do not cover interest and the unpaid amount is added to principal. The [CFPB's definition](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-negative-amortization-en-103/) explains this mechanism. Our example expressly permits monthly capitalization; it is not permission to underpay a different loan.

| Month | Opening balance | Rounded interest | Payment | Interest added to principal | Closing balance before balloon |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | €1,000.00 | €10.00 | €5.00 | €5.00 | €1,005.00 |
| 2 | €1,005.00 | €10.05 | €5.00 | €5.05 | €1,010.05 |
| 3 | €1,010.05 | €10.10 | €5.00 | €5.10 | €1,015.15 |

Month three interest is $1{,}010.05\times0.01=10.1005$, rounded to €10.10. The closing balance is $1{,}010.05+10.10-5=1{,}015.15$ euros. Interest charged totals €30.15. Of that, €15 has been paid in cash and €15.15 has been capitalized. The original principal has not been repaid.

Without rounding, a constant-payment balance satisfies

$$
B_n=P(1+r)^n-C\frac{(1+r)^n-1}{r},\qquad r>0.
$$

With $P=1{,}000$, $r=0.01$, $C=5$, $n=3$, this gives €1,015.1505 before rounding. The cents schedule gives €1,015.15 under our monthly convention. Use the recurrence, rather than rounding this closed form once at the end, when reproducing monthly statements.

### When to examine capitalization closely

Check when unpaid interest is added, whether later interest is charged on it, which payment choices are actually permitted and what ends the deferral. A low required payment can comply with an agreement while debt grows; an unauthorised short payment can breach a different agreement. They are not interchangeable legal or accounting events.

## A balloon is a dated cash requirement

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="At month-three maturity, the €5-payment schedule has a €1,015.15 balance after its third scheduled payment. What total cash is due on that date under the stated immediate-settlement clause?"

  ]}
  explanation="The remaining balance is measured after the third €5 payment. Adding the balloon on that same date gives 5 + 1,015.15 = 1,020.15. It is not an extra interest period."

A **balloon payment** is a large scheduled payment at the end of the contractual term that clears debt not repaid by the earlier instalments. The [CFPB's balloon-payment guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-balloon-payment-when-is-one-allowed-en-104/) warns that refinancing may be unavailable when it is needed. Definitions and permitted product features vary by jurisdiction; here “balloon” describes the agreed large terminal payment, not a universal legal threshold.

For our custom schedule, the chronology is: third month's interest accrues; the scheduled €5 is paid; the remaining €1,015.15 is paid immediately as a separate balloon, **without another month's interest**. Across all dates, cash paid is $5+5+5+1{,}015.15=1{,}030.15$ euros. Subtract the €1,000 borrowed: €30.15 of total interest.

| Three-month contract | Earlier scheduled monthly amount | Separate final balloon | Total due on month-three date | Total interest |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Fully amortizing | €340.02 | €0.00 | €340.03 | €20.07 |
| Interest only | €10.00 | €1,000.00 | €1,010.00 | €30.00 |
| €5 with permitted capitalization | €5.00 | €1,015.15 | €1,020.15 | €30.15 |
| €100 with partial amortization | €100.00 | €727.29 | €827.29 | €27.29 |

The last row repays some principal every month and still has a balloon. Its balances are $1{,}000+10-100=910$, then $910+9.10-100=819.10$, then $819.10+8.19-100=727.29$ euros. **A balloon does not require negative amortization or interest-only payments.** It requires a repayment schedule that leaves a large terminal amount.

If you intend to save the interest-only loan's €1,000 balloon over three months, starting with no reserve and earning no return, you need about $1{,}000/3=333.333\ldots$ euros per month. Saving €333.34 monthly supplies €1,000.02, in addition to each month's €10 interest. The loan instalment is €10; the cash commitment needed for that particular repayment plan is about €343.34 per month.

### When to use the maturity view

Put every payment on its actual date, including a balloon, and identify how each will be funded. Sale proceeds depend on the asset price and transaction costs; a new loan depends on approval and terms. A plan that needs either to succeed is exposed to those uncertainties even if every small instalment has been paid on time.

## Recasts, caps and affordability use the whole calendar

A **recast** here means recalculating payments on the balance then outstanding over the contractual remaining term. It is not the same as refinancing into a new loan, and it does not automatically extend maturity. A **rate reset** changes the interest rate under its own rule. Either event can change payments; they need not occur together.

For this section only, consider **different invented contracts with six-month maturity**, not an extension promised by the earlier three-month contracts. They have the same €1,000 starting principal and 1% monthly rate. Some amortize for all six months; others defer principal or capitalize interest during the first three months, then amortize over the remaining three:

| Six-month schedule | Balance after month 3 | Payment in month 4 | Payment in month 6 | Interest across all six months |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Fully amortizing from the start | €507.45 | €172.55 | €172.53 | €35.28 |
| First three months interest-only | €1,000.00 | €340.02 | €340.03 | €50.07 |
| First three months at €5 with capitalization | €1,015.15 | €345.17 | €345.18 | €50.52 |

For the last row, apply the same formula to the **new balance** and **remaining three months**:

$$
M_{\text{recast}}=1{,}015.15\frac{0.01}{1-1.01^{-3}}\approx345.17344647.
$$

Rounded payments are €345.17, €345.17 and €345.18. Interest in this second phase is €10.15 + €6.80 + €3.42 = €20.37. Add the first phase's €30.15: total interest is €50.52. Total cash is $15+345.17+345.17+345.18=1{,}050.52$ euros. Capitalized interest is not added a second time to that cash total.

### Read what each limit actually limits

A **payment cap** limits an instalment change under specified conditions. An **interest-rate cap** limits the rate. A **balance-growth trigger** can end negative amortization and require recalculation. These are different limits. The [US banking agencies' guidance on nontraditional mortgages](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/guidance/interagency-guidance-on-nontraditional-mortgage-product-risks.htm) highlights payment shock and negative-amortization risks; the actual timing, exceptions and caps must come from the agreement.

Suppose an invented rule tests the balance **after each scheduled payment** and requires a recast when it first exceeds 101% of the original €1,000. The threshold is €1,010; the €5 schedule reaches €1,010.05 after month two, so that rule triggers then. It is a trigger for recalculation, not a rule silently truncating the balance to €1,010. A contract that prevents an overshoot by changing the payment beforehand would work differently.

Likewise, capping a €5 payment increase at 5% gives €5.25. That still does not cover €10.05 interest on €1,005. If capitalization remains permitted, the payment cap has not stopped debt growth. A recast may have separate exceptions to the payment cap; do not assume either way.

Finally, test the dates against cash available. An invented budget of €1,200 net income and €900 of other commitments leaves €300 before the loan payment. A €10 initial interest-only payment fits; a later €340.02 principal-and-interest payment exceeds that amount by €40.02, even with an unchanged interest rate. A low opening payment is not an affordability test for the whole agreement.

### When to compare structures

Compare equal amounts borrowed, common dates, rate assumptions, fees and the whole payment calendar. Keep any reserve or investment plan visible rather than assuming deferred principal vanishes. Some borrowers value temporary cash flexibility; the cost and risk of that flexibility depend on the later obligations and their funding.
