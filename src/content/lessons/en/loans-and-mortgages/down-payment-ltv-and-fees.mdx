Ana has saved \$60,000 toward a \$300,000 home. That's a 20% down payment. Can she sign with exactly that amount in the bank? Only after checking two other numbers: the value accepted for the loan and the costs of completing the purchase. The seller's price, the lender's valuation and the cash needed on signing day answer different questions.

The arithmetic below applies wherever a loan is secured by property. **PMI, discount points and the named disclosure forms are US examples**; their rules do not automatically apply to a mortgage in Spain or another country. All amounts and quotes in the worked cases are invented for teaching.

A **down payment** is the part of the purchase price funded without the mortgage loan. The **principal** is the amount borrowed. With one mortgage and no costs added to its balance:

$$\text{Loan}=\text{Purchase price}-\text{Down payment}$$

For Ana, \$300,000 − \$60,000 = **\$240,000 borrowed**. Ben contributes \$15,000, so he borrows \$300,000 − \$15,000 = **\$285,000**. Ana has \$45,000 less debt at the start, before any repayment.

A down payment is like paying part of a purchase from savings and putting the rest on an account: the part paid now reduces the bill still owed. Unlike an ordinary shopping bill, a mortgage is secured by the home; unpaid debt can put the property at risk.

### When to use it

Use this subtraction to plan the amount borrowed. A bigger down payment reduces the loan at a fixed price, but also leaves less cash available for emergencies. It does not guarantee approval or the lowest rate: income, other debts, credit history and the loan product also affect the decision. A borrower with no down payment still risks missed-payment charges, credit damage and losing the home.

**Loan-to-value (LTV)** compares the loan with the property value used for the lending calculation. An **appraisal** is an estimate of the property's value. Check which valuation the lender and product use; do not silently substitute the advertised price.

$$\text{LTV}=\frac{\text{Loan balance}}{\text{Property value used}}\times100\%$$

When both the price and accepted value are \$300,000, Ana's LTV is \$240,000 ÷ \$300,000 × 100 = **80%**. Ben's is \$285,000 ÷ \$300,000 × 100 = **95%**. In this particular setup, down-payment percentage + LTV = 100%.

### Worked example with a lower appraisal

Keep Ana's agreed price at \$300,000 and loan at \$240,000, but suppose the lender uses an appraised value of **\$280,000**:

$$\text{LTV}=\frac{240{,}000}{280{,}000}\times100\%\approx85.71\%$$

Her contribution is still 20% of the price, but the LTV is about 85.71%. If this hypothetical lender caps this loan at 80% of that accepted value, the maximum loan becomes 0.80 × \$280,000 = **\$224,000**. To keep the same purchase price, Ana would need \$300,000 − \$224,000 = **\$76,000 toward the price**, an extra \$16,000 before closing costs.

| Accepted value | Loan | Cash toward the price | LTV |
|---|---|---|---|
| \$300,000 | \$240,000 | \$60,000 | 80% |
| \$280,000 | \$240,000 | \$60,000 | About 85.71% |
| \$280,000 | \$224,000 | \$76,000 | 80% |

### When to use it

LTV helps compare how much debt sits against the collateral, the property securing the loan. Lower LTV generally gives the lender more room to absorb a fall in value, but does not prove that the borrower can afford payments. The [CFPB explains how LTV can affect loan terms](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-loan-to-value-ratio-and-how-does-it-relate-to-my-costs-en-121/).

## PMI has a country, a product and cancellation rules

**Private mortgage insurance (PMI)** insures the lender against certain default losses. On US **conventional loans**, meaning loans not insured or guaranteed by the federal government, it is commonly required with a down payment below 20%. It can enable a smaller upfront contribution, while adding cost; it does not protect the borrower from foreclosure. Payment can be monthly, upfront or a combination. See the [CFPB's PMI explanation](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-private-mortgage-insurance-en-122/).

For an arithmetic illustration, suppose a quote charges 0.6% a year on a \$270,000 loan, billed in equal monthly amounts. Annual premium = 0.006 × \$270,000 = **\$1,620**; monthly premium = \$1,620 ÷ 12 = **\$135**. This is an invented quote, not a current market rate or a rule for every policy.

### Requesting cancellation is different from automatic termination

For the standard US borrower-paid PMI rules on covered principal-residence loans, the usual tests use **original value**, generally the lower of purchase price and original appraisal for a purchase. A fresh online estimate does not replace it.

| Route | Main test | Important condition |
|---|---|---|
| Borrower requests cancellation | Balance reaches 80% of original value, on schedule or through extra principal payments | Written request, good payment history, current payments, and applicable evidence about value and junior liens |
| Automatic termination | Scheduled balance reaches 78% of original value | Borrower must be current on payments |
| Final termination | Midpoint of the amortization period if PMI has not already ended | Borrower must be current on payments |

A **junior lien** is another claim secured by the property, such as a second mortgage. At an original value of \$300,000, 80% is **\$240,000** and 78% is **\$234,000**. Extra payments can bring forward a cancellation request; they do not simply rewrite the scheduled automatic-termination date.

These are the standard routes, not a promise for every mortgage. High-risk loans can have exceptions, and FHA, VA and lender-paid insurance have different rules. Investor policies may offer other cancellation routes, including routes based on a new valuation. Check the [CFPB cancellation guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/when-can-i-remove-private-mortgage-insurance-pmi-from-my-loan-en-202/) and the [Homeowners Protection Act provisions](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t12/s4902/) for the scope and conditions.

### When to use it

Separate the question “does this new loan require insurance?” from “can this existing policy end?” Keep the original valuation, payment schedule and cancellation disclosure. A rising market value can improve current LTV without automatically satisfying the legal cancellation route.

## Equity can rise or fall

**Home equity** is the property's value minus the debt secured against it. With one mortgage:

$$\text{Equity}=\text{Current property value}-\text{Loan balance}$$

If Ana's home is worth \$300,000 and the debt is \$240,000, equity is **\$60,000**. If the value later rises to \$340,000 and she repays \$25,000 of principal, debt becomes \$215,000 and equity is \$340,000 − \$215,000 = **\$125,000**. That combines \$60,000 initial equity + \$40,000 price appreciation + \$25,000 principal repayment. Interest payments do not reduce principal.

Now hold debt at \$240,000 to isolate price risk:

| Current property value | Mortgage debt | Equity before selling costs | Current LTV |
|---|---|---|---|
| \$360,000 | \$240,000 | \$120,000 | About 66.67% |
| \$300,000 | \$240,000 | \$60,000 | 80% |
| \$225,000 | \$240,000 | −\$15,000 | About 106.67% |

In the last row, \$225,000 − \$240,000 = **−\$15,000**: **negative equity**, meaning the secured debt exceeds the property's value. A sale at that value would not cover the mortgage even before selling costs. The consequences of an unpaid shortfall depend on the contract and local law; returning the keys does not establish that the debt disappears.

### When to use it

Use equity to assess the owner's financial cushion, and LTV to express the debt relative to the property. Equity is not a bank balance: accessing it normally requires selling or borrowing, with costs and conditions. If there are other secured loans, subtract those too when calculating total home equity.

**Closing costs** are the transaction and loan costs associated with completing the purchase, separate from the down payment. **Cash to close** is the remaining amount the buyer must bring after money already paid, borrowing and credits are accounted for. Use the actual estimate and final disclosure; a percentage rule cannot reconcile a particular transaction.

Here is an invented cost sheet for Ana's \$240,000 mortgage. None of these costs has been paid yet, and none is financed:

| Item | Amount | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Origination charge | \$1,200 | Arranging the loan |
| One discount point | \$2,400 | A quoted reduction in the interest rate |
| Appraisal | \$500 | Estimating the property value |
| Title and recording charges | \$1,300 | Specified ownership and registration services |
| Prepaid interest and insurance | \$600 | Payments for a stated initial period |
| Initial escrow deposit | \$2,000 | Funds held for future property bills |
| **Total** | **\$8,000** | **Separate from the down payment** |

**Escrow** here means an account holding funds for specified bills, such as property taxes and insurance. An initial deposit into it is not another down payment and should not be counted again as a lender fee.

Ana's down payment is \$60,000. She has already paid a \$6,000 purchase deposit that counts toward it, and the seller contributes an agreed \$2,000 credit toward eligible costs. Assume no other adjustments:

$$\text{Cash to close}=60{,}000+8{,}000-6{,}000-2{,}000=60{,}000$$

Ana needs **\$60,000 more at closing**; together with her earlier \$6,000 deposit, her own total contribution is \$66,000. The seller pays the other \$2,000. If she wants \$6,000 left in reserve after closing, she needs **\$66,000 available immediately before closing**, since the earlier deposit has already left her account.

The deposit is credited once. Deducting it twice would understate the bill. Financing costs instead of paying them immediately would change the loan balance and may change LTV. The [CFPB Closing Disclosure guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/closing-disclosure/) shows where these amounts appear on the US form.

### When to use it

Reconcile the quoted cash to close before comparing it with available savings. Then separately budget the ongoing payment, insurance, taxes and maintenance. Money reserved for emergencies is not a transaction fee, but spending it on closing leaves less available afterward.

## Discount points need a holding-period comparison

  pretest

A US **discount point** is an upfront charge for a lower interest rate: one point equals 1% of the loan amount. The rate reduction per point is quote-specific, not a fixed legal exchange rate. Compare the same lender, loan amount, term and quote date. See the [CFPB guide to points and lender credits](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-should-i-use-lender-credits-and-points-also-called-discount-points-en-136/).

Suppose one point on Ana's \$240,000 loan costs 0.01 × \$240,000 = **\$2,400**, and the otherwise comparable quote reduces her monthly payment by \$50. The **simple cash-payback period** is:

$$\text{Months}=\frac{\text{Extra upfront cost}}{\text{Monthly payment saving}}=\frac{2{,}400}{50}=48$$

| Time keeping the loan | Payment savings | Savings minus upfront point cost |
|---|---|---|
| 24 months | 24 × \$50 = \$1,200 | −\$1,200 |
| 48 months | 48 × \$50 = \$2,400 | \$0 |
| 72 months | 72 × \$50 = \$3,600 | +\$1,200 |

This calculation only compares cash paid upfront with cumulative payment savings. A full economic comparison also considers the remaining loan balance at sale or refinancing, the timing of savings, other quote differences, taxes where relevant, and what else the upfront money could earn. A lower-rate loan can have a different remaining principal even when monthly savings have not yet repaid the point cost.

**APR**, the US annual percentage rate disclosure, combines interest with specified finance charges. It is broader than the interest rate but is not a complete homeownership budget or a personalized early-exit calculation. The [CFPB explains the APR comparison and its limits](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-difference-between-a-mortgage-interest-rate-and-an-apr-en-135/).

### When to use it

Use simple payback as a first screen, then compare total costs and remaining balances at plausible exit dates. A lender credit may reduce cash needed now in exchange for a higher rate. Choose a comparison horizon before treating either the lowest rate or the smallest upfront bill as the cheapest offer.
