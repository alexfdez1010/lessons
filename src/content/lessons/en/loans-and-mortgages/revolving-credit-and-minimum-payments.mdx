You pay €30 toward a €1,000 balance. Next month you still owe €990, even though you bought nothing. Where did the other €20 go? It paid interest. The payment was real; only part of it reduced the balance.

You already know principal, interest and amortization. **Revolving credit** lets you borrow, repay and borrow again within an agreed limit. Unlike a fixed instalment loan, the balance can rise through new spending and the required payment can change each billing period. This lesson builds a transparent monthly model rather than reproducing a particular card agreement.

## Follow the balance through one month

A revolving balance behaves like a tank with both an inlet and an outlet. Paying opens the outlet; borrowing and charges add water. Watching only the outlet will not tell you whether the level fell. The analogy describes the balance movements, not whether a credit agreement permits a particular payment.

Use a **stated monthly interest rate of 2%** throughout the main example. This is a rate for one month, supplied directly. We are not dividing a regulatory APR by twelve. Real agreements can calculate interest daily, use different balances or charge different rates for different transactions.

Let $B_t$ be the opening balance, $I_t$ interest, $N_t$ new purchases, $F_t$ fees and $P_t$ the payment during month $t$:

$$B_{t+1}=B_t+I_t+N_t+F_t-P_t$$

Our model first charges interest on the opening balance, then adds purchases, then takes the payment. It rounds monthly interest to the nearest cent, with half cents rounded up. No grace period or fees are included. A payment is capped at the amount due, so the model never creates a negative debt balance.

### Worked example

With no purchases or fees, opening balance **€1,000** and monthly rate **2%**:

1. Interest = €1,000 × 0.02 = **€20**.
2. Amount due before payment = €1,000 + €20 = **€1,020**.
3. Pay €30: closing balance = €1,020 − €30 = **€990**.
4. Balance reduction = €1,000 − €990 = **€10**, equal to payment €30 − interest €20.

Without new spending or fees, a payment equal to the €20 interest leaves the balance at €1,000. A €10 payment leaves €1,010: unpaid interest increases what remains owed. The agreement may require a larger payment; a mathematical projection is not evidence that the smaller payment is permitted.

### When to use it

Reconcile a statement's opening balance, charges, purchases, payments and closing balance. Then check the agreement's calculation method and dates. A different interest base or transaction order can change the result even when the headline rate looks the same.

## A changing minimum can repay the balance slowly

A **minimum payment** is the amount the agreement requires for that billing period, subject to its terms. It is a payment requirement rather than a promise to clear the balance quickly. Actual formulas vary by agreement; a provider's educational example is not a universal rule. [Chase, how minimum payments are calculated](https://www.chase.com/personal/credit-cards/education/basics/how-to-calculate-your-minimum-credit-card-payment)

For this lesson, invent the following rule:

$$P_{\min,t}=\min\left(B_t+I_t,\;\max\left(25,\;I_t+0.01B_t\right)\right)$$

Amounts are in euros, with no new spending or fees in this formula. Take the larger of **€25** or **monthly interest plus 1% of the opening balance**, then cap it at the amount due. Interest and the 1% component each round separately to cents, half up. In the explorer, any new purchases are added to the amount-due cap; the 1% part still uses the opening balance. These are invented teaching conventions.

### Worked monthly table

| Month | Opening balance | Interest at 2% | Rounded 1% component | Payment under the invented rule | Closing balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | €1,000.00 | €20.00 | €10.00 | €30.00 | €990.00 |
| 2 | €990.00 | €19.80 | €9.90 | €29.70 | €980.10 |
| 3 | €980.10 | €19.60 | €9.80 | €29.40 | €970.30 |

In month two, €19.80 + €9.90 = €29.70, which is above the €25 floor. Subtract it from €990 + €19.80 = €1,009.80 to get **€980.10**. The payment and the balance reduction both shrink.

Later, the €25 floor becomes larger than interest plus the 1% component. It then takes over. If only €15.39 is due in the final month, the payment is **€15.39**, not €25. That cap matters for an exact schedule.

### When to use it

Read the actual statement's minimum-payment calculation and required date. Paying that amount on time addresses that payment requirement; it does not mean interest stops, the account has no other obligations or the balance is paid in full.

## Compare a fixed payment on the same assumptions

Compare the invented minimum with a **fixed €50 payment each month**, capped at the amount due. Both start at €1,000, charge 2% monthly and have no new purchases, fees or rate changes.

| Month | Opening balance under fixed plan | Interest | Payment | Closing balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | €1,000.00 | €20.00 | €50.00 | €970.00 |
| 2 | €970.00 | €19.40 | €50.00 | €939.40 |
| 3 | €939.40 | €18.79 | €50.00 | €908.19 |

Month three interest is €939.40 × 0.02 = €18.788, rounded to **€18.79**. The closing balance is €939.40 + €18.79 − €50 = **€908.19**. The payment reduces the opening balance by €31.21; compare €9.80 in month three of the minimum-only plan.

With an 84-month horizon, both plans finish inside the displayed window:

| Result under the stated model | Fixed €50 plan | Invented minimum-only plan |

Check the fixed plan's last row: opening €39.10 + interest €0.78 = **€39.88**. Charging a full €50 at that point would overstate the payment. The interest difference between the plans is €886.90 − €289.88 = **€597.02** under these assumptions.

### When to use it

Compare affordable payment plans using the same rate, transaction and fee assumptions. A fixed payment below the required minimum is not a valid substitute for meeting that requirement. The explorer flags months where the chosen fixed amount falls below its invented minimum; that is a model check rather than a verdict about an actual agreement.

### Worked spending offset

Keep the €1,000 opening balance, 2% monthly interest and €50 payment, but add **€30 of purchases** after interest each month:

$$B_{t+1}=1000+20+30-50=1000$$

The fixed plan's balance stays at **€1,000** in this model. Each month €50 leaves the borrower's bank account, but €20 of interest plus €30 of new borrowing replaces it. Over twelve months the payments total €600 and interest totals €240; the debt still has not fallen.

The minimum-only path behaves differently because its payment follows its own changing rule. Read both plotted paths and ledgers rather than assuming the spending offset affects them identically.

### What the simple model leaves out

- **Fees** add to the balance or cash cost and may affect the required minimum. Our explorer excludes them.
- **Variable rates** change future interest. A rate that starts low is not enough to establish the full repayment schedule.
- **Daily timing and different balance types** can change interest and payment allocation. A monthly model with one rate cannot reproduce every purchase, cash advance or transfer balance.
- **Grace periods and residual interest** depend on the agreement and payment history. This model starts with a balance that incurs interest; it does not simulate an interest-free purchase period. [Capital One, payment and interest explanations](https://www.capitalone.com/help-center/credit-cards/payments-credit-card-support/)

For covered U.S. credit-card statements, disclosure rules generally require a minimum-payment warning and repayment illustrations, including a three-year comparison where applicable. The estimates assume no additional charges and have specified calculation assumptions. These U.S. disclosures illustrate why assumptions matter; they do not supply our invented euro rule. [CFPB, periodic-statement rules](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/7/)

### When to use it

Treat a payoff date as conditional on the inputs. Check the current agreement, balance, transactions, fees, rate and minimum-payment requirements, then update the projection when they change. If the chosen horizon ends with debt still outstanding, report that balance and interest incurred so far; do not label it the lifetime cost.
