“Your payment falls from €88.85 to €45.96” is an attractive refinancing headline. If the new loan runs twice as long, however, some of that monthly relief comes from repaying principal more slowly. At the sale date, the unpaid debt is still waiting for you.

This lesson compares the cash you pay **and the debt you still owe**, on the same dates. It also distinguishes a late payment, a contractual breach and enforcement against a home. The financial examples are invented; legal procedures below are tied to their named jurisdictions rather than treated as a universal mortgage timetable.

## Extra principal reduces the balance on which interest is charged

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="You apply €100 directly to principal before the next month's interest at 1%. Ignoring fees, how much lower is that month's interest?" options={[{text:'€1',correct:true},{text:'€100, automatically'},{text:'€101'},{text:'Nothing until the final year'}]} explanation="The balance is €100 smaller, so this month's interest is lower by 100 × 1% = €1. Later savings depend on the payment path and time remaining." />

An **extra principal payment** is money applied to reduce the outstanding amount borrowed, beyond the required instalment. It is different from paying interest, adding to an escrow account for taxes or merely advancing a future scheduled payment. Ask how the servicer applies the extra money and verify the balance on the statement. The [US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency](https://www.helpwithmybank.gov/help-topics/mortgages-home-equity/payments-late-payments/mortgage-payment-principal.html) directs borrowers to their agreement and bank's process for this distinction.

With monthly interest rate $i$, opening balance $B$ and an extra principal payment $E$ applied before that month's interest, the immediate interest reduction is $iE$. The savings are not automatically greater than the extra payment.

Take €1,000 at a directly stated **1% monthly rate**, with a €600 payment at the end of month 1 and the exact remainder at the end of month 2:

| Event | No extra principal | €100 extra at date 0 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Balance before month 1 interest | €1,000 | €900 |
| Month 1 interest | €10 | €9 |
| After paying €600 at month 1 end | €410 | €309 |
| Month 2 interest | €4.10 | €3.09 |
| Final payment | €414.10 | €312.09 |
| All principal and interest paid, including the extra | €1,014.10 | €1,012.09 |

The extra €100 saves **€2.01 of interest**, not €100 or more. Interest falls because the balance is lower earlier. It is not a separate fee secretly charged at the beginning of every amortising loan. With the same rate and agreed recurring payment maintained, an extra payment can bring final payoff forward; a permitted **recast**, which recalculates the instalment, can instead lower later payments while retaining the term.

A **prepayment charge** is a contractual charge for specified early repayments where permitted by the applicable law. Not every loan has one, and small extra payments and full refinancing can have different rules. See the [CFPB explanation of prepayment penalties](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-prepayment-penalty-en-1957/), which is specifically about US mortgages.

### When to use this comparison

Compare the dated after-tax interest and charges avoided with the cash you give up now. A principal payment reduces liquid cash; it is not a freely withdrawable savings account. The best choice depends on the loan terms, other obligations and the borrower's cash needs, rather than a promise that prepayment always wins.

## A new loan changes several terms at once

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A fee of €500 was paid last year and cannot now be refunded. A new refinance quote requires €200 payable only if accepted. Which fee is incremental to today's choice?" options={[{text:'The new €200',correct:true},{text:'Only last year’s €500'},{text:'Both fees must be paid again today'},{text:'Neither fee affects any comparison'}]} explanation="The irrecoverable €500 is a sunk cost at today's decision date. The €200 changes depending on whether the new offer is accepted and belongs in the forward comparison." />

**Refinancing** uses a new loan to repay an existing one. It can change the rate, remaining term, payment structure, required insurance, fees and borrowing amount. **Cash-out refinancing** borrows extra cash as well; the cash receipt and extra debt both belong in its comparison. A **modification** changes the existing contract instead. These are different transactions.

In Spain, Banco de España distinguishes [novation, a change agreed with the existing lender, from creditor subrogation, a move to another lender](https://clientebancario.bde.es/pcb/es/menu-horizontal/productosservici/financiacion/hipotecas/guia-textual/vidahipoteca/Novacion_o_camb_d1cc24e53ab1d51.html). Costs and legal steps depend on the transaction and applicable regime. An English-language refinance calculator does not establish the Spanish legal route.

List cash flows that **change between the alternatives**. If a €20 new origination fee and €5 old-loan payoff charge are both paid now, initial incremental cost is €25. An irrecoverable €40 fee paid when the old loan began does not become a new €40 outflow today. An existing charge that is refundable only on payoff, by contrast, does change with the decision.

Keep principal-and-interest payments separate from unrelated differences in property tax estimates or deposits. The [CFPB's loan-offer comparison](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/compare/compare-loan-estimates/) asks borrowers to examine payments, insurance, fees and lender credits, and warns that low tax or insurance estimates do not make the lender's loan cheaper.

### When to use the alternatives list

Use it before comparing headline rates. Put both offers on a common date, loan amount and planned exit horizon, then identify genuine differences. Where an interest-only phase, balloon or variable rate is involved, use its actual schedule; the [repayment-structure lesson](/loans-and-mortgages/interest-only-balloon-and-negative-amortization) explains why the first instalment can conceal a later obligation.

## Fees divided by payment savings is a limited cash checkpoint

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Fees are €3,600 and the constant principal-and-interest payment falls by €150 per month. What does 3,600/150 = 24 identify by itself?" options={[{text:'When undiscounted payment differences add up to the upfront fees',correct:true},{text:'A complete wealth comparison including debt still owed'},{text:'A guaranteed positive NPV after month 24'},{text:'A rule independent of the remaining loan terms'}]} explanation="The ratio compares cumulative payment differences with an upfront amount. It does not measure how much principal remains, discount the timing or include omitted charges." />

For an upfront incremental fee $F$ and a **constant positive** monthly payment difference $S$, the familiar calculation is

$$
h_{\text{cash}}=F/S.
$$

With $F=3{,}600$ and $S=150$, it gives 24 months. At 12 months, the payment differences total $12(150)=1{,}800$, leaving €1,800 of fees unrecovered by those differences. At 24 they total €3,600. At 60 they total €9,000, or €5,400 more than the fee **on this limited undiscounted cash measure**.

Calling everything after that date “pure savings” ignores unpaid principal, timing and any other changed cash flows. The ratio can be useful for a first check when terms are comparable. If payments vary, stop when one loan ends, or turn into a balloon, calculate the actual dated differences instead of multiplying one initial saving forever. If $S\le0$, there is no positive crossing from this formula; that alone does not establish the result of the complete comparison.

A quote saving €200 per month with €7,200 fees gives a 36-month cash checkpoint. Selling in month 30 means only €6,000 of those differences have arrived. It does not by itself prove the complete refinance loses €1,200: the two payoff balances, any changed cash flows and their dates are still missing.

### When this checkpoint helps

Use it to spot an offer with fees large relative to immediate payment relief. Label it as a payment-based checkpoint. The full comparison at sale or payoff includes what both choices leave to repay on that same date.

## Include the debt left at the same exit date

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="After six payments, refinancing has reduced your cash payments by €257.34 but leaves €252.72 more debt than keeping the loan. Fees were €20 paid now. Ignoring discounting, what is the complete difference?" options={[{text:'−€15.38: 257.34 − 252.72 − 20',correct:true},{text:'+€237.34: count only payments and fees'},{text:'+€490.06: treat extra debt as an asset'},{text:'€0, because a lower rate must break even'}]} explanation="The extra debt must be repaid at the common exit. It offsets most of the lower payments, and the €20 fee exceeds the remaining undiscounted benefit." />

**Net present value**, or NPV, translates cash flows on different dates into one comparison-date amount using an explicit discount rate. A positive *refinancing benefit* below favours the refinance under the model; a negative result favours keeping the loan on that financial measure.

Use old and new principal-and-interest payments $A_{O,t},A_{N,t}$ and balances **after** the final payment at exit month $H$, $B_{O,H},B_{N,H}$. With monthly comparison discount rate $d$ and fees $F_0$ actually paid now,

$$
\text{NPV benefit}=-F_0+
\sum_{t=1}^{H}\frac{A_{O,t}-A_{N,t}}{(1+d)^t}
+\frac{B_{O,H}-B_{N,H}}{(1+d)^H}.
$$

The last term is essential: it is positive when refinancing leaves less debt and negative when it leaves more. Assume both loans are repaid at their remaining principal immediately after that last payment, with no further accrued interest or exit charges. If actual payoff quotes include such charges, add their differences. The same property and sale price cancel from the comparison; differing sale costs or other cash flows would not.

### Worked example with a longer new term

Start now with €1,000 outstanding. Rates below are **monthly interest rates defined directly**, not regulatory APRs divided by twelve. Interest is rounded half up to cents each month, the scheduled instalment to cents, and the final payment settles the exact remaining due.

| Term | Keep current loan | Refinance |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Starting principal | €1,000 | €1,000 |
| Monthly rate | 1% | 0.8% |
| Remaining payments | 12 | 24 |
| Scheduled payment | €88.85 | €45.96 |
| New fees paid now | €0 | €20 |

The level-payment formula is $A=Pi/[1-(1+i)^{-n}]$. For the current loan it gives €88.8488…, rounded to €88.85; the new loan gives €45.9605…, rounded to €45.96.

| Month end | Current payment | Current balance | New payment | New balance |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | €88.85 | €921.15 | €45.96 | €962.04 |
| 2 | €88.85 | €841.51 | €45.96 | €923.78 |
| 3 | €88.85 | €761.08 | €45.96 | €885.21 |
| 4 | €88.85 | €679.84 | €45.96 | €846.33 |
| 5 | €88.85 | €597.79 | €45.96 | €807.14 |
| 6 | €88.85 | €514.92 | €45.96 | €767.64 |

For example, current month 2 interest is $921.15(0.01)=9.2115$, rounded to €9.21; its balance becomes $921.15+9.21-88.85=841.51$. New month 2 interest is $962.04(0.008)=7.69632$, rounded to €7.70; its balance becomes €923.78.

The initial payment saving is $88.85-45.96=42.89$, so the simple fee ratio is $20/42.89\approx0.47$ months. Actual payment differences first exceed fees at month 1. Yet after six payments the extra new debt is $767.64-514.92=252.72$.

Use a directly stated monthly discount rate of **0.5%**. Discounted payment differences total approximately €252.90 and the discounted balance difference is approximately −€245.27:

$$
-20+\sum_{t=1}^{6}\frac{42.89}{1.005^t}
+\frac{514.92-767.64}{1.005^6}
\approx-12.37.
$$

The refinance loses €12.37 of NPV benefit in this six-month comparison, despite the lower rate and payment. Choosing a discount rate is an assumption about timing and opportunity cost, not a guaranteed way to invest the payment savings.

### When to use this calculation

Use the same sale, payoff or review date for both alternatives. Test several plausible dates if the horizon is uncertain. A short-horizon result does not decide a lifetime comparison, and a discounted cost comparison does not establish whether either payment fits the household's budget.

## A longer term and financed fees change the balance sheet

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="The lender adds a €20 transaction fee to a €1,000 replacement loan and requires no upfront cash for that fee. What is the new starting debt?" options={[{text:'€1,020',correct:true},{text:'€1,000, because no cash changed hands'},{text:'€980, because the lender absorbed the fee'},{text:'€20, because the old principal disappears'}]} explanation="Financing changes when the fee is paid. The borrower now owes the extra €20, and pays interest on it under the new loan's terms." />

Financing a fee avoids the immediate cash outflow but increases the replacement principal. In the example, financing €20 makes new principal **€1,020**. Put zero for that fee in $F_0$ and calculate the new payments and exit balance from €1,020. Counting both an upfront €20 and the same financed €20 as separate borrower payments would double-count the fee.

A longer term spreads principal over more payments. It does not impose a special “restart fee” equal to the early interest share. In our example, if both loans run to their own scheduled end, current payments total $11(88.85)+88.84=1{,}066.19$, or €66.19 interest. The new loan totals $23(45.96)+45.98=1{,}103.06$, or €103.06 interest, before its €20 fee. The rate is lower, but the debt lasts longer. This undiscounted lifetime total uses a different horizon measure from the six-month NPV.

Compare a replacement with the **same remaining 12-month term**, as well as the 24-month extension. Adjust the new term in the panel and inspect both the payment and the remaining balance. A lower payment can be useful for cash flow even if it costs more financially; that is a trade-off to identify explicitly.

### When to test more scenarios

Use actual adjustable-rate reset paths, insurance changes and future balloon obligations where relevant. “No upfront costs” can involve financed costs or a higher rate rather than a free transaction. Cash-out is a different borrowing amount and needs its initial cash receipt included. This simulator models neither rate uncertainty nor those additional cash flows.

## A payment breach and a foreclosure are different legal events

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A borrower misses a payment. Which statement can be made without assuming one country's contract and enforcement rules?" options={[{text:'There is a payment arrear to address; the legal consequences depend on the contract and applicable law',correct:true},{text:'No contractual default can exist until day 90 everywhere'},{text:'The lender immediately owns the home'},{text:'Any home equity is automatically confiscated'}]} explanation="A payment can be late and also breach the contract. Default definitions, notices, cure rights, acceleration and enforcement procedures are not a universal sequence of fixed day counts." />

**Delinquency** refers to a payment being overdue. **Default** means a failure to meet a contractual obligation; a missed payment can already be a contractual default. There is no universal rule that delinquency stays outside default until 90 days. A grace period, reporting threshold, right to cure and legal foreclosure restriction are different rules, not interchangeable definitions.

An **acceleration clause** can allow the lender to demand the remaining debt after qualifying breaches and required procedures. It does not necessarily operate automatically on the first late payment. For a €240,000 balance, acceleration could turn the demanded amount from a missed €1,500 instalment into a claim for that larger balance plus permitted charges; it does not mean the borrower borrowed another €240,000.

**Foreclosure** is enforcement against the mortgaged property under the applicable legal process. Default is a breach; foreclosure is a potential enforcement response. Some US jurisdictions use a judicial process and others allow specified nonjudicial sale procedures. Neither label supplies a universal duration, protection ranking or rule that all equity disappears.

For example, the [CFPB's US foreclosure timeline](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-will-it-take-before-ill-face-foreclosure-if-i-cant-make-my-mortgage-payments-what-is-the-foreclosure-timeline-en-1849/) says the legal process generally cannot start until a mortgage is at least 120 days behind, with exceptions and state-dependent timing after that. This is a scoped US procedural statement; it does not define when every contract defaults, set the sale date or establish Spanish rules.

### Sale proceeds, surplus and shortfall

**Negative equity** means the property value is below the secured debt. A €280,000 property against €310,000 debt has €30,000 negative equity before selling costs. It can coexist with on-time payments; conversely, a borrower with positive equity can miss payments because liquid cash is short.

Suppose a sale actually raises €280,000, stated prior sale costs are €10,000 and the debt claim is €310,000. With no other claims, net proceeds are $280{,}000-10{,}000=270{,}000$ and the numerical shortfall is $310{,}000-270{,}000=40{,}000$. Personal liability for a **deficiency** depends on the loan, applicable law, procedure and any settlement; the arithmetic does not establish an enforceable judgment.

If the same sale raises €360,000 instead, net proceeds are €350,000 and €40,000 remain after that debt claim. A borrower can have a right to a surplus after costs and priority claims through the relevant distribution process. [New York Courts](https://www.nycourts.gov/help/homes-evictions/foreclosure-judgments) explicitly distinguishes surplus proceeds from a deficiency and describes an application for surplus funds. Losing the home does not automatically mean every remaining euro belongs to the lender.

### When these distinctions matter

Use the contract, current notices and local official guidance to identify actual options and deadlines. Contact the servicer and a qualified local housing or legal adviser early when payments become difficult. A modification, forbearance or agreed sale may have different eligibility and consequences; delaying payments deliberately is not a universal financial shortcut, and handing back keys alone does not establish debt discharge.
