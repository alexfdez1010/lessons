A credits your account with a new loan. You can see a larger deposit, but the central bank has not just opened a reserve account for you. Lending and settling your next payment are connected transactions with different entries.

Return to the opening books from lesson 1: A has reserves 50, bonds 150, loans 800, deposits 900 and equity 100. H has deposit 100 and bonds 100. B has reserves 30, bonds 70, loans 400, deposits 450 and equity 50. Each visual resets to these balances; it does not accumulate operations from the previous one. All amounts are euros. Ignore interest, fees, valuation and defaults here.

## Origination adds a loan and a deposit

**Origination** means creating the loan contract and recording the initial advance. In our case A lends 100 by crediting H's account. It adds a customer-loan asset 100 and a deposit liability 100. No other customer's account is debited:

$$
A:\quad 50+150+900=1100=1000+100.
$$

The last 100 on the right is equity, unchanged. H's assets become deposit 200 plus bonds 100, but H now owes loan 100. Net worth is $200+100-100=200$, the same as before. H has a more liquid asset and a new obligation, not a free increase in wealth.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="loan" repaymentPct={0} title="Post the two entries that originate the loan" caption={"Initial advance 100 and no principal repayment. A gains a loan asset and deposit liability; H gains a deposit and matching debt. The reserve balances do not change at origination."} />

The [Bank of England explains that lending creates matching deposits](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/explainers/how-is-money-created). That description does not mean a bank can profitably lend any amount to anyone. Capital, credit assessment, funding terms and regulation still matter. Nor must every loan advance be credited at the lending bank in practice; settlement elsewhere needs the additional payment entries we will trace.

### When to use it

Use the paired balance sheets to check a loan story. If deposits rise but both the bank's loan asset and the borrower's debt are missing, the story is incomplete. “Money creation” and “wealth creation” are different propositions.

**Principal** is the amount borrowed still owed, separate from interest. Starting with the new 100 loan, let H repay 30 principal from its deposit at A. A debits H's deposit 30 and reduces the loan asset 30. Relative to the original opening books, loans and deposits have each risen 70:

H's deposit is 170, bonds 100 and loan debt 70. Net worth remains $170+100-70=200$. A still has $50+150+870=1070$ assets against deposits 970 and equity 100.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="loan" title="Keep principal separate from income" caption={"Initial scenario: loan 100 followed by 30 % principal repayment at the same bank. The visual shows the net posted balances and separately reports the amount repaid."} />

**Pitfall.** A principal repayment is not bank revenue. An interest payment, fee, write-off or legal debt forgiveness has different entries. In a cross-bank repayment, settlement reserves also move between institutions; do not infer that from our same-bank example without adding the other bank.

### When to use it

Separate the components on a loan statement. The stock of loans can fall through principal repayment without a loan-loss event, and deposits can be cancelled through repayment without the funds being “lent out again”.

## A payment moves existing balances

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question={"A loses 20 reserves in a payment and B gains 20. What changes in the reserve sum?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "Zero",
    "correct": true
  },
  {
    "text": "Plus 20",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "Minus 20",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"The transfer redistributes existing reserves without changing the central bank’s total liability."}
/>


Reset to the original books. H gives 20 to K at B. This is an **uncompensated transfer**: no purchased good, financial asset or invoice is assumed. A reduces the deposit owed to H 20 and transfers reserves 20 to B. B gains reserves 20 and credits K's deposit 20.

$$
R_A=50-20=30,\quad R_B=30+20=50,\quad R_A+R_B=80.
$$

Total deposits are $(900-20)+(450+20)=1350$, unchanged. H's net worth falls 200→180; K's rises 0→20. Their combined net worth stays 200. Bank equity stays 100 and 50. The central bank changes the ownership of its reserve liability, not the total owed.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="payment" title="Distinguish redistribution from an aggregate increase" caption={"Initial transfer 20 from H at A to K at B. No loan is originated here. The control never exceeds A’s opening reserves, so the transfer needs no assumed overdraft."} />

The [ECB's liquidity explanation](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/ecb-and-you/explainers/tell-me-more/html/excess_liquidity.en.html) describes banks' use of reserves for payments and short-term obligations. A bank-to-bank reserve loan can redistribute available reserves too; it does not by itself increase the banking system's aggregate reserve stock.

### When to use it

Keep the bank-specific and aggregate questions separate. Losing deposits can create a funding need for A even if B gains exactly the same amount. Looking only at B's credit invents an aggregate deposit increase that never occurred.

## Creating a deposit does not settle every future payment

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question={"Net new deposits 70 may be sent to another bank. A has 50 reserves. What is still needed under no overdraft?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "No funding because deposits are reserves",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "A new loan 70 automatically granted by the central bank",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "Explicit funding 20 before full settlement",
    "correct": true
  }
]}
  explanation={"The separate payment would need 70 settlement reserves,20 more than A holds."}
/>


Now consider the origination and repayment case: the net new deposit is 70, while A still holds 50 reserves. If H next sends **all of that net new 70** to B, the assumed settlement needs 70 reserves. The shortfall is $70-50=20$. This possible later transfer has **not** been posted in the visual.

A must obtain the missing 20 before full settlement under our no-overdraft assumption. An inflow from another bank, an eligible borrowing facility or an asset sale could provide it, with counterpart entries, access conditions and costs. A loan 50 against collateral is not the same as selling an asset 50, and neither can be assumed available merely because a payment is desired.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="loan" loan={100} repaymentPct={30} title="Check the next settlement requirement separately" caption={"The posted loan and repayment leave net new deposits 70 and A reserves 50. The separate funding test reports 20 needed if all 70 are then sent to B. It does not post the transfer or supply missing reserves."} />

**Pitfall.** “Loans create deposits” does not make liquidity constraints disappear. The opposite shortcut also fails: assuming the central bank must first multiply reserves into each loan misdescribes these entries. The [BoE's fuller monetary-creation account](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2014/q1/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy) distinguishes deposit creation from reserve and behavioural constraints.

### When to use it

Ask three questions in order: what contract was originated, what account was credited, and what settlement funding is needed when the customer acts? Then check the capital and risk decision as well. The next lesson provides distinct central-bank funding and asset-purchase transactions.
