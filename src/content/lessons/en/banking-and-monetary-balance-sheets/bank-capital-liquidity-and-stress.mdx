“Has enough assets” and “can pay this transfer now” are different tests. A bank can fail either, and trying to solve one can make the other worse. We will trace the entries instead of treating every form of support as the same thing.

Each stress below is an independent invented case with reserves 50, bonds 150, customer loans 800, deposits 900 and equity 100 unless a different starting point is stated. No hidden central-bank advance, insurance payout or asset-price recovery fills a gap. These are teaching scenarios, not predictions or a determination of an actual bank's legal solvency.

## Credit losses reduce assets and equity

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question={"Loans lose 30 from an opening equity 100. With deposits unchanged, what equity remains?"}
  options={[

    "text": "100",
    "correct": false

  {
    "text": "130",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "70",
    "correct": true
  }
]}
  explanation={"The asset loss reduces the residual equity by 30."}

Suppose the bank records a 30 credit loss on its loan assets, with no other transaction. Loans fall 800→770 and equity 100→70; deposits remain 900 and reserves 50:

$$
\text{Assets}=50+150+770=970=900+70.
$$

This **accounting write-down** is not a stated legal forgiveness of the borrower's debt. It records an asset loss. Collection rights, provisions, recoveries and their timing require their own facts. Nor did the write-down automatically pay depositors or move reserves.

Now add an **invented capital-only test**: all shown equity qualifies, loans receive 100 % risk weight, bonds and reserves 0 %, and the minimum ratio is 10 %. Here risk-weighted assets sum recorded asset amounts times the stipulated weights. They become 770, so $70/770=9.090909\%$: the bank fails this test despite positive accounting equity 70. With a loss 20 instead, $80/780=10.256410\%$, a pass for this test alone.

<BankFundingLab client:visible view="credit" title="Distinguish a positive residual from a stated capital pass" caption={"Initial loan loss 30 leaves equity 70 and toy risk-weighted assets 770. The invented 10 % minimum is not a universal regulatory requirement; the visual also distinguishes negative equity from a failed positive-equity capital ratio."} />

A loss 120 would leave assets 880, liabilities 900 and equity −20. The identity still adds up; the negative equity is not repaired by displaying the balance sheet differently. Actual regulatory capital definitions can differ from book equity, and meeting one ratio does not satisfy every capital, leverage, buffer or supervisory requirement. The [Basel Framework](https://www.bis.org/committees/bcbs/basel-framework) describes distinct standards and supervisory processes; implementation depends on jurisdiction and scope.

### When to use it

Specify valuation basis, loss recognition and the applicable capital definition before interpreting a ratio. **Book assets covering shown liabilities**, **positive equity**, **regulatory compliance** and **liquidity** are separate statements.

Keep the loss 30 case and suppose an outside investor buys 40 of newly issued shares with fully funded cash. The bank receives reserves 40 and equity 40, not a debt 40. Its updated book is reserves 90, bonds 150 and loans 770: assets 1010 against deposits 900 and equity 110. The toy ratio is $110/770=14.285714\%$.

The investor surrenders a bank deposit 40 and records a 40 share investment at subscription cost. Its settlement bank loses reserves 40 and deposit liabilities 40. Those counterpart entries are shown in the table. The fully funded outside sending bank is separate from bank B, which starts with 30 reserves. The investor has exchanged assets at the stipulated subscription price, not received shares without paying. Actual share values and future returns are not guaranteed by this entry.

The bank gains reserves and equity; the investor and sending-bank entries identify the funding source. No new aggregate reserves are asserted."} />

Contrast a secured central-bank loan 40 from lesson 3, starting from the **original loss-free books**. It increases reserves 50→90 and adds central-bank debt 40; equity stays 100. An equal-carrying-value asset purchase swaps assets and leaves equity unchanged too.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="central" operation="lendBank" title="Do not classify a loan’s proceeds as new equity" caption={"Separate original-book comparison: the secured central-bank advance 40 creates reserves and repayment debt, not a shareholder contribution. It does not reverse the credit loss in the preceding scenario."} />

### When to use it

Ask which claimant supplies funding and which claim it receives. Borrowing can provide payment resources without absorbing an existing loss. Equity can absorb losses, but access, valuation and settlement still matter. The [Fed's capital/liquidity explanation](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/cat_21427.htm) is a useful starting distinction, not a guarantee that any particular support is available.

## Selling assets can fund withdrawals and realise a loss

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question={"To raise 50 by selling at 80 % of book value, how much book value must be sold?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "62.50",
    "correct": true
  },
  {
    "text": "50.00",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "40.00",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"50/.8 = 62.5; the sale realises a 12.5 loss."}
/>


Reset to the original loss-free bank. Customers request a 100 transfer out to other banks. Paying from existing reserves 50 leaves funding need 50. Suppose the bank can sell its bonds immediately at **80 % of carrying value**, a 20 % sale discount. To obtain 50 cash it sells:

$$
\text{Bond carrying amount sold}=\frac{50}{0.8}=62.5.
$$

It loses $62.5-50=12.5$ on sale. After settling the full 100 transfer, reserves are 0, bonds 87.5 and loans 800. Deposits fall 900→800 and equity falls 100→87.5:

$$
887.5=800+87.5.
$$

This calculation assumes a buyer willing and able to execute at that price. The discount is a realised loss because an asset recorded 62.5 is sold for 50. Market price, book value and regulatory treatment can differ in actual reporting; the example stipulates the accounting amount.

<BankFundingLab client:visible view="liquidity" liquidityRoute="sell" title="Reconcile the cash raised and the asset loss" caption={"Initial transfer request 100 and sale discount 20 %. Selling 62.5 of bond carrying amount raises 50, realises 12.5 loss and completes the transfer. Other assets are not silently sold."} />

If the request is 200, the entire bond pool 150 raises only 120. Together with reserves 50, the bank settles 170 and leaves **30 unpaid**. It does not report a completed 200 transfer or a negative reserve overdraft. The sale then leaves loans 800 as assets against deposits 730 and equity 70; the remaining request still needs a resolution.

### When to use it

A liquidity action can change capital through a sale loss. An initial positive net-worth figure does not show that assets can all be sold at recorded prices or by the required deadline. The [Bank of England discusses withdrawal pressure and bank runs](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/explainers/why-do-we-regulate-banks); regulation can reduce risks without making bank failure impossible.

## A collateral haircut limits the advance without being a sale loss

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question={"A 20 % collateral haircut necessarily means which cash fee?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "A fee equal to 20 % of all deposits",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "A realised sale loss on every pledged bond",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "No fixed fee; haircut limits the advance",
    "correct": true
  }
]}
  explanation={"The retained collateral and loan debt differ from a sold asset."}
/>


Again reset the original bank. Suppose a lender accepts the unencumbered bonds as collateral and applies a **20 % haircut**: it will advance 80 % of their carrying amount under our assumed valuation. Raising 50 therefore requires pledging $50/0.8=62.5$ of bonds. This is numerically the same 62.5 as the sale case, but the ownership and entries differ.

The bank keeps all 150 bonds and incurs new secured debt 50. After paying the requested 100 transfer, reserves are 0, assets 950 and the funding side is deposits 800 plus secured debt 50 plus equity 100:

$$
950=800+50+100.
$$

There is no 12.5 sale loss because no sale occurred. The haircut is an advance limit, not a cash fee of 12.5. This does not make borrowing costless: interest, fees, collateral encumbrance, margin calls (demands for extra collateral) and repayment can matter. They are omitted here, not guaranteed absent. Lender access and collateral eligibility are explicit assumptions.

<BankFundingLab client:visible view="liquidity" liquidityRoute="borrow" request={200} title="Preserve the unpaid amount when collateral capacity is exhausted" caption={"Initial request 200, collateral pool 150 and haircut 20 %. The assumed lender advances at most 120; with opening reserves 50, only 170 is settled and 30 remains unpaid. Pledged bonds stay owned, new debt 120 is retained and equity stays 100."} />

Even this borrowing route cannot fund unlimited requests. For a request 200, the advance limit 120 plus reserves 50 still permits only 170 settlement. The bank now owes 120 to the secured lender; equity remains 100, but that does not complete the pending 30 transfer. A collateral valuation change or withdrawal of access would require another scenario.

### When to use it

Compare the full balance sheets and deadlines, not just cash raised. A sale discount and haircut can have the same percentage and different consequences. Stress tests should state losses, outflows, prices, eligible collateral and assumed responses. They are conditional calculations, not failure probabilities or forecasts of public support. The course has now supplied the accounting needed to ask those questions without confusing deposits, reserves, capital and income.
