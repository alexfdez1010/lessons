Your banking app says €100. The bank's books say it **owes** €100. Both can be correct: you and your bank are looking at opposite sides of the same claim. This course follows those pairs through lending, payments, central-bank operations and bank stress.

We use invented euros throughout, not current bank data. The first three lessons share an opening set of books; later examples explicitly reset the balances. Assume immediate settlement, no banknotes, fees, interest or valuation changes unless stated. Other customers and issuers exist even when they are outside a diagram.

## One claim has two counterparties

An **asset** is a resource or claim owned. A **liability** is an obligation owed. Your commercial-bank deposit is your claim on the bank; it is a deposit liability on the bank's balance sheet. A **balance sheet** records such stocks at a particular moment, rather than summing transactions over a year.

Household H starts with deposit 100 at A and bonds 100. H has assets 200 and no debt in this example. A's deposit liabilities total 900, **including H's 100**. The other 800 belongs to customers outside the picture. Adding H's 100 again to A's 900 would count the same deposit twice.

The bond is another claim: its issuer owes the contractual payments. We omit that issuer's full balance sheet, not its obligation. A reader should be able to name the counterparty even when a diagram has limited space.

**Pitfall.** A customer deposit does not become shareholder equity because the bank can use its funding. The bank still owes the customer. Conversely, a loan to a customer is an asset **of the bank**, but a debt **of the borrower**.

### When to use it

Before interpreting a headline about “new money”, specify holder, issuer, currency and date. The [Bank of England's money-creation explanation](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/explainers/how-is-money-created) distinguishes bank deposits from the central-bank balances banks use for settlement.

## The bank’s equity is a residual

**Equity**, also called accounting net worth here, is assets minus liabilities. It belongs to the owners after the other shown claims. It is not a labelled compartment of reserves waiting to be spent:

$$
A=L+E,\qquad E=A-L.
$$

A owns reserves 50, bonds 150 and customer loans 800. Total assets are $50+150+800=1000$. Against deposits 900, equity is $1000-900=100$. All entries are in euros. The bank has equity 100 but reserves 50; those two numbers need not coincide.

A loan asset can be valuable without being immediately spendable settlement money. An obligation can be due soon even if the bank has positive net worth. Later lessons turn that distinction into a dated funding test. For now, keep **the balance-sheet identity** separate from **how quickly assets can be used to pay**.

**Pitfall.** Neither “assets 1000” nor “equity 100” says that the bank can transfer that amount instantly. And a balance sheet that adds up is an accounting consistency check, not proof that every asset is correctly valued.

### When to use it

Use the identity to find missing entries. If an asset genuinely loses 20 and no other item changes, equity must fall 20. A paid-in equity contribution is a different transaction with an investor and funding entries. The [Federal Reserve explains the distinction between capital and liquidity](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/cat_21427.htm); our later stress examples will keep them separate.

## Reserves belong to another money layer

<MCQ

  explanation={"Sum the reserve claims 50 + 30; deposits and equity have other perimeters."}
/>


A **reserve balance** is a bank's account claim on the central bank. For A it is an asset; for the central bank it is a liability. The [Bank of England's operating guide](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/markets/bank-of-england-market-operations-guide/our-objectives) describes reserves held by eligible firms and their settlement role. Ordinary households in our examples have commercial-bank deposits, not reserve accounts. Access depends on the actual jurisdiction and institution.

The model central bank owns bonds 100 and owes reserves 80, leaving equity 20. A holds 50 reserves and B 30:

$$
R_A+R_B=50+30=80=\text{central-bank reserve liabilities}.
$$

B separately owns bonds 70 and customer loans 400. Its assets $30+70+400=500$ fund deposits 450 and equity 50. The combined customer-deposit stock is $900+450=1350$. The reserve stock 80 is not another 1350 of household deposits, nor is it the shareholders' equity 150.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="layers" title="Reconcile the reserve total across the two banks" caption={"A and B hold all 80 of this model’s reserves. The central bank owes that same 80. Customer deposits 1350 and bank equity 150 answer different questions; they are not interchangeable measures of money."} />

**Broad money** is a statistical grouping of qualifying monetary liabilities held by specified sectors. Its coverage varies by measure. Our diagrams track customer deposits and bank reserves explicitly; they do not calculate M 3, a particular broad-money aggregate used in the euro area. Adding every financial asset in the picture would double-count claims and mix categories.

### When to use it

Use a sum only after defining its perimeter. One bank's reserves, total banking-system reserves, customer deposits and bank equity each have a legitimate use. A transaction can change one while leaving another unchanged. That is the puzzle the next lesson resolves with actual entries.
