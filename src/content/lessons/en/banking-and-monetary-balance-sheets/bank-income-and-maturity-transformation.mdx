A bank can collect more interest when rates rise and still see its margin shrink: the cost of its funding may rise faster. It can also have a profitable two-year asset while facing a payment it cannot fund next year. Income and payment timing deserve separate calculations.

This lesson uses two **new** invented examples. The income bank has assets 1000 funded by deposits 800, wholesale debt 100 and equity 100. It is not the previous opening bank with deposits 900. Keep volumes constant for a full year's interest forecast. The later maturity example has asset 100, debt 90 and equity 10. All amounts are euros, with no taxes or fees beyond those expressly added.

## Net interest income measures a flow

**Interest income** comes from interest-bearing assets. **Interest expense** is owed on interest-bearing funding. **Net interest income**, NII, subtracts those annual flows:

$$\mathrm{NII}=\text{Interest income}-\text{Interest expense}.$$

Our bank owns loans 800 at 5 %, bonds 150 at 3 % and reserves 50 at 2 %. Annual income is $800(0.05)+150(0.03)+50(0.02)=40+4.5+1=45.5$. Deposits 800 cost 1 % and wholesale debt 100 costs 2 %, so expense is $8+2=10$. **NII is 35.5 euros per year.** Wholesale debt here means borrowing from banks or other financial-market investors, separate from the shown customer deposits.

<BankFundingLab client:visible view="income" title="Calculate the annual interest contribution" caption={"Initial annual interest income 45.5 less expense 10 gives NII 35.5. The forecast fixes the stated asset and funding volumes throughout the year. Percentage measures use a separate denominator."} />

**Pitfall.** The bank's 900 funding principal is not an annual interest expense 900. Nor is a customer principal repayment income. Units and period matter: NII is an amount accumulated over a period, not a stock or a rate.

### When to use it

Use NII to measure the contribution from interest-bearing business over a stated period. Compare compatible accrual definitions, hedging treatment and horizons. Non-interest costs and credit provisions still need their own entries before deriving profit.

## Net interest margin chooses an asset denominator

**Net interest margin**, NIM, scales NII by **average interest-earning assets**. Constant volumes make our full-year denominator 1000:

$$\mathrm{NIM}=\frac{35.5}{1000}=3.55\%\text{ per year}.$$

The [Federal Reserve's banking report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-june-supervision-and-regulation-report-report-banking-system-conditions.htm) uses NII/average-earning-assets. We make no tax-equivalent adjustment. A quarterly report needs its stated annualisation and averaging conventions; year-end assets alone need not equal average assets when volumes change.

NIM is not generally **average asset yield minus average liability cost**. At the original rates those are $45.5/1000=4.55\%$ and $10/900=1.111111\%$. Their spread is 3.438889 percentage points, not NIM 3.55 %: the denominators differ.

For another check, raise just the loan rate to 6 %. Income becomes $48+4.5+1=53.5$, expense stays 10 and NII 43.5. NIM is 4.35 %, whereas the yield-minus-funding-cost spread is $5.35-1.111111=4.238889$ percentage points.

<BankFundingLab client:visible view="income" loanRatePct={6} title="Compare ratios only after checking their bases" caption={"Initial loan rate 6 % gives NII 43.5 and NIM 4.35 %. The asset yield 5.35 % and average liability cost 1.111111 % instead give spread 4.238889 pp. All rates can be changed; their definitions remain distinct."} />

### When to use it

NIM allows a size-scaled interest comparison, provided periods and earning-asset definitions match. Portfolio mix, risk and other expenses can differ; a higher NIM alone does not prove a safer or more profitable bank. A loan/deposit quote gap also omits quantities and other assets.

## NII is one part of profit

Add annual fee income 4, operating costs 20 and credit-loss provision expense 6 to the base NII 35.5. With no taxes or other entries:

$$
\text{Pretax profit}=35.5+4-20-6=13.5.
$$

A **provision expense** records an allowance for credit losses under the model's accounting assumptions. It is not a principal repayment by the borrower and does not necessarily mean cash left the bank at that instant. Actual recognition rules need their reporting framework and data.

Now raise only the loan rate from 5 % to 5.5 %, holding all volumes and expenses fixed for the year. Loans 800 earn 4 more, so NII becomes 39.5 and pretax profit 17.5. That is conditional arithmetic, not proof a bank can charge more without losing borrowers or changing credit risk.

<BankFundingLab client:visible view="income" loanRatePct={5.5} title="Bridge an interest contribution to the stated profit measure" caption={"Initial loan rate 5.5 % gives NII 39.5 and pretax profit 17.5 after the explicitly listed fees, costs and provision. Move rates to see why NII and profit remain separate figures."} />

**Pitfall.** Reporting NII as total profit omits costs and credit losses. Reporting a principal repayment as income overstates profit too. A real bank's earnings may include trading, valuation, tax and other items not present in this teaching bridge. Retained profit can affect equity; our constant-volume income forecast is not presented as a separately posted closing balance sheet.

### When to use it

Ask which income measure a comparison uses and what is excluded. A flow measured during a year and the stock of equity at year-end are connected through additional entries, including distributions and other comprehensive changes, not by treating them as synonyms.

## Rate repricing depends on both sides

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question={"Can rising rates lower a bank’s annual NII?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "No, because every loan reprices before every deposit",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "No, because rates cannot affect funding expense",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "Yes, if interest expense rises by more than income",
    "correct": true
  }
]}
  explanation={"The reset amounts, pass-through and timing determine the sign."}
/>


A **repricing date** is when a contractual rate can reset; it need not be the final principal maturity. **Deposit beta** here is a stipulated fraction of a market-rate shock passed into the rate on all deposits. It is a scenario parameter, not a universal behavioural constant.

Start at the base income rates. Suppose 25 % of loans reprice immediately, reserves and wholesale debt reprice fully, bonds remain fixed and deposit beta is 50 %. Shock rates **up 2 percentage points** at the start of the whole following year. Loan income changes $800(0.25)(0.02)=4$; reserve income changes $50(0.02)=1$. Expense changes by $800(0.50)(0.02)+100(0.02)=8+2=10$. NII therefore changes $4+1-10=-5$, to 30.5.

The same result comes from the **effective repricing gap**:

$$
G=(800f+50)-(800\beta+100),\qquad
\Delta\mathrm{NII}=G\Delta r.
$$

At $f=0.25,\beta=0.50$, $G=250-500=-250$ euros and $G(0.02)=-5$ euros per year. A 2-point shock is 0.02 in this calculation, not 2.

<BankFundingLab client:visible view="repricing" title="Let the reset assumptions determine the income response" caption={"Initial floating-loan share 25 %, deposit beta 50 % and shock + 2 pp produce gap −250 and annual NII change −5. Change pass-through or the floating share to see the sign depend on both sides."} />

This stripped-down linear model assumes immediate resets for a full year, fixed volumes and no floors, options, basis differences or credit changes. It can allow negative scenario rates. Actual deposit behaviour, nonparallel curves and delayed resets need more detail. [Basel's IRRBB guidance](https://www.bis.org/committees/bcbs/basel-framework/standard/srp/98/inforce/2026-01-01/published/2024-07-16) distinguishes earnings measures from economic-value measures and discusses these modelling choices. A one-year NII change is not a duration or a complete revaluation of equity.

### When to use it

Use a gap to diagnose a specified reset exposure. Do not infer “rate rises help every bank” or “zero one-bucket gap removes all rate risk”. Changing the horizon or behavioural assumptions can change the answer.

## Short funding and long assets create a renewal decision

**Maturity transformation** means financing longer-lived assets with obligations that can become payable sooner. Use a separate date-zero bank: a two-year zero-coupon asset costs 100 and promises 108.16 in year 2; a one-year deposit supplies 90 and equity supplies 10. The deposit contract owes $90(1.02)=91.8$ in year 1. The asset pays **zero** then.

The balance sheet initially adds up. That does not generate 91.8 cash at the intervening deadline. Assume one of two routes explicitly:

For refinancing, $91.8(1.05)=96.39$ and $108.16-96.39=11.77$. At renewal 10 %, debt becomes 100.98 and equity's year 2 residue is 7.18, below its original 10. The new lender's willingness to advance 91.8 is an assumption, not an entitlement.

For sale, the year 2 claim belongs to the buyer. The original owner cannot also receive 108.16 at year 2. The sale's 10.237736 residual is at **year 1**; the refinancing residual 11.77 is at **year 2**. Comparing them as though simultaneous would omit a time-value and reinvestment assumption.

### When to use it

A positive interest spread does not settle a maturity gap. Check due dates, rollover access and liquidation prices separately. Rate changes can also change asset values before maturity; income over one year and the value of remaining cash flows describe different risks. Next we test whether capital and liquid resources absorb specific stresses.
