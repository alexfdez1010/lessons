Two operations can both add 40 reserves and leave different obligations behind. One buys a bond; another lends against it. “More liquidity” is a useful description of the reserve increase, but an incomplete description of the transaction.

Use the opening books from lessons 1–2. Each visual starts independently. Transactions settle immediately at the assets' recorded values. Ignore fees, interest and market gains here. These are invented euro entries, not current ECB policy volumes or universal facility terms.

An **outright purchase** transfers ownership of the asset to the buyer. Suppose the central bank buys 40 of A's bonds. The central bank adds a bond asset 40 and reserve liabilities 40. Its bonds become 140, reserves owed 120 and equity stays 20.

A exchanges bonds 40 for reserves 40:

$$
\text{A assets}=(50+40)+(150-40)+800=1000.
$$

Its deposit liabilities remain 900 and equity 100. Total reserves are now $90+30=120$, but total customer deposits remain $900+450=1350$. The bank has changed asset composition. At our equal-price assumption, the purchase has not given it a profit 40 or a capital contribution 40.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="purchase" seller="bank" title="Trace the outright purchase from the bank" caption={"Initial purchase 40 of A’s own bonds. The central bank gains the bond, A gains reserves and loses the bond. No customer deposit is credited in this particular seller case."} />

**Quantitative easing**, or QE, names asset-purchase policies used to ease financial conditions. The [Bank of Canada's QE explanation](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2025/02/understanding-quantitative-easing/) describes settlement balances created to pay for purchased assets. A purchase's accounting entries alone do not estimate how much yields, spending or inflation will change.

### When to use it

Identify the security sold, its owner and its transaction price. If price differs from carrying value, a gain or loss may change equity. That is an additional valuation fact, not an automatic consequence of receiving reserves.

## A nonbank seller receives a commercial-bank deposit

Reset the books. Household H now sells 40 of its bonds through A. The central bank still adds bonds 40 and reserves 40. But A is H's settlement bank, not the owner of the sold bond. A receives reserves 40 and credits H's deposit 40:

$$
A:\quad 90+150+800=1040=940+100.
$$

A's balance sheet expands, with equal extra asset and liability. H's deposit rises 100→140 and bonds fall 100→60, so net worth stays 200. System customer deposits rise 1350→1390. The [Bank of Canada's paired-balance-sheet analysis](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2022/09/staff-analytical-note-2022-12/) explicitly distinguishes bank and nonbank sellers.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="purchase" seller="household" title="Separate the ultimate seller from the settlement bank" caption={"Initial purchase 40 from H through A. H receives a deposit, not a direct reserve account. This is a new scenario from the opening books, not a second purchase stacked on the previous one."} />

The result is conditional on the counterparty and transaction. H could next use the proceeds to repay a loan, cancelling deposits, or buy another asset. Actual monetary aggregates also have sector and instrument boundaries. The [ECB's discussion of money and inflation](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/key/date/2023/html/ecb.sp230925_1~7ad8ef22e2.en.html) explains those counterparty and subsequent-transaction effects.

### When to use it

Ask who ultimately sold the asset. A reserve credit and a customer deposit credit are separate entries at separate institutions. A household becoming more liquid does not, at equal prices, mean its net worth rose by the entire receipt.

## A secured loan adds a repayment obligation

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question={"A pledges a bond for a central-bank loan 40. Which obligation is added?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "An equity dividend 40 to H",

  {
    "text": "Debt 40 to the central bank",
    "correct": true
  },
  {
    "text": "No debt because collateral is free money",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"The pledged bond remains owned; the reserve advance must be repaid."}
/>


In a **secured loan**, an asset is pledged as collateral for a debt. Under our simplified treatment, the pledged bond stays on A's balance sheet. Suppose the central bank advances 40 to A against eligible collateral. The central bank adds a loan claim 40 and reserves owed 40. A adds reserves 40 and **central-bank debt 40**:

$$
A:\quad90+150+800=1040=(900+40)+100.
$$

Compare the purchase: A's bonds stayed 150 here, not 110. Its equity is still 100, and it owes 40 principal back. Do not count the loan's reserve proceeds as an unencumbered gift.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="central" operation="lendBank" title="Keep pledged ownership and new debt visible" caption={"Initial secured advance 40 to A. Collateral is assumed eligible and sufficient; no interest, fee or haircut amount is priced in this first ledger. The pledged bond is not treated as sold."} />

Actual facilities have eligibility, collateral valuation, **haircuts** (reductions to collateral value used when calculating an advance), maturity and interest terms. The [BoE's operating guide](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/markets/bank-of-england-market-operations-guide/our-objectives) describes collateralised lending facilities; their rules cannot be replaced by a universal right to borrow. Lesson 5 will calculate a declared haircut and compare it with a sale discount.

### When to use it

Distinguish a collateral pledge, an outright sale and an equity investment. They can each bring settlement funds, but leave different asset ownership, debt and loss exposure. Check the repayment schedule as well as today's reserve credit.

## Repayment and sales remove reserves through different entries

The repayment option has a deliberately different starting point: a pre-existing central-bank loan 40. Before repayment, the central bank has bonds 100 plus loan 40 against reserves 120 and equity 20. A has reserves 90 and central-bank debt 40. Repaying 40 reduces both the loan claim and reserves owed 40; A's reserves return 50 and its central-bank debt becomes 0. Customer deposits are unchanged.

An outright **sale** starts from the original, loan-free opening books. Selling a central-bank bond 40 to A adds bonds 40 to A and drains reserves 40: A reserves become 10 and bonds 190. Selling 40 to H instead debits H's deposit 40 and A's reserves 40; total customer deposits fall 1350→1310.

<ReserveBalanceSheetLab client:visible mode="central" operation="repayBank" title="Choose the starting balance and reverse the correct obligation" caption={"Initial repayment 40 of an explicitly pre-existing 40 loan. The menu’s purchase and sale cases instead begin at the original opening books. Read the Before column before comparing the After balances."} />

**Quantitative tightening (QT)** can involve active asset sales or passive runoff as securities mature without replacement. Runoff also involves issuer payments, government accounts and possibly other financing transactions. The [Bank of Canada distinguishes active and passive QT](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2025/02/understanding-quantitative-easing/). Our sale menu is a specified settlement example, not a complete simulation of public cash management.

### When to use it

A reduction in total reserves does not uniquely identify a fall in customer deposits, or prove a fixed contraction in credit. The counterparty and the rest of the balance sheet matter. The [BoE's discussion of QE and QT](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/speech/2023/april/quantitative-easing-quantitative-tightening-speech-silvana-tenreyro) separates transmission channels from a mechanical reserve-to-loan multiplier.
