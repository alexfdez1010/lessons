Deep learning has a highlight reel that would make any technology blush. It crushed
ImageNet, learned to transcribe speech better than tired humans, and now writes
poetry, code, and passable cover letters. So a reasonable person thinks: point this
firehose of expressive power at the stock market and retire by Thursday.

Then the firehose meets the market, and the market — politely, expensively — hands
it back its lunch money.

Deep models inherit the estimation and selection problems from Machine Learning for Alpha. Signal strength, IC and out-of-sample error depend on the target, horizon, costs and evaluation population. Neither a fixed “honest IC range” nor a generic effective sample count can certify a result. More expressive models still need as-of data and a defensible selection procedure.

This lesson is the "why it's hard" foundation for the whole course. Once you respect
the difficulty, the later tools (RNNs, TCNs, attention, embeddings) become *scalpels*
instead of *footguns*.

## Information for a specified mean

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"Does a persistent input feature identify the effective sample size of every prediction error?"}
 options={[{"text": "No; specify the statistic and relevant covariance", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; one lag correlation fixes all learning uncertainty"}, {"text": "Yes; also divide mechanically by every overlap horizon"}, {"text": "No; correlation never changes mean precision"}]}
 explanation={"The sample-mean formula has an estimator and process scope; covariance effects cannot be multiplied without a joint model."}
/>

The number of rows is not a universal count of independent information. Define the statistic and its covariance structure first. For the mean of a stationary scalar series with variance $\gamma_0$ and lag covariances $\gamma_k$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y)=\frac{\gamma_0}{N}\left[1+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{N}\right)\frac{\gamma_k}{\gamma_0}\right].
$$

When its variance is positive, an IID-equivalent count **for this mean** is $N_{\rm eq}=\gamma_0/\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y)$. With an appropriate AR(1)-style correlation sequence $\gamma_k/\gamma_0=\rho^k$, its large-$N$ approximation is $N(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)$. For an invented $N=5000$, $\rho=0.97$ series, it gives $5000(0.03/1.97)\approx76.142$. It does not say that every financial return or prediction-error series has this correlation, or assign a universal neural-network training budget.

Do not divide that result again by a 20-day overlap factor without deriving the joint covariance. Target overlap and feature persistence need not be independent effects, and persistence of a feature is not automatically persistence of estimation errors. Negative correlation can improve a mean’s precision. A changed distribution is a separate question about which population the old observations describe.

Pooling names can also add information without making every row independent. In this invented example, $Y_{di}=Z_d+\varepsilon_{di}$ has independent dates, one common date shock of variance 1, independent name shocks of variance 1, and hence individual variance 2. For 100 dates×20 names, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y)=1/100+1/2000=0.0105$, giving mean-equivalent $N=2/0.0105\approx190.476$, rather than 2000.

<TemporalModelAudit client:visible mode="information" title="More names do not remove a common date shock" caption="Invented sample-mean model: 100 independent dates, one shared unit-variance shock per date and independent unit-variance name shocks. Individual observation variance is 2. The equivalent N matches variance of this mean to 2/N; it is not a generic neural-network sample count. Increase names while the number of independent dates stays fixed." />

The parameter count is a different arithmetic fact. A one-hidden-layer MLP with 30 inputs, 64 hidden units and one output has $30\times64+64+64\times1+1=2049$ weights and biases. Dividing 2049 by the AR(1) mean-equivalent 76.142 gives about 26.91; that ratio alone neither proves failure nor chooses an optimal capacity. The relevant predictive uncertainty depends on the estimator, target, dependence and selection procedure.


### When to use this distinction

Estimate uncertainty for the statistic you will publish using a justified dependence model or resampling method. More windows, names or synthetic samples do not automatically create independent market histories. Check the as-of availability, evaluation design and target distribution before inferring what a larger model can learn. The final lesson works through these calculations and their evaluation consequences.

## Why a plain MLP both over- AND under-fits

**Analogy.** Tuning an MLP on returns is like adjusting a radio whose station is
broadcasting at a whisper inside a thunderstorm. Turn up the gain and you amplify
static (overfit). Turn on aggressive noise-cancelling and you also cancel the whisper
(underfit). There is no knob setting where you hear the song clearly.

**Definition.** Expected prediction error decomposes as

$$ \mathbb{E}[\text{error}] = \underbrace{\text{Bias}^2}_{\text{too rigid}} + \underbrace{\text{Variance}}_{\text{too flexible}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}. $$

In images $\sigma^2$ is tiny relative to signal: there *is* a cat, and the pixels
scream it. In returns, $\sigma^2$ dominates by orders of magnitude — the signal might
explain 1–5% of variance (the OOS R² range you already know), and noise the other
95–99%. When $\sigma^2$ swamps everything, the bias and variance terms you *can*
control are both fighting over scraps, and reducing one inflates the other with almost
no net win.

**Worked example.** Suppose the *true* edge produces an in-sample monthly IC of 0.04.
Watch what three capacity settings do to a held-out (out-of-sample) Sharpe:

Notice the cruelty: the in-sample Sharpe rises smoothly and seductively from 0.35 to
3.10, while the *real* (out-of-sample) number peaks weakly and then goes **negative**.
The "just right" column technically exists, but in a regime that's drifting under your
feet, last quarter's sweet spot is this quarter's overfit. You are aiming at a target
that moves while you pull the trigger.

The [worked estimator-risk example](/deep-learning-for-market-data/training-and-evaluating-dl-without-fooling-yourself#estimator-risk-example) separates squared bias, estimator variance and independent future noise. Its chosen population gives errors 5,3,5 at shrink factors 1,0.5,0; that is a specified illustration, not a universal capacity law.

<Callout variant="warning" title="More capacity is not more edge">
The reflex "my Sharpe is low, let me add layers/width" is exactly backwards. In a
low-SNR world, added capacity almost always moves you *right* on the overfit curve —
better in-sample, worse out-of-sample. The disciplined move is usually *less* model:
fewer features, stronger regularization, simpler functional form. Bigger is the wrong
default reflex.
</Callout>

### When to use it

A plain MLP on raw return prediction is rarely the right first tool — start linear,
prove an edge exists, and only add nonlinearity if a *held-out, purged-CV* test says
the linear model leaves real money on the table. If your in-sample Sharpe rockets while
OOS sags, you've found the overfit cliff, not alpha.

## Universal approximation is a curse here

<MCQ
  client:visible
  pretest
  question="The universal approximation theorem says a big-enough MLP can approximate any continuous function. In low-SNR return data, why is that a liability?"
  options={[
    { text: "Because most of the 'function' it can fit is noise, so unlimited expressiveness mostly buys an exquisite fit to randomness", correct: true },
    { text: "Because the theorem is false for financial data" },
    { text: "Because it makes the model train too slowly to be useful" },
    { text: "Because approximating any function guarantees a profitable strategy" },
  ]}
  explanation="Universal approximation is power to fit whatever is in front of you. When 95–99% of what's in front of you is noise, that power is mostly aimed at noise."
/>

**Analogy.** Universal approximation is a sculptor who can carve *anything* from a block
of marble. Wonderful — if the block contains a statue. In finance, you hand the sculptor
a block that's 97% sand and 3% marble dust, and say "carve the hidden figure." A
less-talented sculptor would shrug and give you a rough lump (and accidentally be right
about how little is there). The genius confidently carves an intricate, beautiful figure
out of the *sand*. That figure is your overfit.

**Definition.** The **universal approximation theorem** states that a feedforward
network with a single hidden layer of sufficient width can approximate any continuous
function on a compact set to arbitrary accuracy. The catch the theorem never mentions:
it makes *no promise about generalization*. It guarantees the model *can* match your
training data — including every noisy wiggle — not that the matched function is the
*true* one.

**Worked example.** Imagine 60 training points where the true relationship is
$y = 0.05\,x + \varepsilon$ with noise $\varepsilon$ ten times larger than the
$0.05\,x$ term. A linear fit recovers a slope near 0.05 — modest, honest, roughly right.
A wide MLP can drive training error to nearly zero by threading a wiggly curve through
all 60 points. Decompose what each "learned":

The MLP's 0.98 training R² is *mostly a portrait of this particular noise sample*.
Next month's noise is a different sample, the wiggles don't line up, and the gorgeous
fit evaporates. Expressive power converted noise into false confidence.

To inspect estimation noise, use the [explicit bias–variance population](/deep-learning-for-market-data/training-and-evaluating-dl-without-fooling-yourself#estimator-risk-example), whose distribution and error arithmetic are supplied. A generic bell-curve drawing would not identify financial tail shape or show which fitted variation is recoverable signal.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Expressiveness ≠ usefulness">
In high-SNR domains (vision, speech) more expressive power usually helps because there's
a rich true function to capture. In low-SNR finance, expressiveness mostly increases the
amount of noise you can fit. Capacity is a liability you must *defend against* with
regularization, simplicity, and brutal out-of-sample testing — not an asset to maximize.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Treat expressive power as something to *spend deliberately*, only where there's
demonstrable structure to capture (nonlinear interactions a linear model provably
misses, on a held-out purged test). Default to the least expressive model that clears
the bar. "Could a simpler model do this?" should precede every architecture decision.

**Answer.** Because the theorem promises *representational capacity*, not *recoverability
from a finite, noisy sample*. In the stated hypothetical prediction setting with limited information and the assumed noise, there are countless wiggly functions that fit the training data
better than the true (nearly flat) one — and the optimizer happily picks one of those.
The true function is *representable*; it is just not *identifiable* from this data. More
capacity widens the set of noise-fitting impostors it can choose from.

## Search changes the reported estimator

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"Two hundred logged runs establish two hundred independent null tests?"}
 options={[{"text": "No; selection must be logged and the joint trial model defended", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; different file names prove statistical independence"}, {"text": "Yes; purging all candidates removes every search effect"}, {"text": "No; selecting a winner never uses information"}]}
 explanation={"A duplicate test and an independent test contribute differently to the null maximum."}
/>

Choosing the best architecture, stopping epoch or seed uses information. A procedure that trains 200 candidates and reports the winner is a different estimator from a single prespecified fit. Log all choices that influence selection, but do not equate the raw run count with independent experiments.

A precise toy calculation makes the distinction visible. Suppose 20 independent calibrated null tests each have a 0.025 chance of passing a one-sided threshold. The probability that at least one passes is $1-0.975^{20}\approx0.397312$. With 200 it is about 0.993677. Copying the same test 200 times would leave its pass probability 0.025. The independent model is stipulated; correlated neural runs need their own dependence analysis.

<TemporalModelAudit client:visible mode="search" title="Selection probability under a stipulated independent null" caption="Invented calibrated independent null candidates. Each has individual one-sided pass probability 0.025; the chart shows 1−0.975^N. The number is a family probability under that model, not the chance a selected model has skill, an expected maximum or a DSR value." />

The **Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR)** is not observed Sharpe minus $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}$. That root expression is a leading asymptotic description of a Gaussian expected maximum, not a significance cutoff. The [primary DSR paper](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/deflated-sharpe.pdf) evaluates a probabilistic-Sharpe statistic against a search-adjusted expected-maximum benchmark, with sample length and higher return moments under its assumptions.

For an independently specified monthly return record with 101 observations, Sharpe 0.10, skewness 0 and kurtosis 3, a stipulated benchmark 0.05 gives PSR about 0.691024 under a normal-reference approximation. It is only PSR until the benchmark is linked to search. In a separate explicit search model with two independent null Normal monthly-Sharpe estimates of mean 0 and standard deviation 0.10, the expected maximum is exactly $0.10/\sqrt\pi\approx0.05641896$. Using that benchmark gives the illustrated DSR about 0.668119. The final lesson derives both calculations and their units.

These scores do not identify causal skill, guarantee future profit or automatically control familywise error. Real candidate dependence, serial return dependence, nonstationarity and estimated moments remain relevant. A raw trial log cannot identify those assumptions.

### When to account for the search

Predeclare an evaluation and tuning budget. Use inner validation for selection and a genuinely untouched later evaluation of the resulting procedure. Purging label overlap does not undo choosing a winner from many validation results. If the final data influence another revision, they have become selection data; evaluate that revision on new information.

After four sections of bad news, here's the turn: deep learning is not useless in
finance — it's *misapplied* when pointed at low-SNR, low-$N_{\text{eff}}$ single-series
return prediction. Aim it where the math is on your side and it earns its keep.

**Analogy.** A Formula 1 car is a disaster in city traffic and unbeatable on a track.
The problem was never the car; it was driving it through stop-and-go. Find the track.

**Worked example — assumed independent breadth.** Under the simplifying fundamental-law approximation $\mathrm{IR}\approx\mathrm{IC}\sqrt B$, assume the same IC 0.03, the same annual horizon and either 50 or 3,000 effective independent bets. The arithmetic gives $0.03\sqrt{50}\approx0.21$ and $0.03\sqrt{3000}\approx1.64$. These are two assumed breadth scenarios, not counts inferred from ticker symbols, a demonstration of extra neural training observations or forecasts of realised IR. Common shocks, trading constraints and implementation costs can change the interpretation. Sharing model parameters may use cross-sectional structure, but does not establish the independent-bet assumption.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Investigate the information, then the architecture">
Pooling, microstructure or alternative data may supply relevant structure or information. Check their dependence, availability and economic objective rather than assuming the number of events or names is independent breadth. Architecture choice then becomes a comparison on the stated task.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Sequence, cross-sectional or unstructured-data tasks can motivate testing a deep architecture. Compare it with relevant simpler models under the same as-of evaluation and tuning accounting. More names or a faster event clock alone do not prove greater effective information for the target statistic.

## Recap

You arrived expecting markets to fall to the same firehose that flattened ImageNet.
Instead you learned *why* the firehose mostly sprays the floor: statistic-specific information and dependence, a signal-to-noise ratio that punishes both flexibility and rigidity, an
approximation theorem that mostly empowers noise-fitting, and a configuration space so
large that architecture search quietly launders overfitting into a victory-lap Sharpe.
And you learned where the F1 car finds its track — microstructure, pooled cross-sections and alternative-data representation, subject to the task and evidence.
