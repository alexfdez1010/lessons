A bond offers a credit spread of 200 basis points. Protection on the borrower is quoted at 150. Subtract them and 50 basis points remain. Before spending that money, ask who financed the bond, which bond the protection settles against, and what happens if you need to leave early. A subtraction has produced a comparison; the trade still needs a cash-flow ledger.

This lesson extends the earlier CDS mechanics and valuation lessons. We will use **basis = CDS spread minus comparable cash-bond spread**, keeping that sign throughout. This is the **cash–CDS basis**, a different comparison from the index-versus-constituents basis in the index lesson. All numerical trades below are invented teaching cases, not current quotes or recommendations.

## Compare like with like before subtracting

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A bond has a comparable cash spread of 200 bp. Its CDS has a par-equivalent quote of 150 bp, a contractual coupon of 100 bp and an upfront payment. Which basis follows this lesson's convention?" options={[
  { text: '−50 bp, using 150 − 200', correct: true },
  { text: '−100 bp, using the contractual coupon minus the cash spread' },
  { text: '+50 bp, because every basis is cash minus CDS' },
  { text: 'The upfront amount in currency minus 200 bp' },
]} explanation="Compare a CDS spread equivalent with the chosen comparable cash spread: 150 − 200 = −50 bp. A fixed running coupon is only one part of a standardized CDS price. Currency and basis points cannot be subtracted." />

One **basis point (bp)** is 0.01 percentage point, or 0.0001 as a decimal annual rate. A **credit spread** is an additional yield or premium rate relative to a specified reference. Here $s_C$ is the CDS **par-equivalent spread**: the running rate that would make the model value of a new running-only CDS zero, with no clean upfront payment. $s_B$ is a specified comparable cash spread.

$$
B=s_C-s_B.
$$

For $s_C=150$ bp and $s_B=200$ bp, $B=150-200=-50$ bp. Protection is cheaper in this spread comparison than the credit compensation on the cash instrument. A CDS quote of 260 bp against the same 200 bp cash spread gives $+60$ bp. Neither sign contains financing or a guarantee of convergence. [Markit's explanation](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/31032016-credit-cds-bond-basis-tightens-as-sentiment-improves) uses this CDS-minus-cash sign convention.

“Comparable” does real work. Match the borrower/legal reference entity, currency, maturity, seniority and relevant contractual terms. State the cash-spread measure and reference curve. A bond's yield minus a government yield, its **asset-swap spread** and its **option-adjusted spread** are different constructions. An asset swap combines a bond with a rate swap to exchange fixed-rate payments for a floating-rate structure; an option-adjusted measure also depends on a model for embedded options. They cannot be silently exchanged as if all measured the same residual risk. A callable bond and non-callable CDS can have different cash-flow horizons.

### A fixed coupon needs an upfront bridge

The **contractual running coupon** $c$ is the rate actually paid during the life of a standardized CDS. The quote $s_C$ need not equal $c$. Define $A$ as the supplied **risky annuity** in years: the discounted, survival-weighted premium accrual amount per unit annual rate and unit notional, including the relevant model conventions. For a consistent valuation:

$$
PV_{
m protection}=Ns_CA,\qquad PV_{
m running}=NcA,\qquad U=N(s_C-c)A.
$$

$N$ is notional. The sign of **clean upfront** $U$ is cash paid by the protection buyer: positive means the buyer pays; negative means the buyer receives. The buyer pays $U$ to obtain a contract whose protection-leg value exceeds its running-premium-leg value by $U$. Premium accrued to settlement is a separate amount.

Take $N=\text{EUR}\,1{,}000{,}000$, $s_C=0.015$, $c=0.01$ and a **supplied** $A=4$ years. Protection PV is $1{,}000{,}000\times0.015\times4=\text{EUR}\,60{,}000$; running-premium PV is $1{,}000{,}000\times0.01\times4=\text{EUR}\,40{,}000$. The buyer pays $U=\text{EUR}\,20{,}000$ clean upfront. If the coupon is instead 5%, the same supplied valuation gives $U=(0.015-0.05)\times1{,}000{,}000\times4=-\text{EUR}\,140{,}000$: a receipt offsets the larger running payments. The par-equivalent quote and −50 bp basis have not changed.

<CdsQuoteBridge client:visible />

The [ISDA CDS Standard Model](https://www.cdsmodel.com/) exists to make upfront/spread conversion consistent. A real conversion requires its date, discount-curve, recovery and other standard inputs. The panel holds a supplied annuity fixed to isolate the algebra; it does not calibrate a credit curve when you move a spread. Upfront divided by five calendar years is not a valid substitute for that valuation.

**Pitfall:** comparing a 100 bp coupon with a 200 bp cash spread and ignoring the upfront makes protection look too cheap. A cash bond also has a purchase price, accrued interest and redemption amount; its coupon minus a reference rate is not generally its market spread when it trades away from par.

### When to use the comparison

Use the basis to name a relative-price discrepancy after stating the instruments and convention. Use full valuation and executable terms to decide whether a trade can capture it.

## Build the funded no-default cash-flow ledger

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="You buy a par floating-rate bond with reference rate 3% plus 200 bp, borrow its full €1 million cost at the same reference plus 30 bp, and pay a running-only par CDS premium of 150 bp. What annual cash carry remains if there is no default?" options={[
  { text: '€2,000 before other expenses', correct: true },
  { text: '€5,000 because funding can be omitted' },
  { text: '€35,000 because the bond is protected' },
  { text: '€50,000 because the coupon is the profit' },
]} explanation="Bond coupon €50,000 less debt interest €33,000 less CDS premium €15,000 leaves €2,000. Principal financing and protection premium are separate obligations." />

For one clean example, buy a **par floating-rate bond** for its €1 million face amount. It pays reference rate $r$ plus credit spread $s_B$. Fund all of the price with debt paying the **same** $r$ plus funding premium $f$. Buy a running-only CDS at par paying $s_C$, with the same €1 million notional, currency and term. Assume aligned resets/accrual, no upfront, no default and no transaction or margin costs.

The common rate cancels in this particular annual cash ledger:

$$
K=N(r+s_B)-N(r+f)-Ns_C=N(s_B-s_C-f)=N(-B-f).
$$

The rates are decimal annual rates in this equation. Initial borrowing $+\text{EUR}\,1{,}000{,}000$ pays for the bond purchase $-\text{EUR}\,1{,}000{,}000$. That zero net initial cash flow is an ideal financing assumption, not evidence that no capital, margin or loss capacity is needed in practice.

| Invented full no-default year | Calculation | Cash to investor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bond coupon | 1,000,000 × (3% + 2%) | +€50,000 |
| Borrowing interest | 1,000,000 × (3% + 0.30%) | −€33,000 |
| CDS premium | 1,000,000 × 1.50% | −€15,000 |
| Cash carry | 50,000 − 33,000 − 15,000 | +€2,000 |
| Surviving maturity principal | Bond +1,000,000; debt −1,000,000 | €0 net principal |

The gross 50 bp cash-minus-CDS difference becomes $50-30=20$ bp after this funding premium: $1{,}000{,}000\times0.002=\text{EUR}\,2{,}000$. Raise $r$ from 3% to 8% with perfectly matched floating resets: coupon becomes €100,000, debt interest €83,000 and CDS premium remains €15,000, still leaving €2,000. Raise **funding premium** to 80 bp instead: $50-80=-30$ bp, or **−€3,000**, despite the unchanged negative quoted basis.

<CashCdsCarry client:visible />

The floating-rate replication is deliberately narrow. [Bomfim's Federal Reserve treatment, sections 5–6](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/credit-default-swaps.htm) explains why funding and fixed-rate instruments complicate the link. “Unfunded CDS” means you do not ordinarily pay the full reference notional to enter; it does not mean zero upfront, zero collateral or no future funding needs.

**Pitfall:** €2,000 divided by zero initial equity is not an infinite return. Cash carry is one flow measure. A return requires a meaningful capital denominator, timing and all relevant gains/losses.

### When this ledger helps

Use it to locate the source of an apparent spread pickup and test a funding break-even. For this exact no-haircut model, funding premium of 50 bp consumes the gross 50 bp difference before other expenses. It does not establish that financing is available or committed for the whole holding period.

## Match the default claim, not just the borrower's name

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Your held bond ultimately produces 35% of face. An enforceable cash-settled CDS pays using an auction final price of 40%. On equal €1 million notionals, how much is left after repaying €1 million of principal, before all other flows?" options={[
  { text: '−€50,000', correct: true },
  { text: '€0 because the company name matches' },
  { text: '+€50,000', },
  { text: '+€600,000 because CDS receipts alone are profit' },
]} explanation="Bond proceeds €350,000 plus CDS €600,000 total €950,000. Repay €1,000,000: a €50,000 shortfall. The auction price and held-bond proceeds are distinct inputs." />

In the ideal equal-notional case, let $R_B$ be proceeds of the **held bond** per unit face, and $R_A$ the CDS settlement price per unit par. If the seller pays fully after a qualifying credit event:

$$
\text{bond proceeds}+\text{CDS payoff}-\text{funded principal}
=NR_B+N(1-R_A)-N=N(R_B-R_A).
$$

If both are 40%, $\text{EUR}\,400{,}000+\text{EUR}\,600{,}000-\text{EUR}\,1{,}000{,}000=\text{EUR}\,0$ of principal remains. If the bond produces 35% but the CDS auction price is 40%, the result is **−€50,000**. Reverse those two recovery inputs and it is +€50,000 before costs. Neither result tells you the lifetime return; coupons, premiums, funding, purchase price and dates still count.

**Deliverable obligations** are debt instruments satisfying the contract's delivery conditions. Physical settlement exchanges eligible debt for par; you cannot assume any security with the same issuer's logo qualifies. Auction cash settlement uses the specified final price, which need not equal the ultimate proceeds of the exact bond you own. Reference entity, seniority, restructuring provisions, maturity limitations, currency and applicable definitions can matter. [ICE's auction description](https://www.ice.com/service/creditex) explains how the auction final price is used for eligible contracts. [DTCC's 2026 restructuring account](https://www.dtcc.com/insights/2026/tiw-credit-events-market-stability-b6p20i4aw) shows why deliverability, elections, notices and deadlines are operational inputs.

### Accrual and settlement take time

Protection premium can accrue from the last premium date to the credit event. A downgrade alone is not a payout trigger. Nor does a credit event imply cash arrives the same afternoon. Keep these dates separate: event, notice/election, auction and settlement.

For an **illustrative** 150 bp running premium, 30 actual days accrued on an actual/360 basis cost $1{,}000{,}000\times0.015\times30/360=\text{EUR}\,1{,}250$. Suppose the original €1 million debt remains fully outstanding for another 30 days until closing, at simple 3.3% on actual/365: incremental funding is $1{,}000{,}000\times0.033\times30/365=\text{EUR}\,2{,}712.33$. This deliberately assumes no early debt repayment from earlier recoveries; another cash-management rule changes the amount.

Starting with the recovery mismatch, the **partial** ledger is $-50{,}000-1{,}250-2{,}712.33=-\text{EUR}\,53{,}962.33$. It excludes all prior carry, earlier premiums, other costs and any separate accrued-interest claim on the bond. A defaulted issuer's unpaid coupon is not automatically a fully paid cash receipt.

<CreditRecoveryBridge client:visible />

**Pitfall:** “same notional” does not establish the same price exposure. A bond purchased for 80% of face needs 80 units of purchase financing per 100 face, but a 100-face CDS claim is still based on notional and its settlement price. Compare the entire payoff and acquisition cost, rather than mechanically assigning CDS notional equal to cash spent.

### When to inspect the documentation

Before treating protection as an offset, map the actual held instrument to the reference terms and settlement obligations. Counterparty failure, disputes, collateral rules and delayed recovery can still leave losses or urgent cash needs; clearing and collateral mitigate particular exposures without making every pair of instruments identical.

## Budget for financing and interest-rate exposure

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A lender introduces a 10% haircut on the €1 million par bond. What changes immediately in the financing plan?" options={[
  { text: 'Debt funding falls to €900,000 and €100,000 of equity must fund the rest', correct: true },
  { text: 'The bond loses exactly 10% in market value' },
  { text: 'The CDS automatically funds the missing €100,000' },
  { text: 'Only €900,000 of bond principal can default' },
]} explanation="A funding haircut limits lending against collateral; it is not the bond's realized loss or a CDS payout. The difference between purchase cost and available borrowing must come from another source." />

A **repo** is financing secured through a sale and agreement to repurchase securities. A **haircut** limits the cash advanced relative to the collateral's value. On a par €1 million bond, a 10% haircut permits €900,000 of borrowing and requires €100,000 from equity under our simple financing plan.

At borrowing rate 3.3%, debt interest becomes $900{,}000\times0.033=\text{EUR}\,29{,}700$. Bond coupon €50,000 less protection €15,000 less that interest gives **€5,300 cash carry**. This is larger than the fully financed case because less debt interest is paid. However, if the investor compares that equity with an explicitly chosen 6% annual opportunity hurdle, $100{,}000\times0.06=\text{EUR}\,6,000$ is the comparison cost. Cash carry minus that amount is **−€700**. The €6,000 is a benchmark opportunity cost, not a second cash payment to the repo lender.

<CashCdsCarry client:visible haircut={0.1} title="A haircut moves part of the financing into equity" />

A haircut can rise when collateral prices fall; borrowing may then shrink for both reasons at once. If collateral falls to €950,000 and the haircut rises to 20%, available borrowing is $950{,}000\times0.8=\text{EUR}\,760{,}000$. Relative to the old €900,000 debt, **€140,000** must be repaid or refinanced, subject to the actual agreement. Separately, derivatives can require initial and variation margin. Margin is collateral, not automatically a realized expense, but obtaining it can impose financing cost and a liquidity constraint.

### A CDS does not automatically hedge the rate curve

A fixed-rate bond also changes value when reference interest rates change. **Rate DV01** here means the currency loss from a one-basis-point parallel reference-rate increase, using a declared sign convention. It is distinct from **CS01**, sensitivity to a one-basis-point credit-spread change. A CDS itself can have interest-rate sensitivity through discounting and expected cash-flow timing; “credit derivative” does not set that sensitivity to exactly zero.

If the whole position has **supplied residual rate DV01 of €400 per bp**, a +50 bp rate shock gives a first-order $-400\times50=-\text{EUR}\,20{,}000$ price effect, even with unchanged credit spreads. A rate hedge reducing residual DV01 to €20 per bp would reduce this particular approximation to $-20\times50=-\text{EUR}\,1{,}000$, while adding its own cash flows, margin and model risks. Duration/convexity and curve shape mean these are local approximations.

**Pitfall:** matching maturity dates alone does not match duration, reset dates, credit exposure, hedge termination or funding commitment. A callable bond, an amortizing loan and a fixed-notional CDS may need a changing hedge rather than a one-time equal-face pairing.

### When to stress liquidity

Test a price drop, higher haircut, higher borrowing spread and derivative margin call together. A strategy can become impossible to keep open before a hoped-for convergence or a qualifying credit event occurs.

## Price the entry and the early exit

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="You hold the bond and bought protection. With equal local €400-per-bp credit sensitivities, the bond spread widens 100 bp and CDS widens 60 bp. With rates unchanged, which price effect follows to first order?" options={[
  { text: '−€16,000 because −40,000 + 24,000 = −16,000', correct: true },
  { text: '+€16,000 because any CDS widening makes the pair profitable' },
  { text: '€0 because both spreads widened' },
  { text: '+€40,000 because the bond now offers more yield' },
]} explanation="A spread widening hurts the held bond and benefits bought protection, but the moves are different. Equal CS01 does not force equal gains and losses." />

**Bid/ask** separates the price available to a seller from the price charged to a buyer. To buy the bond, use its executable offer price and derive the appropriate cash spread from that price. To buy CDS protection, use the executable protection offer with the correct coupon/upfront terms. A midquote basis is not an executable package.

For a purely illustrative quote sheet, cash spread from the midprice is 200 bp and CDS midpoint is 150 bp. The **bond offer price** corresponds to cash spread 190 bp; protection offer is 160 bp. The executable-entry spread comparison is $160-190=-30$ bp. With funding premium 30 bp, the ideal no-haircut running-only carry is **zero** before other costs, rather than the midpoint's 20 bp pickup. A wider yield spread is usually associated with a lower bond price; do not read a dealer's bond price bid/ask as yield bid/ask in the same order.

### Basis changes can overwhelm carry

For small non-default changes, let $C_B$ and $C_C$ be supplied positive credit sensitivities in currency per bp for the cash bond and bought protection. Let $D_r$ be the whole position's residual rate DV01. Then:

$$
\Delta V\approx-C_B\Delta s_B+C_C\Delta s_C-D_r\Delta r.
$$

Spread/rate changes in this formula are **numbers of basis points**, not decimal rates. With $C_B=C_C=C$, the credit part simplifies to $C\Delta B$. If the basis becomes more negative, the cash bond plus bought-protection pair loses value in this approximation. Unequal sensitivities leave another residual even if the basis does not change.

Our pair starts at cash 200 bp, CDS 150 bp, basis −50 bp. Suppose cash moves to 300 and CDS to 210: basis is now −90 bp, a change of −40 bp. With both CS01s €400 per bp:

| Early-exit component | First-order calculation | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bond credit price | −400 × 100 bp | −€40,000 |
| Bought protection | +400 × 60 bp | +€24,000 |
| Net credit-price change | 400 × (−40 bp) | −€16,000 |
| Previously earned net carry | Explicit assumed amount | +€2,000 |
| Separate total exit-cost allowance | Explicit assumed amount | −€2,000 |
| Approximate exit result | −16,000 + 2,000 − 2,000 | **−€16,000** |

<BasisExitApproximation client:visible />

The sensitivities are held fixed only to teach the relationship. The actual annuities, curves, recovery assumptions, accrued premium, convexity and available prices change. If cash CS01 is €400 but bought-protection CS01 €300, equal +100 bp widening gives $-40{,}000+30{,}000=-\text{EUR}\,10{,}000$, although the basis change is zero. “Same notional” did not match spread sensitivity.

**Closeout** means ending or transferring the position under its contractual and execution terms. Selling an offsetting CDS is not automatically the same as legally terminating the original; counterparty, collateral and settlement exposures can remain. Use the actual closeout or executable offset amounts and release of collateral. An upfront receipt on the new trade is not all profit if the old position required upfront cash and running payments.

**Pitfall:** double-counting execution. If you value entry/exit directly at executable bid/ask prices, the spread cost is already in those prices. Deduct only additional expenses that were not included. Conversely, a midpoint sensitivity calculation needs an explicit execution adjustment, as our €2,000 allowance does.

### When an exit valuation matters

Whenever financing, risk limits or the investment horizon could require selling before the bond and CDS finish. A mark-to-market loss can require collateral or force liquidation even if you believe a held-to-maturity outcome is attractive.

## Decide from a dated, conditional comparison

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A colleague proposes the opposite package for a positive basis: short the bond and sell CDS protection. What must be checked before calling the spread difference capturable?" options={[
  { text: 'Bond borrowing/recall, short-sale funding, margin, deliverables, execution and exit terms', correct: true },
  { text: 'Only that the CDS spread exceeds the cash spread' },
  { text: 'Only whether the issuer has a stock-market listing' },
  { text: 'Nothing: reversing both legs removes financing and default risk' },
]} explanation="The opposite exposure introduces its own stock of obligations: securities must be located and borrowed, the short can be recalled, and CDS/collateral terms still matter. A sign convention is not a trading facility." />

Treat a basis proposal like two plumbing systems connected by a valve: matching the label on each pipe is insufficient if their pressures, payment dates and shutoff rules differ. The analogy concerns cash timing and obligations; it does not say a derivative physically transfers the bond's cash flows.

Build a dated ledger with an explicit objective: hedge a held bond's specified credit events, compare cash and synthetic credit exposure, or take a relative-value view. Then check:

1. **The comparison.** Which cash instrument/spread and CDS terms, currency, maturity, seniority, reference curve, quote time and executable side?
2. **The purchase and carry.** Bond dirty price (including accrued interest), CDS upfront and premium accrual, borrowing terms, rate-hedge payments, margin funding, fees and tax. Identify actual cash payments separately from opportunity costs.
3. **Survival to your exit date.** Coupon/reset dates, amortization, calls, loan commitments, funding roll and adverse marks. Reprice both legs and unwind any rate hedge at that date.
4. **Credit-event paths.** Trigger and notice terms, eligible obligations, recovery mismatch, counterparty/default closeout, accrued claims and settlement lags. Do not assume unpaid bond coupons arrive in full.
5. **Liquidity capacity.** Cash required for a higher haircut or margin call, whether financing can be renewed, and what happens if a bond borrow is recalled on a short position.

Apply this to the €1 million example. The ideal full no-default year generated €2,000. A separate hypothetical €1,000 extra expense would reduce that **conditional year's** carry to €1,000. The specified early-exit path produced an approximate **−€16,000**, while the default-mismatch panel showed a **−€53,962.33 partial event ledger** that excludes prior carry. Those are different paths and scopes: adding the early-exit loss to the default result as if both were realized on the same closed position would invent a loss twice. To compute an expected return, you would need mutually exclusive scenarios, probabilities and fully dated cash flows; this lesson supplies no empirical scenario probabilities.

**Pitfall:** positive and negative basis trades are not symmetric operationally. Shorting a cash bond can require a costly, recallable borrow; selling protection takes contingent credit and collateral obligations. A negative basis can persist or widen because funding, liquidity, documentation, balance-sheet constraints and investor demand differ across markets. “Risk-free arbitrage” requires proving all relevant cash-flow and funding conditions, not naming two opposing positions.

### When to choose a simpler exposure

A bond holder may accept a documented imperfect credit hedge, use a different instrument or reduce the cash position. A relative-value investor may require enough expected compensation for residual risks or decline the trade. The decision depends on the actual purpose and constraints. Current documentation, live executable terms and a stressable ledger make the proposal reviewable; the basis alone cannot make that decision.
