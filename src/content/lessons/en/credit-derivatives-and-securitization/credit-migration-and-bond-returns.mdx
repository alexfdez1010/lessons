A bond pays its coupon and avoids default, yet its owner loses money over the year. Nothing has gone missing from the bank account: the bond's sale price fell far enough to outweigh the coupon. To understand that outcome, we need a credit-state model and a cash ledger with dates.

The earlier lessons priced default protection and separated physical default frequencies from pricing intensities. Here we add **migration**, a change in creditworthiness before default. Every probability and yield below is invented for instruction. The letters H and L are our own stronger and weaker states, rather than actual agency grades or historical estimates.

## Read a transition row as a conditional cohort

A **credit rating** is an opinion about creditworthiness, the ability and willingness to meet debt commitments. A rating can change without a missed payment. [S&P's own explanation](https://www.spglobal.com/ratings/en/credit-ratings/about/understanding-credit-ratings?os=0) distinguishes these opinions from investment merits; the rating is one input to an investment decision.

A **state** is a category in a model. Use H for stronger credit, L for weaker credit and D for a defaulted claim whose contractual settlement ends our exposure. A **transition matrix** is a table of conditional probabilities. Read each row from its starting state to all possible ending states over **one year**:

For example, $T_{HL}=0.15$ means a 15% chance of ending in L conditional on starting in H. It says nothing by itself about a bond's market price. Our D row makes default **absorbing**: once the tracked claim has defaulted and settled, it stays recorded as D. A company can restructure and issue new debt; that would be a different tracked claim or a richer state model.

If 100 initially H claims share this model, expected counts after one year are $100\times0.80=80$, $100\times0.15=15$ and $100\times0.05=5$. A realized cohort need not equal those expectations. This calculation does not assume independent issuers; independence would matter for a distribution of portfolio default counts.

**Pitfall:** reading down a column and assuming it must add to 100%. Our convention conditions on the starting state in a **row**; different starting states are different experiments. Some software uses column vectors instead, so state the orientation before multiplying.

To organize rating-migration scenarios and identify which conditional probabilities are required. Real estimation also needs a population, observation dates, rating definitions and a treatment of withdrawn or missing ratings. A “not rated” observation is not automatically a default or a survivor.

## Add all paths before calling a probability cumulative

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="The one-year H-to-default probability is 5%. Does repeating the entire matrix twice necessarily give a two-year cumulative default probability of 10%?" options={[
 {text:'No; surviving claims can move to states with different next-year default probabilities',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; default probabilities always add directly across years'},
 {text:'No; cumulative default must stay exactly 5% forever'},
]} explanation="At the first year-end, some claims are weaker, some stronger and some already defaulted. Each group needs its own second-year transition." />

A **row vector** lists state probabilities in an agreed order. Starting in H means $v_0=(1,0,0)$. After one year, $v_1=v_0T=(0.80,0.15,0.05)$. Multiplying by T again means: multiply the probability of each intermediate state by its next transition, then add the mutually exclusive paths. [The University of Ulm's probability notes](https://www.mathematik.uni-ulm.de/stochastik/lehre/ss06/markov/skript_engl/node8.html) derive this path-product rule.

Write X₂ for the state at year 2 and work through all three destinations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(X_2=H)&=0.80(0.80)+0.15(0.10)+0.05(0)=0.655,\\
\Pr(X_2=L)&=0.80(0.15)+0.15(0.70)+0.05(0)=0.225,\\
\Pr(X_2=D)&=0.80(0.05)+0.15(0.20)+0.05(1)=0.12.
\end{aligned}
$$

The default paths are **H→H→D** with probability 0.04, **H→L→D** with probability 0.03 and **H→D→D** with probability 0.05. The last group defaulted in year 1; its presence in D at year 2 does not create a second default or a second recovery payment. The probabilities $0.655+0.225+0.12=1$ cover all model outcomes.

Now distinguish three questions:

**Pitfall:** confusing a matrix with a promise about future years. The compact notation $v_n=v_0T^n$ assumes the next transition depends only on the modeled current state (**Markov property**) and uses the **same annual matrix** each year. It does not establish those assumptions for real credit data.

## Change the matrix when the transition assumptions change

A rating is a coarse summary. Two issuers with the same rating can have different sectors, leverage, outlooks and migration histories. Even if a current-state Markov approximation is useful, its transition probabilities may change with the environment. Reusing an old average is an assumption to test.

Starting H still gives $v_1=(0.80,0.15,0.05)$. The second-year distribution becomes

$$
v_2=v_0T_1T_2=(0.5675,0.25,0.1825).
$$

Check the default component: $0.80(0.10)+0.15(0.35)+0.05=0.1825$. Of the original cohort, 13.25% defaults for the first time in year 2; conditional on year-1 survival, that is $0.1325/0.95\approx0.139474$. The stress cumulative PD is **18.25%**, compared with 12% under the unchanged matrix. Reversing the order of the matrices describes a different chronology.

A data-based estimate such as “15 downgrades out of 100 initial H names” also has sampling uncertainty. Pooling sectors or decades can hide different conditional risks. Removing all withdrawn ratings can create selection effects; keeping them may require an extra state and an explicit subsequent observation rule. Issuer ratings and individual bond ratings need not measure identical obligations.

**Pitfall:** treating the absence of a downgrade as evidence that market credit risk is unchanged. Prices, spreads and rating opinions can react on different schedules. The model state is an information choice; it is not the whole market.

## Give every surviving and defaulted state a cash ledger

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A bond pays a coupon of 5 at year 1. Immediately afterward its market price is 91.32, following a purchase at 100. With no costs, what determines the one-year result?" options={[
 {text:'Coupon plus the ex-coupon sale price, compared with the initial 100',correct:true},
 {text:'The 5 coupon alone, because there was no default'},
 {text:'The sale price plus every future coupon again'},
]} explanation="A horizon mark already values the cash flows remaining after that date. The coupon paid at the horizon is a separate cash receipt; future coupons inside the mark are not added twice." />

A **holding-period return** compares all receipts and the ending asset value with the initial cash spent. A **mark** is the value assigned to an asset at a date. Selling at that assumed executable price realizes the mark; holding leaves future price and payment risk.

Consider a bond purchased today for **100 currency units**, face value 100, annual coupon **5**, originally three years to maturity. We evaluate it **at year 1 immediately after the coupon**. A survivor has exactly two promised annual payments remaining: 5 at year 2 and 105 at year 3. At that ex-coupon date there is no accrued coupon in this simplified schedule, so clean and dirty prices coincide.

Supply hypothetical market-yield scenarios: H has an annual yield of **4%**, L **10%**. They could be described initially as a 3% reference plus 1% or 7% spread, respectively. These are assumed endpoint quotes; a rating or physical transition probability does not mechanically produce them.

$$
M_H=\frac{5}{1.04}+\frac{105}{1.04^2}=101.8860947,\qquad
M_L=\frac{5}{1.10}+\frac{105}{1.10^2}=91.3223140.
$$

For D, assume the claim settles **at year 1** with **40% recovery of face**, or $100\times0.40=40$, no coupon and no remaining bond. This convention excludes earlier recovery reinvestment, recovery of accrued interest and any delayed restructuring payments.

## Weight complete state returns with physical probabilities

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="The physical probabilities of H, L and D are 80%, 15% and 5%. Is the bond's expected one-year return necessarily its 5% coupon rate?" options={[
 {text:'No; weight the full coupon, mark and recovery outcomes, relative to the purchase price',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; coupon rate and expected holding return are definitions of the same quantity'},
 {text:'Yes; an expected return calculation excludes default outcomes'},
]} explanation="The coupon rate describes a promised coupon relative to face. A holding return includes the ending value and settlement scenarios as well as the initial price." />

**Physical probabilities**, denoted P, describe the model's beliefs about which states will occur. A historical transition estimate is a possible input to those beliefs, subject to its population and forecasting limits. [CFA Institute's credit-analysis curriculum](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/credit-analysis-models) includes calculating expected bond returns from rating migration; the cash/price assumptions must accompany the probabilities.

Because H, L and D are mutually exclusive and exhaustive here, their complete endpoint wealth can be averaged:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^P[W_1]&=0.80(106.8860947)+0.15(96.3223140)+0.05(40)\\
&=85.5088757+14.4483471+2\\
&=101.9572228,\\
E^P[R]&=\frac{101.9572228}{100}-1=0.019572228\ldots.
\end{aligned}
$$

That is **1.957223%** over one year. Weighting the three state returns gives the same answer because all states share the same initial price. A useful reconciliation is

$$E^P[C_1]=0.95(5)=4.75,\quad E^P[M_1]=95.2072228,\quad E^P[K_1]=2.$$

Adding these gives the same expected wealth. Neither the expected wealth nor the expected return is the promised outcome of an individual bond. This example also has a **20% probability of a negative one-year return**, from L or D, even though its mean return is positive.

Change the purchase price or the mutually exclusive outcome probabilities. The stronger-state probability is the residual that makes all three add to 100%; the conditional marks stay at their supplied quote assumptions." />

**Pitfall:** applying “5% default × 60% loss” to a coupon yield and calling the result exact. That shortcut omits migration marks, the purchase price, coupon timing and the specified cash foregone in default. The explicit state ledger shows which flows actually enter.

For scenario analysis, expected returns and horizon-loss probabilities, conditional on stated assumptions. A multi-bond portfolio additionally needs joint migration/default dependence to estimate its tail. Marginal probabilities suffice for a linear expected wealth calculation; they do not identify the probability of many simultaneous defaults.

## Supply a pricing measure before discounting an expectation

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A historical transition matrix describes physical state frequencies. Can its expected bond payoff automatically be discounted at the risk-free rate to obtain the market price?" options={[
 {text:'No; a pricing model needs appropriate state-price weights or risk adjustments as well as discounting',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; every conditional probability system is already a pricing measure'},
 {text:'Yes; a rating fixes the bond’s price independently of its cash flows'},
]} explanation="A probability model for outcomes and a valuation model answer different questions. Physical expectations can be used in valuation only with a justified risk-adjustment/discounting specification." />

A transition matrix does not contain a market price tag. The **pricing measure**, denoted Q, supplies weights appropriate to a stated valuation model. In a model with a deterministic one-period risk-free gross factor $1+r$, a payoff X has price $E^Q[X]/(1+r)$. Q probabilities are pricing inputs or calibrated quantities; they are not automatically forecasts of real-world event frequencies. We used supplied continuation quotes in the previous ledger rather than pretending to derive them from P.

Use a **separate, one-year maturing bond** to isolate the distinction. It pays 105 if it survives, or recovery 40 if it defaults, with no default coupon. The effective one-year risk-free rate is 3%. Supply physical default probability 5% and pricing default weight 10%, both strictly between zero and one.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^P[X]&=0.95(105)+0.05(40)=101.75,\\
E^Q[X]&=0.90(105)+0.10(40)=98.50,\\
P_0^Q&=98.50/1.03=95.63106796.
\end{aligned}
$$

Discounting the physical expected cash at that same risk-free rate instead gives $101.75/1.03=98.78640777$. Those are **different model assumptions**, not two rounding choices. At the supplied Q-based price,

$$E^P[R]=101.75/95.63106796-1\approx6.398477\%,\qquad E^Q[R]=3\%.$$

The physical expected return exceeds 3% in this invented example because the supplied P and Q differ in this direction. Different inputs need not give the same ordering. No single realized payoff equals an average of the two maturity states.

<CreditMeasureLab client:visible title="Keep physical outcomes and pricing weights distinct" caption="Initial one-year example: physical default 5%, pricing default 10%, survival cash 105, default cash 40 and risk-free rate 3%. The model price is 95.631068 and physical expected return 6.398477%. Move physical default with pricing inputs fixed to see the expectation change while the price stays fixed. This is a separate maturing bond, not an extra year added to the migration ledger." />

**Pitfall:** replacing every physical transition entry with “risk neutral” in a spreadsheet heading and treating the result as calibrated. Pricing weights, recovery, cash timing, discount curves and continuation values need a consistent model and market information. A rating opinion or one spread is insufficient to identify every state price.

### When to keep both systems visible

When comparing an investor's expected return with a model price or pricing a credit derivative alongside the underlying bond. State whether default, migration, rates and recovery are modeled under P or Q, which inputs are observed and which are assumed. Historical transition frequencies can inform risk analysis while market quotes inform a separate pricing calibration.
