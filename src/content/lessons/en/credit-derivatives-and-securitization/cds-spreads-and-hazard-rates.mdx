A CDS quote of 200 basis points can help calibrate a default-pricing model. It does not hand you a measured “3.33% chance of default next year.” To reach even a model-implied number, you must specify recovery, payment dates, discounting and the probability measure. A price tag comes with a recipe, not a crystal ball.

The previous lesson introduced a **credit default swap**, or CDS: the protection buyer owes a contractual premium and receives the contractual protection payment if a defined credit event occurs. This lesson follows that contract through its pricing inputs and cash flows. All numbers are **invented teaching examples**, with no counterparty default, transaction cost or funding adjustment unless stated. R below means a recovery fraction, while λ means an annual default intensity; their units matter.

## Read the quote before inferring a probability

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A CDS is described as 200 bp, but its contract pays a fixed 100 bp coupon plus an upfront amount. What does 200 bp tell you by itself?" options={[
 {text:'It can be a par-equivalent pricing quote; it is neither automatically the cash coupon nor a physical default probability',correct:true},
 {text:'The buyer certainly pays 200 bp every year in running coupons'},
 {text:'Exactly 2% of comparable firms will default next year'},
]} explanation="First identify the quotation convention. A fixed-coupon contract can require an upfront transfer, and translating a quote into default intensities requires a pricing model and recovery/discount assumptions." />

A **basis point**, or bp, is 0.01 percentage point: 200 bp = 2% = 0.02. The **contractual coupon** c is the annual rate used to calculate actual periodic premium payments. A **par spread** s is the running rate that makes the premium and protection present values equal under specified dates and pricing assumptions, with the corresponding zero-upfront convention. A **par-equivalent quote** can describe a fixed-coupon contract after a model-based conversion; confirm the provider's convention before using it.

For example, a 100 bp coupon on €10 million with an explicitly stated 0.25-year accrual fraction produces a scheduled €25,000 payment if the contract survives that payment date:

$$10{,}000{,}000\times0.01\times0.25=25{,}000.$$

A 200 bp par-equivalent quote does not turn that contractual €25,000 into €50,000. The difference can be settled through upfront value. [ISDA's converter documentation](https://cdsmodel.com/documentation.html) separates spread, coupon, upfront and cash settlement; those fields answer different questions.

The next distinction is between **physical probabilities**, used in a proposed real-world default-frequency model, and **pricing probabilities**, which reproduce market values under the chosen valuation assumptions. [CFA Institute's credit-analysis material](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/credit-analysis-models) uses risk-neutral expectations with discount rates for the corresponding default-free maturities. The pricing intensity inferred from CDS is therefore conditional on that model; it is not an observed default count.

For a deliberately simple comparison, suppose a flat CDS pricing model implies intensity 2% per year, while a separate physical model assumes 1% per year. The five-year default probabilities are respectively $1-e^{-0.02\times5}\approx9.5163\%$ and $1-e^{-0.01\times5}\approx4.8771\%$. Both calculations are coherent within their stated models. They have different roles; neither assumption is measured by this example. Risk compensation, liquidity, contract features and model choices can affect the relationship between quoted spreads and physical risk.

<CreditTriangle client:visible initialSpreadBp={120} title="Follow a pricing quote through an explicit recovery assumption" caption="Initial example: 120 bp and 40% recovery imply a flat pricing intensity of 2% per year in the continuous-premium triangle. The resulting survival/default curve uses pricing probabilities. A physical-frequency forecast requires its own model; moving this quote does not estimate one." />

**Pitfall:** equating “the market prices default risk” with “the spread is an unbiased default forecast.” Even a perfectly recorded quote needs interpretation. Physical probabilities can enter valuation with appropriate state risk adjustments; using them with a default-free discount factor without such an adjustment is unsupported.

### When to make this distinction

When comparing a CDS quote with historical default studies, a rating model or an investment forecast. Keep quotation, contractual cash payment and modeled probability measure in separate columns.

## Keep intensity, cumulative default and interval default apart

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A constant default intensity is λ = 4% per year. Is λ itself the exact probability of default during the next year?" options={[
 {text:'No; the conditional one-year probability is 1 − exp(−0.04), about 3.9211%',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; every quantity stated per year is a probability'},
 {text:'No; the probability is exp(−0.04), about 96.0789%'},
]} explanation="λ is an instantaneous conditional rate in inverse years. Integrating it over a finite interval gives a probability. The exponential itself is survival, not default." />

Imagine checking a lamp that is still working. A **hazard rate**, or **default intensity**, asks how quickly failure arrives **conditional on survival so far**. It has units of inverse time. Formally, if τ is the default time,

$$
\lambda(t)=\lim_{h\downarrow0}\frac{\Pr(t<\tau\le t+h\mid\tau>t)}{h}.
$$

For a deterministic intensity curve under the chosen measure, survival is $S(t)=\exp[-\int_0^t\lambda(u)\,du]$. With constant λ this becomes

$$S(t)=e^{-\lambda t},\qquad F(t)=\Pr(\tau\le t)=1-S(t).$$

The measure is whichever one generated λ. In our quote-calibrated examples it is the pricing measure. The [MIT lecture notes on credit valuation](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2013/e4c52338bad489162192dd0cc44375ab_MIT18_S096F13_lecnote24.pdf) use this survival/intensity parameterization.

At λ = 0.04 per year:

| Horizon in years | Survival $e^{-0.04t}$ | Cumulative default $1-e^{-0.04t}$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 96.078944% | 3.921056% |
| 3 | 88.692044% | 11.307956% |
| 5 | 81.873075% | 18.126925% |
| 10 | 67.032005% | 32.967995% |

For a payment window, the **unconditional default mass** measured today is $S(a)-S(b)$. Between years 3 and 5 it is $e^{-0.12}-e^{-0.20}\approx0.068189684$, or **6.818968 percentage points**. If the question instead assumes survival to year 3, the **conditional** probability is

$$\Pr(3<\tau\le5\mid\tau>3)=1-\frac{S(5)}{S(3)}=1-e^{-0.08}\approx7.688365\%.$$

Those are different denominators. Dated protection cash flows use the unconditional mass as seen from the valuation date; conditioning again would count survival incorrectly.

<CreditTriangle client:visible initialSpreadBp={240} title="Compare a per-year intensity with finite-horizon probabilities" caption="Initial example: 240 bp and 40% recovery give λ = 4% per year in the flat continuous model. Exact one-year pricing default is about 3.9211%, rather than 4%; five-year pricing default is about 18.1269%, rather than 20%. The readouts are rounded, while calculations retain full precision." />

**Pitfall:** treating λt as a probability at every horizon. It is a short-horizon approximation, can exceed 100%, and overstates $1-e^{-\lambda t}$ for positive λt. An intensity can itself exceed 100% per year without creating a probability above one; the finite-interval exponential transformation matters.

### When each number is useful

Use intensity to parameterize conditional default timing, cumulative default for a horizon, and interval default masses to value dated default cash flows. State whether a probability is conditional on surviving to the window's start.

## State the assumptions behind the credit triangle

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Keep a 200 bp pricing spread fixed. In the flat triangle, recovery falls from 40% to 20%. What happens to inferred intensity?" options={[
 {text:'It falls from about 3.3333% to 2.5% per year: each default pays a larger loss',correct:true},
 {text:'It rises because lower recovery must always mean a higher inferred default rate'},
 {text:'It stays fixed because the spread has not changed'},
]} explanation="At fixed s, λ = s/(1 − R). Lower R raises the loss per default, so fewer pricing defaults are needed to support the same premium. This is different from holding λ fixed and asking how s changes." />

The **recovery assumption** R is the fraction of notional used in the model's default payment. **Loss given default**, or LGD, is $1-R$. An R of 40% gives a modeled protection payment of 60% of notional. An eventual workout recovery, a CDS auction price and the quote-conversion recovery assumption need not be the same number.

Consider an ideal CDS with continuous premium payments until default or maturity T, protection paid immediately at default, flat deterministic pricing intensity λ and recovery R, and the same deterministic discount factor D(t) for both legs. Ignore settlement lags, counterparty risk and other frictions. Define the unit annual premium annuity

$$A=\int_0^T D(t)S(t)\,dt.$$

The protection present value per unit notional is

$$P=(1-R)\int_0^T D(t)\lambda S(t)\,dt=(1-R)\lambda A.$$

At par, $sA=P$, so **within these assumptions**

$$s=\lambda(1-R),\qquad \lambda=\frac{s}{1-R}.$$

This is exact in the declared flat continuous-premium model. It is a **credit-triangle approximation** when applied to a scheduled market CDS without matching all conventions. Time-varying intensity/recovery, payment dates, accrual, discount curves and contract details require the actual legs. A fixed spread does not determine both intensity and recovery.

| Par spread s | Recovery R | LGD | Triangle intensity | Exact one-year default under that flat intensity |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 200 bp = 0.02 | 40% | 0.60 | $0.02/0.60=1/30$ per year | $1-e^{-1/30}\approx3.278390\%$ |
| 200 bp = 0.02 | 20% | 0.80 | $0.02/0.80=0.025$ per year | $1-e^{-0.025}\approx2.469009\%$ |
| 600 bp = 0.06 | 40% | 0.60 | $0.06/0.60=0.10$ per year | $1-e^{-0.10}\approx9.516258\%$ |

<CreditTriangle client:visible title="Test recovery while holding the spread fixed" caption="At a fixed spread, raising assumed recovery raises the inferred intensity; lowering recovery lowers it. At a fixed intensity, lower recovery would instead require a larger spread. These are different conditional experiments. The initial example uses 200 bp and 40% recovery, an assumption rather than a universal market rule." />

[ISDA's published fee-computation settings](https://cdsmodel.com/fee-computations.html) distinguish contract region, type and seniority, with several coupon and recovery conventions. The table includes 40% for some senior corporate contracts, but other entries use different figures. A common convention is a quoting input, not a promise about what creditors ultimately receive.

**Pitfall:** moving recovery and intensity independently while claiming the quote still fits. In calibration, changing one assumption generally requires solving the other inputs again. Nor does a larger protection loss per default prove that the calibrated intensity must rise when the spread is held fixed.

### When the triangle is useful

For a quick model-consistency estimate or sensitivity discussion, with recovery, units and limitations attached. For executable upfront amounts, use a converter with the actual contract's curves and dates.

## Value scheduled premiums and default accrual on matched dates

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A CDS defaults halfway through a premium period. Does saying that future coupons stop at default mean all premium for that partly elapsed period vanishes?" options={[
 {text:'No; the contract can require accrued premium through the default date, which belongs in the premium leg',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; stopping future coupons also erases every accrued obligation'},
 {text:'No; accrued premium is added to the protection payment as a buyer gain'},
]} explanation="Accrued premium is a premium obligation under the applicable contract. It belongs on the buyer-payment side, not as a second protection receipt. The exact accrual dates and payment timing are contractual." />

With scheduled coupon dates $t_i$ and accrual fractions $\alpha_i$, a simplified unit annual premium annuity is

$$A=\sum_i\alpha_iD(t_i)S(t_i)+A_{\text{default accrual}}.$$

The protection leg uses discounted default masses and its own specified settlement timing. **Accrual on default** accounts for premium already earned since the previous accrual date. [ISDA's historical converter specification](https://cdsmodel.com/assets/cds-model/docs/ISDA%20Standard%20CDS%20Contract%20Converter%20Specification%20-%20Sept%204,%202009.pdf) explicitly includes it. Its historical LIBOR/calendar details are not a description of today's curve conventions; use the current documentation for the actual contract.

Here is a fully declared **three-year annual/midpoint teaching approximation**. Notional N = €1 million, pricing λ = 4% per year, recovery 40%, annual continuously compounded discount rate r = 3%, fixed coupon c = 100 bp. Scheduled coupons occur at each year end. Every default during a year is assigned to its midpoint; the premium accrued at default is half a year's coupon. This is not a standard quarterly CDS calendar.

For year 1, survival is $S(1)=e^{-0.04}=0.960789439$, default mass is $1-S(1)=0.039210561$, year-end discount is $e^{-0.03}=0.970445534$, and midpoint discount is $e^{-0.015}=0.985111940$:

$$
A_{\text{scheduled},1}=0.970445534\times0.960789439\approx0.932393820,
$$
$$
A_{\text{accrual},1}=0.5\times0.985111940\times0.039210561\approx0.019313396.
$$

| Year | End survival | Default mass in year | Scheduled annuity weight | Default-accrual weight | Protection PV per unit notional |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 0.960789439 | 0.039210561 | 0.932393820 | 0.019313396 | 0.023176075 |
| 2 | 0.923116346 | 0.037673093 | 0.869358235 | 0.018007691 | 0.021609229 |
| 3 | 0.886920437 | 0.036195910 | 0.810584246 | 0.016790260 | 0.020148312 |
| **Sum** | — | **0.113079563** | **2.612336301** | **0.054111346** | **0.064933616** |

Thus $A=2.612336301+0.054111346=2.666447648$ years and $s=P/A\approx0.024352106$, or **243.521060 bp**. The continuous-premium comparison is 240 bp. The difference comes from the explicitly changed payment approximation, not a discovered trading profit.

<CdsLegsLedger client:visible />

At the contractual 100 bp coupon, premium PV is $1{,}000{,}000\times0.01\times2.666447648\approx26{,}664.48$. Protection PV is about €64,933.62, giving protection-buyer value **€38,269.14 before an offsetting upfront**. This value is not zero merely because the coupon is standardized.

### Keep the annuity's units visible

We use A for the present value of **one unit of annual coupon**, with time units in this notation. The currency value of one bp of coupon at a fixed curve is

$$\text{currency per bp}=N A\times10^{-4}.$$

Here that is $1{,}000{,}000\times2.666447648\times0.0001\approx266.64$ euros per bp. Providers use “risky annuity,” “risky PV01” and “RPV01” with different scaling. Verify whether an input means A, A times 0.0001, or an already notional-scaled currency amount. Multiplying by 10,000 twice is an expensive unit conversion.

**Pitfall:** calling A the maturity, dropping default accrual, or discounting a default payout at a coupon date without stating that approximation. With accrual switched off in this teaching model, A falls to 2.612336301 and the par spread rises to about **248.565300 bp**. That switch changes the assumed contract.

### When to use the full legs

For quote conversion, cash settlement and revaluation, especially short periods, distress or nonstandard dates. Identify the discount curve, recovery, payment schedule, accrued-on-default convention and valuation/settlement dates before comparing results.

## Separate today's CDS value from the trade's profit

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A protection buyer's current contract value is €645,000. They paid €450,000 upfront and €25,000 of running coupons. With no other cash flow, what is the undiscounted net result if they close at that value?" options={[
 {text:'€170,000: current receipt minus entry upfront and coupons',correct:true},
 {text:'€645,000: the current value is always the gain'},
 {text:'€1,120,000: add past payments to current value'},
]} explanation="The current mark values remaining rights and obligations. Entry upfront and already paid coupons are historical cash flows. A profit ledger must include them once, with their signs." />

At a given valuation date and on a **matched model/convention**, let P be protection PV per notional, A the unit annual premium annuity, and $s=P/A$ the corresponding par spread. A protection buyer paying fixed coupon c has value

$$V=N(P-cA)=N(s-c)A.$$

This is an algebraic identity given those matched inputs. Calling it “approximate” usually reflects how s/A were estimated, or an omitted clean/dirty/accrual conversion. A general quoted conventional spread and an unrelated annuity cannot be inserted into the identity without checking their definitions.

At inception, a positive buyer value before upfront means the buyer **pays** an offsetting upfront; if the value is negative, the buyer receives money. Clean upfront and the actual settlement transfer can also differ by accrued premium and settlement discounting. In the historical ISDA converter's stated convention, cash settlement is notional times upfront minus accrued premium. Apply the exact convention, dates and sign used for the actual trade; an entry upfront is not the same object as coupon accrual on a later default.

Use a separate **invented surviving position**, with values supplied on compatible clean conventions and no additional accrued coupon at either marking instant. It has N = €10 million, c = 100 bp, entry par spread 200 bp and entry A = 4.5 years. After a quarter-year coupon payment, current par spread is 250 bp and current A = 4.3 years:

| Dated item for protection buyer | Arithmetic | Signed cash flow |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Entry fair upfront | $10{,}000{,}000(0.02-0.01)4.5$ | −€450,000 |
| Running coupon already paid | $10{,}000{,}000(0.01)(0.25)$ | −€25,000 |
| Current exit value before costs | $10{,}000{,}000(0.025-0.01)4.3$ | +€645,000 |
| **Undiscounted net cash result** | $645{,}000-450{,}000-25{,}000$ | **+€170,000** |

The contract's value changed by €195,000; the paid coupon leaves €170,000. Financing, timing, bid/ask, transaction costs and any other cash flows would change the economic result. The example supplies both annuities; it does not pretend that A remains fixed as credit, remaining maturity or discount rates change.

<CdsMarkLedger client:visible />

A **spread sensitivity** such as CS01 estimates the change for one bp of credit spread under its specified curve bump. Freezing A gives $\Delta V\approx NA\,\Delta s$; with current A = 4.3, N = €10 million, a one-bp change gives approximately €4,300. For a large move, recompute the credit curve and annuity. Even locally, differentiating $N(s-c)A(s)$ gives $N[A+(s-c)A'(s)]$, so the exact spread sensitivity is not always just NA.

**Pitfall:** treating a cheap fixed coupon as evidence that protection was acquired cheaply. The upfront may already have paid for that difference. A current mark, a change in mark and a total financed P&L are three different quantities.

### When to build the historical ledger

Whenever a statement says “the CDS gained X.” Ask for entry value/upfront, running cash, current closeout conventions, accrued premium and costs. Compare receipts and obligations over the same holding horizon.

## Bootstrap a curve and check what its slope actually says

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="One-year, three-year and five-year par spreads rise with maturity. Does that prove every successive forward default-intensity bucket is higher than the last?" options={[
 {text:'No; each maturity prices a weighted collection of earlier and later cash flows, so solve and reprice the buckets',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; a maturity average and the last forward bucket are always identical'},
 {text:'Yes; a rising pricing curve is a measured forecast of worsening physical default'},
]} explanation="A par spread is not the forward intensity at its last date. Discounting, survival, premiums and recovery determine its weights. A forward can decline while the longer maturity average still rises." />

A **forward intensity** describes conditional default timing within a later interval given survival to it. A **piecewise constant hazard curve** uses one such intensity in each maturity bucket. **Bootstrapping** means solving the earliest bucket from its quote, then holding it fixed while solving the next bucket so the longer contract reprices, and continuing.

For intervals of length $\Delta_i$ with intensity $\lambda_i$, survival multiplies:

$$S(T_k)=\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i\Delta_i\right).$$

With deterministic continuous discount rate r and continuous premiums/default settlement, the incremental annuity in a bucket beginning at a is

$$A_i=D(a)S(a)\frac{1-e^{-(r+\lambda_i)\Delta_i}}{r+\lambda_i},$$

with the continuous limit $D(a)S(a)\Delta_i$ when the denominator is zero. Add $A_i$ to A and $(1-R)\lambda_i A_i$ to P; the maturity par spread is $P/A$. Actual scheduled CDS calibration uses its actual leg formulas instead.

Here is a reproducible **counterexample to a universal slope rule**: r = 3% continuously, recovery 40%, bucket lengths 1/2/2 years, pricing intensities **1%/5%/4%** per year:

| End date | New bucket intensity | Survival to end | Cumulative A | Matched maturity par spread |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Year 1 | 1% | $e^{-0.01}=0.990049834$ | 0.980264021 | 60.000000 bp |
| Year 3 | 5% | $e^{-(0.01+0.05\times2)}=0.895834135$ | 2.755997597 | 214.635860 bp |
| Year 5 | 4% | $e^{-(0.01+0.05\times2+0.04\times2)}=0.826959134$ | 4.284003744 | 223.682668 bp |

For the second bucket, $A_2=e^{-0.03}e^{-0.01}(1-e^{-0.08\times2})/0.08\approx1.775733576$. Cumulative protection PV is $0.6[0.01(0.980264021)+0.05(1.775733576)]\approx0.059153591$, giving $s_3=0.059153591/2.755997597\approx214.635860$ bp. Adding the third bucket raises the maturity quote, although its **4% forward intensity is lower than the preceding 5%**.

<HazardCurveLedger client:visible />

In reverse, start with the generated quotes 60/214.635860/223.682668 bp. The one-year quote identifies λ₁ = 1% in this continuous model. Hold that fixed and solve the three-year leg equality for λ₂ ≈ 5%; then solve the five-year equality for λ₃ ≈ 4%. Use full-precision quotes for an exact numerical round-trip; displayed rounding produces tiny calibration differences. Solving means finding a nonnegative intensity whose calculated contract value matches the quote, not dividing each long quote by LGD and treating the result as its last forward.

**Pitfall:** silently accepting a negative forward hazard to force inconsistent data into this model. Some quote/recovery/convention sets have no nonnegative calibration in the chosen family. Check feasibility and reprice every input quote. A fitted pricing curve is still conditional on recovery, discounting and model choice, rather than a unique physical forecast.
