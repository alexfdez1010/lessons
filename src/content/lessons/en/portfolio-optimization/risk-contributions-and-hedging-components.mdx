A risk report assigns one position 110% of the portfolio’s volatility and another −10%. The totals reconcile. The report may be correct: one position adds risk while the other partly offsets it. A pie chart would hide the useful part.

The [previous lesson](/portfolio-optimization/risk-parity-and-constraints) introduced equal risk contribution. Here we examine the accounting behind that rule and the trade decisions it can support. **Risk means standard deviation of portfolio returns**, over one specified horizon. The examples use invented one-year covariance inputs, fixed while weights change. They do not forecast losses or expected returns.

Think of a group carrying a flexible platform. Where a person pushes matters as well as how hard they push. In a portfolio, the corresponding relationship is **covariance**: how two returns vary together around their means.

Let $w$ be exposures as fractions of a fixed reference capital, and $\Sigma$ a symmetric, positive semidefinite covariance matrix for the same return horizon. Positive semidefinite means every portfolio variance is nonnegative. Write

$$V=w^\top\Sigma w,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{V}>0$$

The vector $q=\Sigma w$ contains each asset’s covariance with the portfolio. The **marginal contribution to volatility** is the partial derivative, holding every other exposure fixed:

$$m_i=\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial w_i}=\frac{q_i}{\sigma}$$

A small additional exposure $\varepsilon$ to asset $i$ changes volatility by approximately $\varepsilon m_i$. This is a derivative per unit exposure, not the amount of volatility already assigned to the position. The **Euler component contribution** is

$$\mathrm{RC}_i=w_im_i=\frac{w_iq_i}{\sigma}$$

Multiply and add to check the allocation:

$$\sum_i\mathrm{RC}_i=\frac{\sum_iw_iq_i}{\sigma}=\frac{w^\top\Sigma w}{\sigma}=\sigma$$

The equality uses the degree-one homogeneity of volatility: scaling all exposures by a positive factor scales volatility by that factor. It does not require independent assets. [MOSEK derives the Euler allocation and marginal contributions](https://docs.mosek.com/portfolio-cookbook/risk_parity.html); [Tasche develops the differentiability and homogeneity conditions](https://arxiv.org/abs/0708.2542).

### Work through the matrix

Set weights to 80% in A and 20% in B, standalone volatilities to 20% and 10%, and correlation to −0.8. Covariance is correlation times the two volatilities:

$$\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.20^2&-0.8(0.20)(0.10)\\-0.8(0.20)(0.10)&0.10^2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.04&-0.016\\-0.016&0.01\end{pmatrix}$$

$$q_A=0.04(0.8)-0.016(0.2)=0.0288$$

$$q_B=-0.016(0.8)+0.01(0.2)=-0.0108$$

$$V=0.8(0.0288)+0.2(-0.0108)=0.02088,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{0.02088}\approx0.14449913$$

Portfolio volatility is **14.4499%**. Divide each covariance by this unrounded volatility, then multiply by the position’s weight:

| Asset | Weight | Marginal derivative | Component in return units | Component in volatility percentage points |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 0.8 | 0.19930915 | 0.15944732 | 15.944732 |
| B | 0.2 | −0.07474093 | −0.01494819 | −1.494819 |
| Total | 1.0 | Not added | 0.14449913 | 14.449913 |

### Variance and volatility use different units

The **variance components** $\mathrm{VC}_i=w_iq_i$ sum to $V$, not to $\sigma$. Here they are 0.02304 and −0.00216, summing to 0.02088. Dividing each by $\sigma$ produces the volatility components above. If you start with the variance gradient $\partial V/\partial w_i=2q_i$, remember the factor of one-half: summing $w_i\partial V/\partial w_i$ gives $2V$.

### When to use it

Use marginal derivatives to compare small exposure changes; use components to reconcile the existing portfolio’s volatility. A derivative of 0.199309 means that adding 0.01 of A, holding B fixed, changes volatility by about 0.00199309, or **0.199309 percentage points**. It does not mean A contributes 19.9309 percentage points at its current weight. Implementing an isolated added exposure needs cash or borrowing; any funding risk must be specified separately.

## A hedging component can carry a negative contribution

With positive portfolio volatility, the **risk share** is

$$s_i=\frac{\mathrm{RC}_i}{\sigma}=\frac{w_iq_i}{V},\qquad\sum_i s_i=1$$

For the same example,

$$s_A=\frac{0.02304}{0.02088}\approx1.10344828,\qquad s_B=\frac{-0.00216}{0.02088}\approx-0.10344828$$

A carries **110.3448%** and B **−10.3448%** of the modelled volatility. Those signed shares still sum to 100%. B is a positive holding with positive standalone volatility; its negative covariance with the portfolio makes its marginal derivative negative here. Increasing B a little **with A held fixed** reduces this volatility measure locally.

This is not a promise that B rises whenever A falls, nor that B has a positive expected return. Correlation describes a joint distribution or estimate, not a scenario-by-scenario guarantee. A component is also not the exact risk removed by selling the whole position: selling all of B leaves 80% of capital in A and 20% in zero-covariance cash under that explicit funding assumption, with volatility $0.8(0.20)=16\%$. The difference from the current 14.4499% is **1.5501 percentage points**, not the 1.4948-point Euler component. A finite removal changes the derivatives along the path.

### When to use it

Read negative components as a prompt to examine diversification and covariance assumptions. Use signed bars, as above, rather than forcing every contribution into a positive pie slice. Removing a hedge simply because its share looks “too small” can increase total modelled volatility.

A partial derivative answers “what if I add a little B while keeping A fixed?” A fully invested trade answers “what if I sell A to buy B?” The cash drawer must reconcile before the risk calculation does.

Move a positive weight $\delta$ from A into B, with no costs or outside cash:

$$\Delta w=(-\delta,+\delta),\qquad \Delta\sigma\approx\nabla\sigma^\top\Delta w=\delta(m_B-m_A)$$

At the original 80/20 weights, move five percentage points: $\delta=0.05$, giving 75/25. The local estimate is

$$\Delta\sigma\approx0.05(-0.07474093-0.19930915)=-0.01370250$$

$$\sigma_{\text{linear}}\approx0.14449913-0.01370250=0.13079663$$

Now calculate the exact value from the new weights, keeping the covariance matrix fixed:

$$V_{\text{new}}=0.75^2(0.04)+0.25^2(0.01)+2(0.75)(0.25)(-0.016)=0.017125$$

$$\sigma_{\text{new}}=\sqrt{0.017125}\approx0.13086252$$

Exact volatility is **13.0863%**; the linear estimate is **13.0797%**. Using only $0.05m_B$ would estimate 14.0762%, because it omits the sale of A. The exact funded reduction is about **1.3637 percentage points**.

### Negative contribution is sufficient in some trades, not necessary

At different weights, 35/65, both marginal derivatives are positive:

$$\Sigma w=(0.0036,0.0009),\quad\sigma=\sqrt{0.001845}\approx0.04295346$$

$$m_A\approx0.08381164,\qquad m_B\approx0.02095291$$

A one-percentage-point shift from A to B has local change $0.01(0.02095291-0.08381164)\approx-0.00062859$. Exact volatility at 34/66 is about **4.2417%**, below the previous **4.2953%**. B’s component is positive, yet it is a lower-volatility destination than A at this margin.

### When to use it

Rank the **directional derivatives of feasible trades**, including the funding leg and constraints. Then reprice the proposed finite trade, consider costs and expected returns, and test alternative covariance estimates. The tangent can become negative for a large shift; actual standard deviation cannot. A local hedge effect can disappear as the weights move.

## Zero volatility makes the normalized allocation singular

The division by $\sigma$ was a condition, not decoration. A positive semidefinite covariance matrix may be singular: some nonzero exposure vector can have zero variance in the model.

Keep volatilities at 20% and 10%, set correlation to **−1**, and choose weights $1/3$ and $2/3$. The opposing one-factor exposures cancel:

$$\sigma=\left|0.20\frac13-0.10\frac23\right|=0$$

The marginal formula becomes $0/0$. There is no unique volatility gradient at this kink, and the normalized shares do not exist. Reporting every volatility component as zero would conceal the undefined derivative.

After moving $\delta$ from A to B, exact volatility is

$$\sigma(\delta)=\left|0.20\left(\frac13-\delta\right)-0.10\left(\frac23+\delta\right)\right|=0.30|\delta|$$

A shift of either plus or minus 0.01 produces **0.3%** volatility: the left and right derivatives differ. Variance itself remains differentiable, with zero variance components at the exact hedge, but that does not rescue the volatility allocation.

### When to use it

Flag zero or numerically near-zero volatility explicitly and inspect the unnormalized exposures and covariance matrix. Near a hedge, normalized shares can become unstable and large. It is not certifying a real investment as riskless. Estimation error, nonlinearity, funding and basis risk can break a model hedge.

## Stress the inputs before acting on a contribution

A risk component describes **this portfolio, this covariance matrix, this risk measure and this horizon**. Reusing the 80/20 weights but changing correlation from −0.8 to zero gives

$$V=0.8^2(0.20^2)+0.2^2(0.10^2)=0.026,\qquad\sigma\approx16.1245\%$$

Now $q_B=0.002$ is positive and B’s volatility component is about **0.2481 percentage points**. The holding did not change; its measured relationship with A did. At correlation +0.8, total volatility is about **17.6409%**. These are sensitivity cases, not assigned probabilities for tomorrow.

### When to use it

Use Euler contributions for a reconciled volatility report and for diagnosing feasible small trades. For a trading decision, add return expectations, transaction and funding costs, constraints, and sensitivity to input estimates. A volatility contribution does not allocate expected shortfall, default loss or liquidity risk. Each of those requires its own model and allocation conditions.

## Rebuild the reasoning

Start from a common-horizon covariance matrix. Calculate portfolio variance and positive volatility, then covariances with the portfolio, marginal derivatives and weighted components. Check the sums and units. A trade needs a direction and a funding source; a finite trade needs a new calculation. At zero volatility, stop dividing and explain the singularity.
