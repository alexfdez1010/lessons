A pricing team asks for an answer within one dollar. A quantum vendor answers with “one thousand oracle queries”. There is a missing conversion: one thousand calls to what, estimating which probability, at what confidence, and with what dollar error?

**Quantum amplitude estimation (QAE)** estimates a probability encoded in a quantum state. Under a specified coherent-oracle model, it can reduce the number of oracle uses needed for additive estimation accuracy relative to ordinary independent sampling. That is a mathematical result about a defined resource. The financial task also needs a distribution, a payoff encoding, an error budget and a complete implementation.

We will count those objects separately. The panels are deterministic calculations for invented models, not quantum-hardware simulations or measurements of commercial performance. The earlier lesson supplies qubits, gates and measurement; the Monte Carlo prerequisite supplies expectations and sampling.

## 1. Standard error is a spread, not a promise

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"Before the calculations: does one standard error bound every realised sample-mean error?"}
options={[{"text": "No; it describes the distribution of estimates under the sampling model", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; no estimate can miss by more than its standard error"}, {"text": "Yes; the central limit theorem guarantees each finite result"}, {"text": "No; the variance of an IID mean never depends on N"}]}
explanation={"A sampling standard deviation is distinct from an absolute-error event with stated probability."}
/>

Imagine weighing the same shipment with different noisy scales. The spread of repeated answers is useful; it does not mean every answer misses by exactly that spread.

For independent, identically distributed (IID) observations $Y_j$ with finite variance $\sigma^2$, the sample mean $\bar Y=N^{-1}\sum_jY_j$ has

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y)=\frac{\sigma^2}{N},\qquad \operatorname{SE}(\bar Y)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N}.
$$

This variance identity does not require a normal approximation. A central limit theorem can justify an approximate normal confidence interval under its assumptions. Neither statement bounds the realised error with certainty.

Take an invented Bernoulli variable: it pays 1 with probability 0.3 and 0 otherwise. Its mean is 0.3 and variance is $0.3(0.7)=0.21$. With $N=10{,}000$,

$$
\operatorname{SE}=\sqrt{0.21/10000}\approx0.004582576.
$$

To halve this standard error, quadruple N to 40,000. This comparison fixes the distribution and the estimator. It does not say every classical integration algorithm must have this rate: closed forms, quadrature, [quasi-Monte Carlo](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/tutorial/stats/quasi_monte_carlo.html) and structural variance reduction need their own assumptions and resource comparisons.

For a different question—an **absolute-error guarantee with specified confidence**—bounded independent observations in $[0,1]$ satisfy [Hoeffding’s bound](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830):

$$
\Pr(|\bar Y-\mu|>\varepsilon)\le2e^{-2N\varepsilon^2},\qquad
N\ge\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^2}.
$$

Here $\delta$ is a failure-probability target, not a dollar tolerance. At $N=10{,}000$ and $\delta=0.05$, the conservative radius is $\sqrt{\ln40/20000}\approx0.013581015$. It differs from the standard error because it answers a different question without using the supplied Bernoulli variance.

<QaeEstimationBudget client:visible mode="sampling" title="Separate standard error and a confidence radius" />

### When to compare estimation methods

Write the estimator, distribution assumptions, error units and success probability first. “Error 0.001” could mean a standard error, a high-probability absolute bound or relative error. Those are different contracts. Dependence and infinite variance can invalidate the simple IID calculation.

## 2. Turn a signed payoff into a marked probability

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"A marked probability is estimated to additive error 0.01. Does this alone specify a dollar error?"}
options={[{"text": "No; the payoff range and discount conversion are also needed", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; the price error is exactly 0.01 USD in all models"}, {"text": "Yes; this always means 1% of the option price"}, {"text": "No; a probability can never encode a signed payoff"}]}
explanation={"Affine scaling can encode signed bounded payoffs, but the inverse conversion determines the money units."}
/>

A probability cannot be −20 or 80 dollars. The encoding therefore needs a scale, just as a map needs a legend.

Suppose the chosen model’s payoff $F$ is bounded by known numbers $L<U$. Encode

$$
g(F)=\frac{F-L}{U-L}\in[0,1],\quad a=\mathbb E[g(F)],\quad
V=d\{L+(U-L)a\},
$$

where $d>0$ is a specified discount factor. If the joint state is prepared correctly, the probability of a marked ancilla measurement is $a$. Its associated amplitude is $\sqrt a$; **probability and amplitude are not interchangeable**. The distribution and the controlled payoff operation both belong to the state-preparation circuit $\mathcal A$.

Use an invented pricing model with probabilities 70% and 30% on payoffs −20 and +80 USD. These are stipulated **pricing probabilities**, conventionally denoted $\mathbb Q$; they are not a forecast inferred by the algorithm. Then $g$ takes 0 or 1,

$$
a=0.7(0)+0.3(1)=0.3,\quad \mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[F]=-20+100(0.3)=10.
$$

At $d=0.95$, value is $V=0.95(10)=9.50$ USD. An amplitude-estimation probability error $|\hat a-a|\le\varepsilon_a$ converts to

$$
|\hat V-V|\le d(U-L)\varepsilon_a.
$$

At $\varepsilon_a=0.01$, the bound is $0.95(100)(0.01)=0.95$ USD, or **10% of the 9.50 USD value**, not 1% relative error. A one-dollar tolerance permits $\varepsilon_a=1/95\approx0.010526316$ in this particular encoding.

<QaeEstimationBudget client:visible mode="encoding" title="Convert a marked probability into a monetary value" />

Changing an upper bound changes the conversion. An unbounded European-call payoff $\max(S_T-K,0)$ has no finite universal U. A finite register or truncation needs an explicit tail treatment and a bound on the resulting bias; more amplitude queries cannot remove omitted tail value. A risk-neutral pricing law and a physical loss-forecast law, often denoted $\mathbb P$, answer different questions. QAE estimates the supplied law; it does not make one law correct for every task.

### When to inspect the encoding

Check signed payoffs, range, discounting, probability measure, discretisation and payoff approximation before translating precision into a resource request. The [primary option-pricing paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.02666) describes construction of distribution and payoff circuits; those operations are part of the implementation, rather than free input data.

## 3. State the canonical QAE theorem and its counted oracle

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"A canonical QAE theorem says success probability at least 8/π². Is a 95% guarantee already established?"}
options={[{"text": "No; the stated lower bound is about 81%, and confidence needs additional analysis", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; all quantum algorithms succeed with probability one"}, {"text": "Yes; increasing the register makes the written confidence automatically 95%"}, {"text": "No; canonical QAE cannot estimate probabilities at all"}]}
explanation={"The error bound and its success probability are separate parts of the theorem."}
/>

An **oracle** is the specified callable operation whose uses an algorithmic analysis counts. A cheap oracle in a theorem is a modelling assumption, rather than a claim about a physical device.

Canonical phase-estimation QAE uses a phase register of $m$ qubits and resolution parameter $M=2^m$. For an exactly implemented coherent state-preparation/amplification construction, the [Brassard–Høyer–Mosca–Tapp theorem](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0005055) gives an estimate obeying

$$
|\hat a-a|\le\frac{2\pi\sqrt{a(1-a)}}{M}+\frac{\pi^2}{M^2}
$$

with probability at least $8/\pi^2\approx0.810569469$. This is an additive **probability-error** bound on the encoded model. It is neither an exact error on every run nor 95% confidence by default.

At stipulated $a=0.3$ and $m=10$, $M=1024$:

$$
\frac{2\pi\sqrt{0.21}}{1024}\approx0.002811833,
\qquad\frac{\pi^2}{1024^2}\approx0.000009412,
$$

so the total bound is approximately **0.002821246**. The leading term is order $1/M$. Ordinary IID sampling reaches a fixed standard-error or fixed-confidence target with order $1/\varepsilon^2$ samples; ideal QAE has order $1/\varepsilon$ query dependence at fixed nontrivial confidence. Constants and error contracts still matter. Substituting $1/\varepsilon$ as an exact measured query count discards them.

<QaeEstimationBudget client:visible mode="canonical" title="Resolve the finite-M canonical bound" />

If $a$ is unknown, you cannot size the algorithm using its favourable unknown value. Use $\sqrt{a(1-a)}\le1/2$ for a worst-case bound, or state why a tighter bound is available.

The original theorem counts its defined function evaluations. Our explicit **circuit accounting convention** instead expands the controlled powers into $M-1$ elementary amplification operations $Q$, plus one initial $\mathcal A$. With one $\mathcal A$ and one $\mathcal A^\dagger$ inside each Q, that is $2M-1$ preparation/inverse-preparation blocks. At M=1024: **1,023 Q uses and 2,047 such blocks per run**. Reflections, controlled implementations, phase readout and other gates are additional. These counts must not be silently labelled equal to each other.

### When to use query complexity

It isolates a possible algorithmic advantage once the oracle contract is specified. For an implementation comparison, include the gate/depth cost of applying $\mathcal A$ and its inverse, the cost of constructing or compiling them, and the longest coherent circuit. A short classical sample and a coherent controlled oracle call need not cost the same.

## 4. Confidence and coherent depth are separate budgets

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"Seven independent repetitions improve confidence. What do they necessarily leave unchanged in this example?"}
options={[{"text": "The longest coherent power inside each M=1024 canonical circuit", "correct": true}, {"text": "The total number of Q applications over all seven circuits"}, {"text": "The binomial failure bound compared with one run"}, {"text": "The number of initial preparations across the runs"}]}
explanation={"Independent restarts multiply work; they do not shorten the Q^512 power inside each circuit."}
/>

Repeating a journey improves the chance that a vote among results is reliable; it does not shorten the longest road on each journey.

Suppose independent canonical runs each satisfy the same error bound with success at least $p_0=8/\pi^2$. For an odd number r of runs, the median lies inside that error interval whenever a majority of estimates do. A conservative failure bound is

$$
\Pr(\text{median fails})\le\sum_{k=(r+1)/2}^{r}\binom rk(1-p_0)^kp_0^{r-k}.
$$

With five runs this upper bound is **0.050123798**. That bound alone does not certify a 5% failure target: rounding it to 5% would cross the requested threshold. With seven runs the bound falls to **0.027637943**, so the success lower bound is approximately **97.236206%**, which meets an at-least-95% target conservatively.

At M=1024, seven independent runs use $7(1023)=7161$ Q operations and $7(2047)=14329$ A/inverse-A blocks under our convention. The longest controlled power in each canonical circuit remains $Q^{512}$. Counting only seven final measurements would hide most of the work.

<QaeEstimationBudget client:visible mode="confidence" title="Buy confidence with independent repetitions" />

**Iterative QAE (IQAE)** avoids the quantum phase-estimation register and uses amplification powers with repeated measurement and classical interval updates. The [primary IQAE paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05559) proves near-quadratic improvement with stated confidence and logarithmic factors for its procedure. “No phase estimation” does not mean constant depth, zero sampling cost, or arbitrary precision on noisy hardware. Other variants need their own error/confidence analysis. Statistical confidence in an ideal circuit does not bound systematic gate or encoding bias.

### When to choose a variant

Compare total amplification calls, maximum coherent depth, state qubits, measurement repetitions, confidence guarantees and the available noise/fault-tolerance assumptions. A shallower implementation at one tolerance is evidence about that implementation, rather than proof of a universal near-term advantage.

## 5. Risk needs a CDF, a quantile convention and the right tail mass

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"A loss has an atom at its 95% quantile. Must ES equal the average of all losses at or above that quantile?"}
options={[{"text": "No; exactly the worst 5% mass may require only part of the boundary atom", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; every atom at the threshold must be included in full"}, {"text": "Yes; ES is always numerically identical to VaR"}, {"text": "No; ES cannot be defined for discrete loss distributions"}]}
explanation={"A quantile-integral or weighted-tail definition handles boundary mass explicitly."}
/>

A **cumulative distribution function (CDF)** gives $F_L(q)=\Pr(L\le q)$. QAE can estimate such a probability by marking losses below a proposed threshold. **Value at Risk** is an inverse-CDF quantile,

$$
q_c=\inf\{q:F_L(q)\ge c\}.
$$

Finding it generally requires a threshold search and a rule for ambiguous noisy comparisons. An absolute CDF error of 0.001 is not a loss error of 0.001 USD. Converting one into the other needs distributional information around the quantile; gaps, atoms or low density can make the conversion unstable. Search calls and their joint confidence also count.

Use a complete invented physical loss model:

At c=95%, q=50. **Expected Shortfall (ES)** averages exactly the worst 5% probability mass, using only the required part of the boundary atom: 2% at 100 and 3% at 50. Therefore

$$
\operatorname{ES}_{.95}=\frac{.02(100)+.03(50)}{.05}=70\text{ USD}.
$$

The strict-tail conditional mean $\mathbb E[L\mid L>50]=100$ is a different number. Including the whole atom with $L\ge50$ gives 60, also different. For this bounded distribution and its exact inverse-CDF quantile, a useful expectation identity is

$$
\operatorname{ES}_c=q_c+\frac{\mathbb E[(L-q_c)_+]}{1-c}.
$$

Here expected excess is $.02(100-50)=1$ USD, so $50+1/.05=70$. Encode the excess divided by its bound 50: the marked probability is $.02$. An additive estimate error .001 then contributes at most $50(.001)/.05=1$ USD of ES error **with this exact q held fixed**. Quantile estimation and other approximation errors require additional analysis. At the same .001 probability tolerance, relative error is 5% of a .02 probability but only 0.2% of a .5 probability.

<QaeEstimationBudget client:visible mode="tail" title="Keep the boundary mass in Expected Shortfall" />

### When to apply QAE to risk

Separate loss-scenario generation under the chosen physical model, threshold search and a bounded tail expectation. For fixed absolute tolerance, rare Bernoulli events actually have smaller variance $p(1-p)$ than p=0.5; **fixed relative** rare-probability accuracy is a different, harder requirement. A small tail does not automatically give a universal speedup ratio. The quantum algorithm cannot create unsupported tail scenarios or validate the financial loss model.

## 6. Compare complete resource and error budgets

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"Which single comparison is sufficient to prove a real runtime advantage?"}

A lower number of engine cycles is useful only after you know what an engine cycle costs. Here is an invented cost comparison with an explicit common target: **absolute probability error at most .001 and success at least 95%** for one bounded expectation.

Classical Hoeffding sampling needs the sufficient count

$$
N=\left\lceil\frac{\ln40}{2(.001)^2}\right\rceil=1{,}844{,}440.
$$

This is a conservative guarantee, not a claim that the best classical method needs exactly that many samples. Quantum sizing uses the unknown-a worst-case bound $\pi/M+\pi^2/M^2$. The smallest permitted power of two satisfying the target is M=4096. Seven independent runs meet the confidence target from the previous section, giving **28,665 Q operations and 57,337 preparation/inverse blocks**.

Now stipulate 1 microsecond per classical sample, 100 microseconds per counted quantum preparation/inverse block, and 0.05 seconds for **all remaining quantum work**, including construction/compilation, reflections, controlled-operation overhead, readout and classical processing in this toy accounting. Assume these costs include their respective execution overheads; they are not measured hardware specifications:

<QaeEstimationBudget client:visible mode="resources" title="Price each counted block in an invented cost model" />

A complete error budget is separate. In the pricing encoding above, probability error .01 contributes a .95 USD value-error bound on its stated success event. If separately justified monetary bounds for truncation, discretisation/payoff approximation and implementation bias are .40, .20 and .10 USD, the triangle inequality gives a total bound of **1.65 USD** on that event. These numbers are stipulated, not automatically certified by QAE. Errors in the economic model or probability law sit outside that numerical guarantee. Multiple random error bounds would additionally need joint confidence control.

### When to believe a practical advantage claim

Compare the same task, measure, error units and confidence against appropriate classical alternatives, including closed forms and variance reduction when available. Count construction separately from repeated applications, coherent depth separately from total calls, and physical hardware resources separately from logical oracle complexity. The theorem motivates that investigation; the complete evidence determines the outcome.
