A coin offers +50% or −40% on the amount exposed, with equal chances. Its expected one-round return is +5%. Yet exposing all your wealth to the same independent gamble repeatedly produces negative long-run log growth. The arithmetic is sound on both sides. The questions differ.

Before borrowing the word **ergodic**, put a label on the average: *which variable, over which people or dates, under which process?* A weather station averaging rain through time and a map averaging rain across places need not report the same climate. A crowd of investors and one investor's compounded wealth require the same care.

## Time averages need an observable and a process

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Every date has the same average across a population. Must one person's time average approach that population average?" options={[{text:'No: a persistent hidden regime can make the limits differ',correct:true},{text:'Yes: a constant mean alone proves full ergodicity'},{text:'No: all processes with randomness violate the law of large numbers'}]} explanation="A constant marginal mean is weaker than stationarity, and stationarity is weaker than ergodicity. We need assumptions about the joint process, not just one mean." />

A **stochastic process** describes random observations through time, including their dependence. An **observable** is the particular quantity you measure: a return, a log return, an indicator of profit, or wealth. Changing the observable changes the question.

An **ensemble mean** averages that observable across the model's possible outcomes at a given date. A **time average** averages the *same observable* along one history. For observations $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, the latter is $\bar X_n=(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n$.

**Strict stationarity** means the joint distribution is unchanged by shifting every date together. Constant mean alone is insufficient. Under a stationary, ergodic law, time averages of integrable observables converge almost surely to their ensemble expectations. *Integrable* means the expected absolute value is finite; *almost surely* means with probability one under the model, rather than after a guaranteed finite waiting time. Equality for one chosen observable is a weaker property than ergodicity of the whole process. These distinctions follow the [stationary-process treatment in Stanford's information-theory notes](https://ee376c.sites.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj31816/files/media/file/combined_lecture_notes_0.pdf).

**A stationary counterexample without a ruin barrier.** Imagine a synthetic indicator $X_t$: 1 means a profitable trading day, 0 means an unprofitable one. Profit sizes are deliberately outside this example. First select a hidden regime once:

Conditional on that regime, daily indicators are independent with the stated constant probability. The regime never changes. Each observable indicator can still switch between 0 and 1; neither observed value is absorbing.

At every date the marginal mean is $E[X_t]=0.5(0.25)+0.5(0.75)=0.5$. This mixture is strictly stationary. Nevertheless, a low-regime history has $\bar X_n\to0.25$, and a high-regime history has $\bar X_n\to0.75$, each by the conditional law of large numbers. Neither limit is 0.5. The permanent regime supplies an invariant event with probability 0.5, so the stationary mixture is not ergodic.

The observations are not unconditionally independent. For distinct dates, $E[X_sX_t]=0.5(0.25^2)+0.5(0.75^2)=0.3125$, so their covariance is $0.3125-0.5^2=0.0625$. A persistent common cause ties the dates together even though each date has the same marginal distribution.

### When to use it

Ask whether a pooled historical estimate mixes persistent regimes before treating it as the time average available to one account. The toy model is not evidence that real market regimes are permanent. It isolates the assumption that fails: stationarity does not imply ergodicity.

## Positive wealth factors: the law of large numbers still applies

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Independent positive wealth multipliers have finite expected absolute log size. Does compounding invalidate the law of large numbers for their log increments?" options={[{text:'No: the average log increment converges to its expectation',correct:true},{text:'Yes: the law only works for monetary gains added to a balance'},{text:'Yes: every compounded path reaches exactly zero after enough finite rounds'}]} explanation="Taking logs turns a product into a sum. Under the stated iid and integrability conditions, the strong law applies to those log increments." />

Return to the fair coin, with independent **multipliers** $M_t=1.5$ or $0.6$. A multiplier is final wealth divided by starting wealth for a round. With initial wealth $W_0=100$ and full exposure:

$$
W_n=100\prod_{t=1}^{n}M_t,\qquad E[M_t]=0.5(1.5)+0.5(0.6)=1.05.
$$

Independence gives $E[W_n]=100(1.05)^n$. This expectation includes every path with its probability; it does not delete losers. It also does not promise that a finite crowd's sample mean will equal it.

The log increment $Y_t=\ln M_t$ is iid, with finite absolute expectation. Therefore:

$$
\frac1n\ln\frac{W_n}{W_0}=\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\ln M_t\ \longrightarrow\ g=E[\ln M_t].
$$

Here $g=0.5\ln1.5+0.5\ln0.6=0.5\ln0.9\approx-0.05268026$ log units per round. Its **equivalent geometric simple return** is $e^g-1=\sqrt{0.9}-1\approx-5.13167\%$. A log rate and a percentage return are related, but are not the same number.

In this fair binary model with an **even** number of rounds, the middle binomial outcome has equally many ups and downs. Its wealth is the unique population median and equals the geometric benchmark. Do not generalise that coincidence. With **one round**, wealth is 60 or 150, each with probability 0.5. Every value in $[60,150]$ is a population median; $100\sqrt{0.9}\approx94.86833$ is one such value, not a unique median. At odd horizons the adjacent central outcomes again bound an interval of medians. The chart's *sample median* is a separate finite-sample statistic, using the average of the two middle observations when the number of players is even.

The iid multiplier process and its log increments are stationary and ergodic. The wealth-level process started at 100 has a changing distribution: it is not stationary under this initialisation. The gap between $\ln E[M]$ and $E[\ln M]$ compares a nonlinear transformation of a mean with a mean of that transformation. It is **not a failure of the law of large numbers for log increments**. Calling compounded wealth “nonergodic” without naming the observable conceals these distinctions.

All finite wealth values remain strictly positive because both factors are positive. Negative $g$ makes wealth tend to zero almost surely as the horizon tends to infinity; it does not produce exact zero after a finite number of rounds. A minimum viable balance, debt payment or margin threshold introduces an additional stopping rule.

### When to use it

Use expected wealth for an explicitly chosen finite-horizon expected-wealth objective. Use expected log growth for the long-run compound-growth objective under the stated assumptions. Neither objective alone handles spending needs, drawdown limits, taxes or uncertain probabilities. [Peters and Gell-Mann's dynamics-based approach](https://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0585) advocates time growth as a decision criterion; that advocacy is not a theorem requiring every investor to have the same preferences.

## Volatility drag: an exact model before an approximation

Jensen's inequality for the concave logarithm gives $E[\ln M]\le\ln E[M]$ for positive factors when the relevant expectations exist. It explains this gap. Variance by itself does not determine expected log growth across arbitrary distributions: tail shape and dependence also matter.

For a narrower model, let each independent simple return be $+s$ or $-s$ with equal probability and $0\le s<1$. The mean simple return is zero, but:

$$
g=\tfrac12\ln(1+s)+\tfrac12\ln(1-s)=\tfrac12\ln(1-s^2),\qquad e^g-1=\sqrt{1-s^2}-1.
$$

One +30% round and one −30% round give $100(1.3)(0.7)=91$, in either order. The exact log growth is $\tfrac12\ln0.91\approx-0.04715534$ per round, and its equivalent simple return is −4.60608%. For small $s$, the approximation $g\approx-s^2/2$ gives −0.045 at $s=0.3$. It is an approximation to the **log rate**, not an exact realised return. At $s=0.1$, exact $g\approx-0.00502517$, while the approximation is −0.005.

<EnsembleTimeFan client:visible rounds={20} upFactor={1.3} downFactor={0.7} title="Zero mean return does not make wealth stationary" caption="Independent +30%/−30% returns have expected wealth 100 at every date. The distribution changes, the sample mean is random, and the geometric benchmark falls. No trajectory is forced to alternate its returns." />

Here the constant mean wealth 100 still does not establish stationarity. Since $E[M^2]=(1.3^2+0.7^2)/2=1.09$, $\operatorname{Var}(W_n)=10,000(1.09^n-1)$: variance is 900 after one round and 1,881 after two. The distribution widens while its mean stays put.

### When to use it

Use an exact factor model to check whether a shortcut is reasonable. Over the same set of positive returns, the terminal product does not depend on their order. Interim drawdowns, withdrawals and barrier hits can depend on order, so a terminal-product identity is not a complete path-risk analysis.

## Kelly sizing: define what the fraction measures

Changing the exposure changes the factors. Keep fraction $x$ of current wealth in the +50%/−40% asset and the rest in cash earning zero in this invented model. Each round resets that fraction without costs, borrowing or withdrawals. Wealth factors become $1+0.5x$ and $1-0.4x$.

$$
g(x)=\tfrac12\ln(1+0.5x)+\tfrac12\ln(1-0.4x),\qquad 0\le x\le1.
$$

Differentiating gives $g'(x)=0.25/(1+0.5x)-0.2/(1-0.4x)$. Setting it to zero gives $0.25-0.1x=0.2+0.1x$, hence $x^*=0.25$.

At 25% exposure, $g=0.5\ln1.0125\approx0.00621126$, with equivalent geometric return $\sqrt{1.0125}-1\approx0.623059\%$ per round. No individual round is guaranteed to earn that amount.

A binary-bet chart often uses another fraction: **$f$ is the fraction of wealth lost on a losing bet**. Here $f=0.4x$ and winning net odds are $b=0.5/0.4=1.25$. With win probability $p=0.5$ and $q=0.5$, $f^*=(bp-q)/b=(0.625-0.5)/1.25=0.1$. Thus **10% loss stake means 25% asset exposure**, not 10% exposure.

### When to use it

Kelly answers a particular optimisation problem: maximise expected log growth with known stable probabilities, repeatable payoffs and the specified constraints. Estimated inputs, trading costs, finite horizons, liabilities and drawdown tolerance can justify a different stake or objective. Positive expected return alone does not select a safe or optimal exposure.

## Absorbing barriers are an additional model choice

An **absorbing state** is a state that cannot be left under the specified process. Account closure at a floor can make failure absorbing. That feature is not necessary for the stationary-regime counterexample above, nor does every process containing an absorbing state hit it with probability one from every starting point.

Start with a different model: each surviving round independently has an immediate fatal-event probability $h=0.01$, constant through time. Survival through $n$ rounds requires no fatal event:

$$
P(\text{survive }n)=(1-h)^n,\qquad P(\text{ruin by }n)=1-(1-h)^n.
$$

For constant $h>0$, survival tends to zero as $n$ tends to infinity. This conclusion follows from the **constant immediate hazard**, not from the word “absorbing”. A wealth threshold typically has a hazard depending on the current distance to it.

For comparison, take independent fixed monetary steps +1 or −1, win probability $p=0.55$, a fixed absorbing floor 10 stake units below the start, no upper target and infinite horizon. The eventual floor-hit probability is $(q/p)^{10}=(0.45/0.55)^{10}\approx13.44306\%$, not 100%. The underlying derivation and finite-horizon limits are taught in [Risk of Ruin](/risk-of-ruin). Those fixed steps differ from wagering a fraction of changing wealth.

### When to use it

Define the failure event, horizon, position-sizing rule and ability to recover before calculating ruin. A finite expected payoff can remain relevant, but does not encode a survival constraint. Pooling many risks can change an institution's exposure if the risks really are pooled; dependence, tail losses and finite capital still matter. An unconditional ensemble mean includes failed accounts. Excluding them is survivorship bias, a separate error.

## Carry the assumptions into the decision

A useful calculation names both its objective and its limits. “Expected wealth rises” is a distributional statement. “Average log growth is negative” describes a long-run rate under a particular model. “The account may close below this floor” adds a path-dependent constraint. All three may be true at once.
