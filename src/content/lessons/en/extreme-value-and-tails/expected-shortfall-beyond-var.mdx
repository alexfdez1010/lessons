Two models both report a finite 95% VaR of 20 loss units. One has ES 40; the other has infinite ES. There is no contradiction: a finite quantile does not establish that the whole upper tail has a finite average.

**Expected Shortfall (ES)** averages the worst specified probability mass of losses. That definition needs care at probability atoms and at an unbounded tail. We will calculate both, using invented losses, a fixed horizon and the convention that **positive L is loss and negative L is gain**. These model calculations do not guarantee future coverage or identify a real portfolio’s tail distribution.

Let $F(\ell)=P(L\le\ell)$ and $0<c<1$. Use the lower inverse-CDF quantile

$$q_c=\operatorname{VaR}_c=\inf\{\ell:F(\ell)\ge c\}$$

The inequality matters for discrete losses: the CDF may jump over $c$. The strict exceedance probability can be smaller than $1-c$. VaR is a threshold, not the maximum possible loss.

Define upper-tail ES by the quantile integral

$$\operatorname{ES}_c=\frac1{1-c}\int_c^1q_p\,dp$$

when it is finite, or as an extended value such as positive infinity for the positive tails below. Think of filling a measuring cup with exactly $1-c$ probability from the worst outcomes. If a probability block sits on the boundary, use only the fraction needed to finish the cup.

With $q=q_c$, the boundary correction for an integrable loss is

$$\operatorname{ES}_c=\frac{E[L\mathbf1_{\{L>q\}}]+q\,[F(q)-c]}{1-c}$$

An **atom** is positive probability at one exact loss. For a continuous distribution at the boundary with $F(q)=c$, the correction vanishes and ES equals $E[L\mid L>q]$. With an atom, neither the average of all losses strictly above VaR nor the average of all losses at or above it is generally the required ES. [Acerbi and Tasche explain why the precise discontinuous-distribution definition matters](https://arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0104295).

### A finite empirical calculation with partial boundary mass

Take ten equally probable losses: EUR 1 through EUR 9, and EUR 100. At 85% confidence, the ascending index is $\lceil0.85(10)\rceil=9$, so VaR is **EUR 9**. The tail has 1.5 scenario-equivalents: all of EUR 100 and half the mass at EUR 9.

$$\operatorname{ES}_{0.85}=\frac{0.10(100)+0.05(9)}{0.15}=\frac{10.45}{0.15}\approx69.6667\ \mathrm{EUR}$$

The strict conditional mean would be 100; the mean of all losses at least 9 would be $(100+9)/2=54.5$. Neither equals 69.6667.

### When to use it

Report horizon, confidence, loss sign, scenario probabilities and quantile convention. Under this definition ES is at least VaR at the **same** confidence; equality is possible. It is not necessarily the largest observed loss. The [historical-simulation lesson](/value-at-risk/historical-simulation-var) develops the exact empirical boundary convention, including ties and small tail counts.

Subadditivity does **not** say that adding a second full-sized book cannot raise risk above the first book alone. Two certain losses of 3 and 2 have combined ES 5: larger than either individually, equal to their sum. Comparing a fixed-budget mixture is a different operation. [Acerbi and Tasche derive the aggregation properties of ES](https://arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0105191).

### Recalculate the two-bond example completely

Each invented bond independently loses EUR 100 with probability 4%, or gains EUR 2 with probability 96%. At 95%, each bond’s VaR is **−2 EUR**. That negative quantile does not erase its default risk. Each ES fills 5% probability with 4% loss at 100 and 1% gain at −2:

$$\operatorname{ES}_{0.95}(A)=\operatorname{ES}_{0.95}(B)=\frac{0.04(100)+0.01(-2)}{0.05}=79.6\ \mathrm{EUR}$$

The joint loss distribution is

Combined VaR is **98 EUR**, so it exceeds the sum of the standalone VaRs, −4 EUR. This is a subadditivity violation by VaR. It is not a violation of monotonicity, which compares losses outcome by outcome.

For ES, use all of the 0.16% both-default mass and 4.84% from the atom at 98:

$$\operatorname{ES}_{0.95}(A+B)=\frac{0.0016(200)+0.0484(98)}{0.05}=101.264\ \mathrm{EUR}$$

Thus $101.264\le79.6+79.6=159.2$. The combined ES is larger than one bond’s ES but smaller than their sum. Averaging every loss at least 98 would instead use 7.84% probability and produce about 100.0816 EUR, a different statistic.

### When to use it

Use coherent aggregation under a common horizon, loss model and probability law. Coherence is a mathematical property, not a guarantee of a correct tail fit, a finite answer on every distribution, or protection against every liquidity and model risk.

## Pareto I separates a finite quantile from a finite tail mean

Use a positive **Pareto I loss** with lower bound $x_m>0$ and shape $\alpha>0$:

$$P(L>\ell)=\left(\frac{x_m}{\ell}\right)^\alpha,\qquad\ell\ge x_m$$

This is Pareto I, not a shifted Pareto or Lomax parameterization. Solve the survival probability $1-c$ for the quantile:

$$q_c=x_m(1-c)^{-1/\alpha}$$

It is finite for every $c<1$ and $\alpha>0$. At $x_m=1$, $c=0.95$, $\alpha=2$, it is $\sqrt{20}\approx4.472136$.

For this continuous positive tail, the conditional survival beyond $q$ is $(q/\ell)^\alpha$. Its tail mean is

$$\operatorname{ES}_c=q+\int_q^\infty\left(\frac q\ell\right)^\alpha d\ell$$

When $\alpha>1$,

$$\int_q^\infty\left(\frac q\ell\right)^\alpha d\ell=\frac q{\alpha-1},\qquad\operatorname{ES}_c=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}q$$

For $\alpha=2$, ES is $2\sqrt{20}\approx8.944272$. For $\alpha=1$, the integral is logarithmic and diverges. For $0<\alpha<1$, the positive power in its antiderivative also diverges. Therefore **ES is infinite when alpha is at most one**, despite a finite VaR. [The actuar documentation specifies this Pareto I parameterization and its moment conditions](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/actuar/refman/actuar.html#SingleParameterPareto).

The parameters differ, but both quantiles are exactly 20. The second model allocates enough severity to farther losses that the average never converges. “Infinite ES” describes a divergent model expectation, not an infinite loss on every path. Every single draw can be finite.

### When to use it

Check the tail’s **first moment** before quoting a finite ES. Pareto I’s kth positive moment requires $\alpha>k$. In particular, $1<\alpha\le2$ gives finite ES but infinite second moment. The existence of a tail mean and the finite-variance assumptions behind ordinary mean standard errors are separate checks.

## A finite cap changes the distribution; a finite sample does not prove a cap

Suppose the loss really changes to $L_B=\min(L,B)$, with $B\ge q_c$. This is **capping**, also called censoring or winsorizing the upper loss: outcomes beyond B are replaced by B, placing an atom there. It is different from **conditioning** on $L\le B$, which discards and renormalizes outcomes.

For the capped loss, the original VaR remains $q$ and its ES is finite:

$$\operatorname{ES}_c(L_B)=q+\int_q^B\left(\frac q\ell\right)^\alpha d\ell$$

Writing $R=B/q\ge1$ gives

$$\frac{\operatorname{ES}_c(L_B)}q=1+\frac{R^{1-\alpha}-1}{1-\alpha}\quad(\alpha\ne1)$$

$$\frac{\operatorname{ES}_c(L_B)}q=1+\log R\quad(\alpha=1)$$

At $x_m=1$, $\alpha=1$, $c=0.95$, VaR is 20. A genuine cap B = 200 gives

$$\operatorname{ES}_{0.95}(\min(L,200))=20(1+\log10)\approx66.051702$$

Raising the cap to 2,000 gives $20(1+\log100)\approx112.103404$. Every finite cap gives a finite number, while the original uncapped ES remains infinite.

### When to use it

Document whether an upper bound is part of the actual exposure, a contractual payoff assumption, a modelling choice or simply the largest observed loss. Evaluating a plotted integral only up to a finite endpoint cannot establish convergence of the unbounded tail integral. Conversely, a model’s infinite mean does not make its finite empirical summaries infinite.

## The GPD formula retains the threshold and scale

The previous [peaks-over-threshold lesson](/extreme-value-and-tails/extreme-value-theory) fits excess losses above a threshold $u$. Let the GPD excess shape be $\xi$ and scale $\beta>0$. At a quantile $q_c\ge u$ inside the modelled support, the conditional scale above $q_c$ is $\beta+\xi(q_c-u)$. For $\xi<1$,

$$\operatorname{ES}_c=q_c+\frac{\beta+\xi(q_c-u)}{1-\xi}=\frac{q_c+\beta-\xi u}{1-\xi}$$

The threshold-scale correction matters. If $u=10$, $\beta=2$, $\xi=0.25$ and a previously calculated tail quantile is $q_c=20$,

$$\beta+\xi(q_c-u)=2+0.25(10)=4.5$$

$$\operatorname{ES}_c=20+\frac{4.5}{0.75}=26$$

The ratio is 1.3, whereas simply multiplying VaR by $1/(1-\xi)=4/3$ would give 26.6667. [Chavez-Demoulin and Embrechts give the full threshold-and-scale ES expression](https://people.math.ethz.ch/~embrecht/ftp/edge_revisiting.pdf).

For a positive GPD tail with $0<\xi<1$, the ratio approaches $1/(1-\xi)$ at sufficiently high positive q when the additive correction is negligible. That limiting ratio is not a substitute for the full finite-threshold formula. Negative ξ describes a bounded GPD tail; the positive power-law identity $\alpha=1/\xi$ is not its tail-index interpretation.

### When to use it

Keep threshold, scale, shape, exceedance probability and the target confidence from the same fitted model. Extrapolation remains conditional on that model and threshold choice; a fitted formula does not establish the unseen tail. Near ξ = 1, ES is especially sensitive to estimation uncertainty.

## Finite calculations still need tail-model uncertainty

With 250 equally weighted finite losses at 99% confidence, the empirical tail has **2.5 scenario-equivalents**. If its three largest losses are 500, 300 and 200, inverse-ECDF VaR is 200 and

$$\operatorname{ES}_{0.99}^{\mathrm{emp}}=\frac{500+300+0.5(200)}{2.5}=360$$

The tail mean is finite and reproducible, but only two-and-a-half observations’ mass determines it. Adding a large new loss can move it sharply. This arithmetic alone does not identify whether a Pareto population has alpha above or below one.

At $x_m=1$ and 95%, a fitted Pareto alpha of 1.2 gives VaR about **12.139245** and finite ES **72.835468**. At alpha 1.05, VaR is about **17.341082** but ES is **364.162717**. Both have infinite second moments. Small shape changes near the mean-existence boundary can matter far more than extra displayed decimals.

### When to use it

Separate empirical ES, capped-payoff ES and extrapolated population ES in reports. State the moment assumptions and sensitivity to tail shape, threshold, dependence and data selection. Do not infer a well-calibrated ordinary root-count SE just because an ES estimate is finite. If plausible tail specifications cross the first-moment boundary, report that existence uncertainty explicitly. Breach counts alone assess a quantile threshold; tail severity requires additional evidence.

## Rebuild the tail report

Start with a quantile convention and exactly the required upper probability mass. Check whether the population tail mean exists before interpreting a finite ES output. A cap, a truncated model and a sample maximum are different assumptions. Use full GPD parameters for finite-threshold calculations, and keep shape uncertainty beside the reported number.
