A model can assign zero **asymptotic tail dependence** and still give a substantial chance of two assets crossing the same finite crash threshold. The word *asymptotic* matters: it asks what happens as the threshold moves indefinitely farther into the tail, not whether joint losses are possible today.

The earlier [tail-index lesson](/extreme-value-and-tails/power-laws-and-tail-index) examined one distribution's extremes. Here we compare two assets. All examples concern **lower tails of returns**, so a low value is bad. For loss variables, the corresponding bad tail is usually the upper one. Probabilities below are model quantities for a specified observation period; no annual crisis frequency or causal contagion is inferred.

**Pearson correlation** measures linear co-movement when the necessary second moments exist:

$$\rho_P=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}$$

It is one summary of a joint distribution, not the whole distribution. A single unconditional estimate does not tell you every conditional relationship or the probabilities of joint threshold crossings. You can estimate different correlations in different regimes; that is a separate modelling choice, rather than a feature encoded by one fixed number.

Think of a weather report's average temperature: it tells you something useful, but does not specify the chance that two towns both freeze on the same night. The missing question needs a joint event, not a louder average.

### Compare marginal and joint probabilities

Suppose each return is below its own chosen threshold with probability **5%**. Compare two hypothetical joint-event models:

| Model | Each marginal tail probability | Both below their thresholds | B below, given A below |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Independent events | 5% | 0.05 × 0.05 = 0.25% | 0.0025 ÷ 0.05 = 5% |
| A more clustered joint event | 5% | 1% | 0.01 ÷ 0.05 = 20% |

The marginal probabilities match; the joint and conditional probabilities do not. This table does not claim the two complete distributions have the same Pearson correlation. It isolates the distinction between “each is rare” and “rare together.”

### When to use it

Ask for the joint event you actually care about: two defaults, losses in several markets or simultaneous funding shortfalls. A modest correlation is neither a guarantee of diversification nor proof that no diversification exists. For sufficiently heavy tails, population variances may not exist, so Pearson correlation itself may be undefined even when a copula remains well defined.

## A copula joins marginal ranks

A **marginal distribution** describes one variable by itself. Its cumulative distribution function, or **CDF**, gives the probability that the variable is no larger than a stated value. For continuous marginals, the transforms $U=F_X(X)$ and $V=F_Y(Y)$ are uniform on the unit interval.

A **copula** is their joint distribution on that probability scale. Sklar's theorem gives

$$F_{X,Y}(x,y)=C\big(F_X(x),F_Y(y)\big)$$

With continuous marginals the copula is unique; with discrete marginals uniqueness is more limited. The separation is like describing each instrument's volume separately from the timing between the instruments. Changing volumes does not automatically preserve a linear correlation measured in the original units.

### Map a return threshold into probability space

Suppose asset A's −8% return is its 5th percentile, and asset B's −12% return is also its 5th percentile. Then $F_X(-0.08)=F_Y(-0.12)=0.05$. For a hypothetical copula with $C(0.05,0.05)=0.01$:

- Joint probability of crossing both return thresholds = **1%**
- Probability of crossing A's threshold = **5%**
- Probability of crossing B's threshold given A's crossing = **1% ÷ 5% = 20%**

The return cutoffs differ, but both map to the same rank. The dependence model acts on the ranks; the marginal models determine what those ranks mean in actual returns.

### When to use it

Use the separation to compare dependence assumptions while holding marginal loss models fixed, or to change marginals without assuming dependence disappears. A copula parameter called “correlation” belongs to its construction. It need not equal the Pearson correlation of the final asset returns. For the Gaussian and t families, equal latent correlation parameters also give equal Kendall rank correlation, but not identical joint distributions.

## A finite threshold is different from a limit

Write the **finite lower-tail conditional probability** as

$$p_L(q)=P(V\le q\mid U\le q)=\frac{C(q,q)}{q},\qquad0<q<1$$

The **lower tail-dependence coefficient** is the limit, if it exists:

$$\lambda_L=\lim_{q\downarrow0}p_L(q)$$

For a nondegenerate Gaussian copula with $-1<\rho<1$, $\lambda_L=0$. This is **asymptotic independence**, not independence at every finite threshold. At the excluded perfectly co-moving boundary, $\rho=1$, both ranks coincide and the coefficient is one.

### Work a Gaussian finite-threshold calculation

For a Gaussian copula with latent $\rho=0.5$, the numerical reference at $q=0.01$ is

$$C(0.01,0.01)\approx0.0012939244$$

Therefore

$$p_L(0.01)\approx\frac{0.0012939244}{0.01}=0.12939244\approx12.9392\%$$

The **joint** event is about **0.1294%**; the **conditional** probability is about **12.9392%**. Neither is zero. Under an independent copula at the same quantile, the joint probability is 0.01² = **0.01%** and the conditional probability is **1%**. The correlated Gaussian model therefore predicts more simultaneous finite-threshold losses than independence, despite its zero limiting coefficient.

### A reproducible numerical route

With $z=\Phi^{-1}(q)$, where $\Phi$ and $\phi$ are the standard normal CDF and density:

$$C_G(q,q)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\phi(x)\Phi\!\left(\frac{z-\rho x}{\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\right)\,dx$$

The chart's values were precomputed from this conditional integral and independently checked after changing variables to the uniform probability scale. They are rounded numerical references on the displayed grid, not certified integration bounds or a general browser solver. The Gaussian finite conditional probabilities at $\rho=0.5$ fall from about **32.4015%** at the lowest decile to **5.4259%** at the lowest 0.1%; a finite endpoint is still not the zero limit.

### When to use it

For a specified risk threshold, calculate the finite probability. Use the coefficient to compare limiting tail behaviour, with estimation uncertainty and the observation horizon stated. Zero tail dependence does not make a Gaussian copula automatically unsuitable; it makes its asymptotic structure an assumption to check against the use case. It cannot by itself explain or rule out a historical financial crisis.

## The t coefficient depends on correlation and degrees of freedom

A **Student-t copula** uses the rank dependence of a multivariate t distribution. A common random scale can create dependence in large observations beyond what its latent correlation parameter describes alone. For finite $\nu>0$ and $-1<\rho<1$, its lower and upper coefficients are equal and positive:

$$\lambda_L=\lambda_U=2T_{\nu+1}\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{(\nu+1)(1-\rho)}{1+\rho}}\right)$$

$T_{\nu+1}$ is the CDF of a univariate t distribution with $\nu+1$ degrees of freedom. This is a model formula, not a claim that one asset causes another to fall. The [Demarta–McNeil paper derives the coefficient and discusses finite joint-quantile events](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-5823.2005.tb00254.x).

### Compute the limit and compare it with a finite value

For $\nu=4$ and $\rho=0.5$, the argument is

$$-\sqrt{\frac{5(0.5)}{1.5}}=-\sqrt{\frac53}\approx-1.290994$$

Then $T_5(-1.290994)\approx0.126585$, so the coefficient is approximately 2 × 0.126585 = **0.253170**, or **25.3170%**. This is the limiting conditional probability as both marginal quantile levels shrink toward zero. It does not mean that there is a 25.3170% chance of a crash on any day, or a precise forecast conditional on an undefined “worst-ever day.”

At the finite **1%** quantile, the reference conditional probability is instead **28.7678%**. Joint probability = 0.01 × 0.287678 ≈ **0.2877%**. At the finite **0.1%** quantile, the conditional value is about **26.3493%**, closer to the limit but still a different quantity.

### Hold degrees of freedom fixed

| Pairwise latent correlation, with degrees of freedom fixed at 4 | Limiting coefficient |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | About 7.5587% |
| 0.25 | About 14.3811% |
| 0.50 | About 25.3170% |
| 0.75 | About 43.6588% |

The common degrees-of-freedom setting does not make these coefficients equal. At a fixed correlation in this family, lower degrees of freedom strengthen limiting tail dependence; increasing degrees of freedom approaches the Gaussian limit. A zero latent correlation in a t copula does not imply independence. The t copula's degrees of freedom govern its dependence construction; after attaching different marginals, they do not automatically become the tail indices of those asset returns.

### When to use it

Compare multiple parameter combinations rather than treating “t copula” as a complete specification. The standard t copula is symmetric between upper and lower tails and uses a common degrees-of-freedom parameter; pairwise correlations still vary. Asymmetric or more flexible copulas may be needed for asymmetric or heterogeneous dependence. No family is a universal crisis model.

## Choose and validate the model at the relevant threshold

Model choice is a comparison of assumptions and evidence. A tail-dependent family is not automatically more accurate, and a zero-limit family does not predict no joint losses. Calibrate and compare the **finite** joint probabilities at thresholds relevant to the portfolio, alongside marginal fit, stress scenarios and sensitivity.

### Put the sample size beside the probability

With 1,000 observations and a 1% conditioning event, the expected number in A's tail is **10**. Under the illustrative $\rho=0.5$ models, expected joint-event counts are approximately:

- Gaussian: 1,000 × 0.0012939244 = **1.2939**
- t with 4 degrees of freedom: 1,000 × 0.0028767843 = **2.8768**

These are expected counts, not fractional observed events or a claim of independent daily returns. Very few joint observations can make it hard to distinguish models. Fitting more parameters to the same sparse tail does not manufacture evidence.

### What each model still leaves open

| Choice | Assumption or limitation to examine |
|---|---|
| Gaussian copula | Zero asymptotic coefficient, but nonzero finite joint losses for positive latent correlation |
| Standard t copula | Symmetric upper/lower tail dependence and dependence on both correlation and degrees of freedom |
| Static parameters | Do not automatically describe changing market regimes |
| Estimated marginal distributions | Determine actual return/loss cutoffs and can be wrong themselves |

The [BIS report on risk aggregation discusses dependence assumptions and model risk](https://www.bis.org/publ/joint25.pdf). A stress test should examine exposures and funding consequences under plausible joint shocks, including scenarios the fitted copula may not represent. A copula is a joint-distribution tool, not a causal explanation, a guarantee of diversification or a promise that the next crisis resembles the calibration sample.
