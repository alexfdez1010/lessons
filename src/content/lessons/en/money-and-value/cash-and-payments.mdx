Start with the simplest money there is. **Cash** (also called **currency**) is the
physical notes and coins issued by a country's central bank or government. A \$5 bill, a
quarter — that's cash. Each note or coin has a **denomination**: the face value printed
on it (\$1, \$5, \$20…). A small handful of denominations can tile up to make any amount,
the way a few LEGO brick sizes build any shape.

Cash is a **bearer instrument**: the holder can generally transfer it by handing it over, without an account entry identifying the next holder.

<Callout variant="info" title="Analogy: a movie ticket">
Cash is like a movie ticket. Whoever's holding it gets into the show — the usher never
checks whose name is on it, because there *is* no name. Drop your ticket in the parking
lot and a stranger can use it; drop a \$50 bill and it's just… gone. That's the bearer
property: brilliant for privacy and finality, brutal if you lose it.
</Callout>

### Worked example: making change

You owe **\$13** and pay with a **\$20** note. The cashier owes you **\$7** in change,
which gets assembled out of denominations — say a **\$5** note plus **two \$1** notes.
The whole exchange settles on the spot: the instant the notes change hands, the
transaction is *done*. No bank, no waiting, no "pending."

<Callout variant="warning" title="Misconception: 'cash is the same as my bank balance'">
They feel interchangeable, but they're different *kinds* of money. Cash is a bearer
object you physically hold. A bank balance is a **claim** — a promise the bank owes you
(that's the whole next lesson). Cash needs no third party to spend; your bank balance
always does.
</Callout>

**When it matters:** cash can work when payment systems are down and does not automatically create an electronic payment record. A receipt or another record may still exist. You cannot block a lost note in the way you can block access through a bank card.

## What a Payment Actually Is

Strip away the technology and a **payment** (or **transaction**) is just this: a
**transfer of value from one party to another**, usually in exchange for goods or a
service. It has three pieces worth naming:

- The **payer** — the party who *sends* the value.
- The **payee** — the party who *receives* it.
- The **amount** — how much value moves.

A **sale** connects a payment with goods or a service. They need not arrive at the same time: you might pay before delivery or after receiving an invoice. Payments also cover gifts, taxes and debt repayments, so not every payment is a sale.

<Callout variant="info" title="Read the two directions separately">
For a coffee purchase, money goes from customer to café and the drink goes from café to customer. The animation shows that sale. The payment itself is the transfer of value, even when nothing is delivered in return.
</Callout>

## Cash vs Digital — and the Middle-Men

With cash, payer hands payee the notes directly; the transfer needs no bank-account update. The parties may still keep a receipt or other record. With a **digital payment** — a card tap, a bank transfer,
a phone wallet — *no physical money moves at all*. Instead, **instructions** move, and
a chain of institutions updates their records to reflect who now owns what.

Those institutions are **intermediaries**: parties that sit *between* payer and payee and
move the value on their behalf.

- A **bank** holds your money as an account balance and moves it when you instruct it to.
- A **card network** (think Visa or Mastercard) routes the authorization and the
  instruction between the buyer's bank and the seller's bank — it's the messaging layer,
  not a vault.

## Settlement & Finality

A card purchase has several stages, which may happen at different times:

1. **Authorisation** checks and approves the transaction under the card system’s rules, often placing a hold on funds or credit.
2. **Clearing** exchanges and reconciles the transaction information and the amounts owed.
3. **Settlement** transfers funds to discharge the relevant obligation between institutions.

**Settlement finality** is the point at which that transfer is irrevocable and unconditional under the system’s rules. This is a property of the settlement, not a promise that the customer can never receive a refund. A card dispute or **chargeback** can create a later obligation to return value after the original settlement. See the [BIS payments glossary](https://www.bis.org/cpmi/glossary.pdf) and [Visa’s dispute explanation](https://www.visa.com/en-us/support/business/dispute-resolution).

<Callout variant="warning" title="A status message is evidence of one stage">
“Approved” does not mean the shop has received final settlement. Equally, a refund or chargeback does not by itself prove that the original payment never settled. Identify the payment method and the specific status rather than treating every digital payment as the same process. Some bank-transfer systems settle in real time.
</Callout>

### Worked comparison

On a card sale, the terminal approves the purchase now and the banks may settle later. A customer may then dispute the transaction under the card rules. With genuine cash handed over for the sale, the payment settles on the spot; returning that cash as a refund would be a new payment.

## Fees: The Price of the Middle-Men

Convenience isn't free. For the payer at the register, cash is essentially free to spend.
Card acceptance can involve a fee — usually paid by the **merchant** (the seller) —
to the intermediaries for moving the value, doing it fast, and keeping a record. One possible
shape is a small **percentage** of the sale plus a fixed **per-transaction** fee.

### Worked example: a card fee

A customer buys **\$40** of goods on a card with a fee of **2.9% + \$0.30**:

- Percentage part: `2.9% × $40 = $1.16`
- Fixed part: `+ $0.30`
- Total fee: `$1.16 + $0.30 ≈ $1.46`

So the merchant collects \$40 from the customer but **nets about \$38.54** — the \$1.46
is the price paid to the banks and card network for moving the money and keeping the
books. That's why some shops post "card minimum \$10" signs: on a \$2 sale, a 30-cent
fixed fee is a painful 15%.

(the network validating the transaction, not a bank). We'll compare them head-to-head in

</Callout>

## Putting It Together

A payment transfers value from payer to payee; a sale also involves goods or a service. Cash settles by delivery without a bank-account update. A card payment typically separates authorisation, clearing and settlement; other digital methods can use different timing and rules.
