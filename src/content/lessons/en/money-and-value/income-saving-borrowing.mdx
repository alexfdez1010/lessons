Your account receives 1000 USD. Was it wages, a loan or money moved from your other account? The balance rises in all three cases, but the consequences are different. Before deciding what you can spend, find out what that receipt represents.

We will use a simple household boundary: both your current and savings accounts belong to you. “Cash” below includes those bank balances. The examples use paid, received amounts and exclude unpaid bills or income owed to you unless stated. These are household teaching accounts, not tax definitions or a national statistical measure.

## A receipt tells you that money arrived

A **receipt** is money coming into the account you are watching. An **outflow** is money leaving it. **Income** in our examples is money received from work or from an asset, such as wages or credited interest. Not every receipt is income. A new loan brings cash and a matching obligation to repay; a transfer from your own savings moves money you already owned.

An **asset** is something you own with an assigned value, including a bank balance. A **debt** is an amount you owe. **Net worth** is the value of all the assets in the chosen boundary minus its debts. It is a snapshot, while receipts and payments happen over a period.

Start with current-account cash 2000 USD, savings cash 1000 and debt 1500. The initial net worth is $2000+1000-1500=1500$ USD. Consider each case separately, returning to that start each time:

### When to use it

Classify receipts before treating them as recurring spending capacity. When examining one statement, identify which account it covers. When examining your household, count transfers between your own included accounts on both sides.

## Surplus, cash change and net worth answer different questions

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="In the monthly example, should the new 1000 USD loan be added to the 3010 USD income before calculating the surplus?" text="No. Income is 3010 and consumption plus borrowing costs are 2650, leaving 360. The loan enters the cash and debt reconciliation separately." />

A **cost** uses up value: for example, consumed groceries, rent, paid loan interest or a fee. In this lesson's paid household budget, **surplus** means income minus those consumption and borrowing costs; a negative result is a **deficit**. A purchase of an asset retained at equal value, a transfer and repayment of debt principal are different uses of cash, not consumption costs in this definition.

A practical budget can still reserve money for savings or principal under its “expenses” heading. That is a planning category. Keep its meaning separate from the classification used to calculate the result below. [Consumer.gov's budget guide](https://consumer.gov/your-money/making-budget) includes saving allocations as a possible budget item; the label does not make an own-account transfer destroy wealth.

### A month that reconciles

Open with current cash 500 USD, savings cash 1000 and debt 2000. Receive wages of 3000 after payroll deductions into current and interest 10 into savings. Pay consumption 2600, loan interest 30 and fees 20 from current. Also receive a new loan 1000, repay existing principal 150 and transfer 400 from current to savings. Assume all these amounts occur as stated, with no other assets, revaluations or unpaid items.

$$
\text{Budget result}=3000+10-2600-30-20=360\text{ USD}.
$$

The result is a 360-USD surplus. Combined cash additionally includes the financing flows:

$$
\text{Cash change}=360+1000-150=1210\text{ USD}.
$$

The internal transfer cancels in the two-account total. Current closes at $500+3000-2600-30-20+1000-150-400=1300$ USD. Savings closes at $1000+10+400=1410$. Their total is $2710=1500+1210$ USD. Debt closes at $2000+1000-150=2850$ USD.

Net worth rises by $1210-850=360$ USD, matching the budget result **under these assumptions**. This identity needs adjustment if asset values change, income or costs are recorded before cash changes, or other items enter. Negative net worth is possible: here debt still exceeds the model's cash assets at month-end.

If consumption rises to 3160 while the other income and costs stay fixed, the result becomes $3010-(3160+30+20)=-200$ USD. With the new loan still 1000 and no principal payment, combined cash changes by $-200+1000=800$ USD while debt rises 1000. Cash can rise during a deficit. Set the consumption and principal controls to reproduce that case.

### When to use it

Reconcile the period's income and costs, all cash movements and the opening/closing stocks. A deficit can be funded with existing cash, an asset sale or borrowing; funding changes how it is covered, not the income-minus-cost result. A positive closing balance also does not prove that every earlier bill date was funded; the later [cash-planning lesson](/money-and-value/cash-flow-budgets-and-liquidity-buffers) checks timing.

## Saving is not the same as moving money into a savings account

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"You transfer 400 USD from your current account to your own included savings account, without fees. What changes?"}
  options={[{"text": "The location of cash, while combined cash and net worth stay the same", "correct": true}, {"text": "New income of 400 USD and household cash higher by 400 USD"}, {"text": "Consumption costs of 400 USD and net worth lower by 400 USD"}, {"text": "Both accounts rise by 400 USD because the transfer is called saving"}]}
  explanation={"The current account falls 400 and the savings account rises 400. The two sides cancel for the household."}
/>

**Saving** means leaving some income unconsumed, carrying resources into a later period. The **stock of savings** is the accumulated amount already held. An own-account transfer changes where that stock sits. It need not create new saving during this period.

Take current cash 2000 and savings cash 1000. Transfer 400 to savings: $2000-400=1600$ in current and $1000+400=1400$ in savings. Combined cash stays $1600+1400=3000$. Neither income nor consumption occurred. The transfer can help organise a goal, but it did not generate another 400 USD.

<HouseholdTransactionEffects client:visible event="transfer" amount={400} />

Conversely, receive 100 USD of wages and use all of it to repay principal. Ending cash need not rise: the income adds 100 cash and repayment removes 100. Debt falls 100, so net worth rises 100. The **income** produced that improvement; the subsequent principal repayment exchanged cash for a lower obligation. The [BEA explanation of saving](https://www.bea.gov/help/faq/65) also distinguishes income left after outlays from the choice to acquire assets or repay debt, within its own statistical framework.

A new loan deposited in a savings account is still matched by debt. A household can also transfer money into savings while running a period deficit and borrowing elsewhere. Account names do not classify the source of funds.

### When to use it

Separate a savings allocation from the result that funds it. Check the combined accounts and debt as well as the labelled savings balance. Interest is additional income only if the account terms pay it and it is actually credited under the basis being used; a transfer itself earns no return.

## Borrowing creates an obligation and principal repayment removes it

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="You repay 150 USD of principal from existing cash with no fee. Does that movement alone reduce net worth by 150?" text="No. Cash falls 150 and the matching debt falls 150. Their difference is unchanged. Paying interest would instead be a cost in this example." />

**Borrowing** provides resources now in exchange for a repayment obligation. It can fund consumption, buy an asset or replace another debt; it does not always mean consuming future wages today. **Principal** is the borrowed amount, and **outstanding principal** is the part not yet repaid. Contract terms may separately add interest or fees, including charges added to the balance rather than paid immediately.

Start again with cash assets 3000 and debt 1500. Borrow another 1000 with no fee: cash becomes 4000 and debt 2500. Net worth is $4000-2500=1500$, unchanged. Spending the proceeds later is a separate event with its own classification.

Now use the original starting position instead and repay 150 of existing principal: cash becomes $3000-150=2850$, debt $1500-150=1350$, and net worth remains $2850-1350=1500$. Interest is different: paying 30 of interest from the original cash leaves assets 2970 and principal debt 1500, so net worth falls to 1470 in this example.

<HouseholdTransactionEffects client:visible event="principal" amount={150} />

A repayment uses cash and can reduce spending flexibility even when that principal portion is not a cost in the result. Future payments require future cash: earnings, existing savings, asset sales or new financing. A new loan used to repay an old one creates a replacement obligation; hoped-for earnings are not guaranteed cash.

### When to use it

Split a debt payment into principal, interest and fees before interpreting it. Check the outstanding amount, due dates and source of payment. A lender's promised interest is not guaranteed to be received if payment fails; the contract and the realised outcome are different questions.

## Interest and fees determine more than the headline loan amount

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"A loan has principal 1000 USD and a 20 USD fee withheld from delivery. How much cash arrives?"}
  options={[{"text": "980 USD, with principal still owed at 1000", "correct": true}, {"text": "1000 USD, because fees never reduce delivered funds"}, {"text": "1020 USD, because the fee is a bonus for the borrower"}, {"text": "980 USD, with no debt because the difference was paid"}]}
  explanation={"The stipulated fee is deducted now: 1000−20=980. That does not change the stated principal obligation."}
/>

**Interest** is an agreed charge for using borrowed resources over time. A rate is a proportion applied to a specified amount and period; 10% means 10 per 100. Some loans charge no interest, and some charge fees as well. Money held in a savings account may or may not earn interest under its terms.

### A one-year cash comparison

Borrow principal 1000 USD at **10% simple interest for exactly one year**, with one repayment at the end. Simple means the example calculates interest on that original principal, without adding interest-on-interest. If there are no fees, interest is $1000(0.10)(1)=100$ USD and the final payment is $1000+100=1100$ USD.

Now suppose the lender withholds a 20-USD fee when the loan starts while principal owed remains 1000. The cash received today is $1000-20=980$ USD; the final payment is still 1100. Total stated borrowing cost is **$100+20=120$ USD**, equal to $1100-980$ across these two dates.

The one-year cash-cost ratio is $120/980\approx12.2449\%$, measured against net proceeds rather than the 1000-USD face amount. This is the arithmetic of the specified two-date contract, not a universal legal APR or TAE formula. The [CFPB's US explanation](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-difference-between-a-loan-interest-rate-and-the-apr-en-733/) distinguishes interest from broader fee-inclusive borrowing cost. Other schedules, fee dates, compounding and legal definitions require their own calculation.

In our simple net-worth view, the fee reduces resources when the loan starts: cash rises 980 and debt rises 1000, a net change of −20. Principal repayment later reduces cash and debt; interest is a further cost. Do not count the withheld fee both as an extra later payment and as already withheld proceeds.

### When to use it

Compare actual cash received, all promised payments and their dates, not just the headline rate. Keep estimates of future income separate from contractual amounts due. Later lessons develop interest, compounding and time value; this calculation establishes which amounts belong in those formulas.
