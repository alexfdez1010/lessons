You expect to receive **€1,800** this month and pay **€1,700**. The difference is +€100. You can still run out of cash on the third day of the month if the money arrives on the twenty-fifth.

Earlier lessons explained cash movements and reconciled records of what happened. A **cash-flow budget** projects *when* money will become usable and *when* it must leave. The [CFPB's cash-flow budget tool](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/documents/10038/cfpb_creating-cash-flow-budget_tool_2021-08.pdf) carries each period's closing amount into the next opening amount. We will use dates within a month because a weekly total can also hide an earlier payment.

All amounts and timing scenarios below are invented. There is no interest, tax change or fee unless explicitly introduced. A negative projected amount represents cash that is missing under the stated plan; it does not assume a lender or bank will supply it.

## 1. A budget is a plan; a balance is an amount at a date

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An account currently contains €300 and next month's planned receipts exceed payments by €100. How much money is already there?" options={[
  { text: "€300. The extra €100 is a future planned flow", correct: true },
  { text: "€400. A forecast becomes cash immediately" },
  { text: "€100. The opening money disappears when budgeting starts" },
]} explanation="Opening cash is carried forward from the past. Expected receipts and payments have not yet happened. A forecast can change." />

A **budget** is a plan for future receipts, payments and allocations over a chosen period. A **balance** is an amount at a particular date. **Actuals** are the movements that have occurred, supported by records. Compare a forecast with actuals to discover where assumptions changed; do not rewrite history to make the plan look right.

For a consistently defined pot of money:

$$
\text{projected closing cash}=\text{opening cash}+\text{planned inflows}-\text{planned outflows}
$$

Start with €300, expect €1,800 of wages and plan €1,700 of payments. Projected net cash flow is $1{,}800-1{,}700=100$, and projected closing cash is $300+100=400$. The €300 opening amount is not another wage receipt. This preserves the stock-versus-flow distinction from the ledger lesson.

| Question | Amount in this plan | What it tells you |
|---|---:|---|
| What is available at the opening date? | €300 | Starting resources |
| What do the period's movements add? | +€100 | Planned net flow |
| What remains at the closing date? | €400 | Opening amount plus that flow |

### When to use the distinction

Write down the period, currency and account boundary before adding numbers. A monthly estimate is useful for totals; a bank statement verifies posted history. Neither alone establishes that every future bill can be paid on time. [Canada's financial consumer agency](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/make-budget.html) describes budgeting as a plan to compare with what you actually spend.

## 2. Order the movements before judging the total

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Rent of €1,200 is due on day 3, but €1,800 of wages becomes usable on day 25. Does a positive month-end forecast prove rent can be paid on time?" options={[
  { text: "No. Check the resources available before day 3", correct: true },
  { text: "Yes. A monthly surplus funds every earlier date" },
  { text: "Yes. Banks must advance every expected salary" },
]} explanation="A future receipt does not fund an earlier payment unless an additional resource or agreed arrangement bridges the dates." />

At each movement $t$, update the previous amount:

$$
B_t=B_{t-1}+I_t-O_t
$$

$B_t$ is projected cash after that movement; $I_t$ is its inflow and $O_t$ its outflow. This is a cash identity, not an assumption that a negative payment will be accepted.

Our €1,700 plan consists of €1,200 rent on day 3, €400 of other bills on day 10 and €100 of further spending on day 28. Wages of €1,800 arrive on day 25.

| Day | Movement | Arithmetic | Projected cash |
|---|---|---|---:|
| 0 | Opening own cash | Carried forward | €300 |
| 3 | Pay rent | $300-1{,}200$ | −€900 |
| 10 | Pay bills | $-900-400$ | −€1,300 |
| 25 | Receive wages | $-1{,}300+1{,}800$ | €500 |
| 28 | Further spending | $500-100$ | €400 |

The lowest amount, including the opening amount, is −€1,300. **An additional €1,300 of accessible existing cash at the start** would shift the entire path up to a minimum of zero. Total opening cash would then be $300+1{,}300=1{,}600$, not €1,300. That is a calculated requirement for this exact path, not a general emergency-fund target.

<CashBudgetTimeline client:visible />

Move payday to day 2: closing cash remains €400, but the lowest amount becomes €300. Now return to day 25 and move only the rent payment to day 26. The day-10 bills still leave $300-400=-100$ before wages; one changed due date does not solve every gap.

### Same-day order also matters

If wages and rent share day 3, the simulator assumes **payments occur before income**. Its first step can still be $300-1{,}200=-900$. If a provider instead makes wages usable first, the path differs. Check actual posting and availability rules, weekends and cut-off times; do not infer intraday funding from a daily total. The displayed order is an explicit conservative scenario, not a universal bank rule.

## 3. Choose the pot: income, borrowing and transfers differ

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="You move €100 from your current account to your own immediately accessible savings account. Has the household earned €100?" options={[
  { text: "No. The transfer changes location, not household income", correct: true },
  { text: "Yes. Every receipt in one account is income" },
  { text: "No. It is necessarily a new loan" },
]} explanation="The receiving account gains €100 and the sending account loses €100. Both sides belong to the same household boundary." />

An **inflow** brings cash into the pot you are tracking. **Income** describes its source, such as wages; not every inflow is income. **Borrowing** supplies cash while creating a debt. **Loan principal repayment** uses cash while reducing debt; interest is a cost. A **transfer** between your own accounts changes one account's balance without creating household income.

Extend the original plan with a €200 loan receipt and €100 principal repayment plus €5 interest. Assume all occur inside the period, no other debt changes, and keep the €300 opening amount.

| Movement category | Cash in | Cash out |
|---|---:|---:|
| Wages | €1,800 | — |
| New borrowing | €200 | — |
| Original spending | — | €1,700 |
| Principal repayment | — | €100 |
| Interest | — | €5 |
| Total | €2,000 | €1,805 |

Net cash flow is $2{,}000-1{,}805=195$, giving $300+195=495$ closing cash. Income less costs is $1{,}800-(1{,}700+5)=95$; the remaining €100 cash increase comes from net new debt, $200-100=100$. Cash rising by €195 does not mean €195 was earned or saved from income.

### Consolidate without counting twice

For a single current account, a €100 transfer to savings is an outflow. For the household's combined immediately accessible cash, the −€100 and +€100 cancel. If savings is locked until a later date, it is outside the *currently usable* pot until released. Name that boundary rather than pretending the same money is available in both places.

## 4. Available at the bank does not mean uncommitted in your plan

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A provider displays €820 available after deducting an €80 hold from €900 posted. Should you subtract that same hold again when starting from €820?" options={[
  { text: "No. It is already included in that starting amount", correct: true },
  { text: "Yes. Every pending item must always be deducted twice" },
  { text: "No. A hold can never affect availability" },
]} explanation="Use the provider's stated calculation consistently. Starting from posted cash and starting from available cash require different adjustments." />

A **posted balance** reflects completed entries. An **available balance** applies the provider's rules about what can currently be used, including relevant holds and restrictions. Those rules vary. The [OCC's official explanation](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2023/bulletin-2023-12.html) distinguishes posted funds from holds; [U.S. Bank's own explanation](https://www.usbank.com/customer-service/knowledge-base/KB0180641.html) also lists future payments that may not yet be reflected. These are scoped examples, not identical rules for every bank.

Suppose the account's stated rule gives $900-80=820$ available, and rent of €600 is due tomorrow but is **not** included in that hold or any other deduction. Cash left after reserving for that rent is $820-600=220$. This €220 is a **planning remainder**, not a bank-provided balance or proof that all other bills are covered.

| Calculation | Result | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Posted €900 − hold €80 | €820 | Provider's available funds in this example |
| Available €820 − separate unpaid rent €600 | €220 | Remainder after one known commitment |
| Available €820 − the same hold €80 again | €740 | Incorrect double deduction |

When the €80 purchase posts and the corresponding hold is removed, posted cash becomes €820 and the hold becomes zero. With no other changes, available cash remains €820: the same purchase has not suddenly used another €80. Mark items already allowed for in the opening amount, then forecast each remaining movement once.

### When to use this check

Reconcile today's starting resources before projecting tomorrow. Do not add an unused credit limit to your own cash: it is conditional borrowing capacity, with repayment terms. A positive app balance also cannot tell you about every unpresented payment or future obligation.

  { text: "€450, because a planned bill does not matter" },
]} explanation="450 − 180 = 270. This is a planning remainder after the stated bill, not a guarantee that all later obligations fit." />

## 5. Net worth and liquidity answer different questions

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="You own a car estimated at €6,000, have €300 cash and owe €1,000. Does positive net worth guarantee you can pay €1,200 rent in two days?" options={[
  { text: "No. The car may not be sold and paid for in time", correct: true },
  { text: "Yes. Every asset is immediately spendable at its estimated value" },
  { text: "No. Positive net worth means every payment must fail" },
]} explanation="Net worth compares asset values with debts. Paying a dated bill requires usable funds by that date." />

An **asset** is something you own with economic value. A **liability** is something you owe. **Net worth** is assets minus liabilities. **Liquidity** is the ability to meet a payment using resources that can be made usable in time, with attention to costs and uncertainty. An asset can contribute to wealth while being difficult to turn into timely cash.

| Item at the starting date | Estimated value | Assumed cash usable before rent |
|---|---:|---:|
| Own cash | €300 | €300 |
| Car, with no sale arranged | €6,000 | €0 |
| Debt outstanding | −€1,000 | Not a source of cash |
| Net worth | €5,300 | Not a cash balance |

Net worth is $300+6{,}000-1{,}000=5{,}300$. With no incoming cash or car-sale proceeds before rent, the payment gap is $1{,}200-300=900$. A possible later sale is not an already completed deposit.

### When to use both measures

Use net worth to describe a financial position and a dated cash plan to test payment capacity. An additional €500 in unrestricted savings that can actually arrive before rent would raise usable cash to €800, leaving €400 still missing. Do not count the transfer as new income or count the same €500 in two accounts.

## 6. A buffer belongs to a scenario and a review process

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A €1,300 reserve fills the earlier base-case gap. Is it guaranteed to cover a later payday and a new €600 bill as well?" options={[
  { text: "No. Recalculate the combined path", correct: true },
  { text: "Yes. A base-case minimum is a universal safe reserve" },
  { text: "Yes. Unexpected payments cannot change cash timing" },
]} explanation="The original €1,300 came from one dated path. New amounts and dates can change its minimum." />

A **liquidity buffer** is accessible money held to absorb timing gaps or adverse cash movements. An **emergency fund** is money set aside for unplanned expenses or financial emergencies. Known annual insurance or tax bills deserve explicit budget entries; an average monthly allocation does not change their actual due date. [MoneyHelper's irregular-income guide](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/everyday-money/budgeting/how-to-budget-for-an-irregular-income) discusses variable earnings and high-cost months. The [CFPB emergency-fund guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/) says the amount needed depends on the person's situation.

For the original path with **no extra reserve yet**, let $B_t^{(0)}$ be each projected amount including the opening cash. The minimum extra amount that keeps this particular path nonnegative is

$$
R_{\text{scenario}}=\max\left(0,-\min_t B_t^{(0)}\right).
$$

This minimum only covers the specified horizon and sequence. It is not a probability of safety, and it adds no allowance for omitted bills or model error.

| Scenario, original €300 opening cash | Lowest cash with no extra reserve | Required extra starting reserve |
|---|---:|---:|
| Base: payday 25, original bills | −€1,300 | €1,300 |
| Payday delayed to 30 | −€1,400 | €1,400 |
| Extra €600 paid on day 12 | −€1,900 | €1,900 |
| Both delay to 30 and extra payment | −€2,000 | €2,000 |

For the combined case, before payday all €1,700 of original bills and the €600 extra bill have fallen due: $300-1{,}700-600=-2{,}000$. Adding the old €1,300 reserve leaves a €700 gap. An extra reserve of €2,000 in total makes this path's minimum zero; its closing cash is $300+2{,}000+1{,}800-1{,}700-600=1{,}800$.

### Make the assumptions reviewable

A useful plan records expected dates and confidence, essential and adjustable payments, known irregular bills, access restrictions and the next review date. For irregular income, test a lower receipt or delay rather than treating the best month as guaranteed. The [CFPB's guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/) also discusses asking whether bill dates can be adjusted; that requires agreement, not silently moving a contractual payment in a spreadsheet.

If a scenario fails, quantify what would have to change: an earlier usable receipt, genuinely accessible savings, an agreed date, reduced spending or financing with explicit costs and repayment. Then test later periods too. A finite reserve cannot repair a recurring spending shortfall forever. Retain some uncertainty in the conclusion: covering these four invented paths does not cover every possible future.
