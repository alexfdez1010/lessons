Your bank reports €920. Your own register says €925. A five-euro gap might be a fee you missed, an entry the bank got wrong or two records covering different dates. Changing your total until the numbers agree would hide the clue.

The previous lesson explained how a ledger produces a balance. Now use a statement, your payment records and a few checks to establish **why the balances agree or differ**. Every account and transaction below is invented, and all amounts are in euros.

## Fix the account and the period before adding

A **bank statement** records activity for a particular account over a stated period. An **opening balance** is the amount at the start; a **closing balance** is the amount at the end. A **posted entry** has been recorded in the account ledger. In this lesson, the statement includes only posted entries through its closing date.

Check the account identifier, currency, period and whether the first balance is brought forward from the previous statement. An opening balance is a starting stock of money, not another salary payment. Banco de España's [guide to consulting account activity](https://clientebancario.bde.es/pcb/es/blog/ayuda-a-tus-mayores-con-sus-finanzas--consulta-de-movimientos-bancarios.html) describes the movement details you may find in online banking.

### Rebuild one complete statement

Start with €500. These are the only posted movements in the period:

| Date | Entry | Money in | Money out | Balance after entry |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 November | Opening balance | | | €500 |
| 3 November | Salary | €1,800 | | €2,300 |
| 5 November | Rent | | €1,200 | €1,100 |
| 9 November | Groceries | | €140 | €960 |
| 14 November | Purchase refund | €60 | | €1,020 |
| 20 November | Electricity | | €95 | €925 |

$$500+1{,}800-1{,}200-140+60-95=925$$

Money in totals €1,800 + €60 = **€1,860**. Money out totals €1,200 + €140 + €95 = **€1,435**. Therefore closing balance = €500 + €1,860 − €1,435 = **€925**. The account gained €425 in the period; it did not earn €925 of new income.

### When to use this check

Rebuilding the total catches arithmetic errors, missing rows in an export and a mistaken opening figure. It does not establish whether every charge was legitimate. A statement can add up perfectly and still contain a duplicate purchase.

## A pending hold is not another posted purchase

A **pending transaction** has not yet been posted in the ledger used for this comparison. An **authorisation hold** reserves an amount against a card transaction. The final charge can differ from the hold, and a hold may be released without a charge. **Available balance** is the amount the provider currently presents as usable under its rules; it can differ from the posted balance.

There is no universal “posted minus all pending equals available” formula. Providers may treat deposits, holds, overdraft facilities and scheduled payments differently. The [OCC explains how holds can affect available balances](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2023/bulletin-2023-12.html). As a concrete provider example, [U.S. Bank lists items that may be absent from its available balance](https://www.usbank.com/customer-service/knowledge-base/KB0180641.html), including some future payments and delayed card transactions.

### Work through one explicitly defined account

Suppose our fictional account has **no overdraft facility, unavailable deposits or other restrictions**. Its rule is to deduct the full pending card hold from the posted balance. Start after the earlier €925 statement, with no new posted entries:

| Stage | Posted balance | Active hold | Available under this example’s rule |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Before hotel authorisation | €925 | €0 | €925 |
| Hotel reserves €80 | €925 | €80 | €845 |
| Final €65 charge posts and the whole hold is released | €860 | €0 | €860 |

At authorisation, €925 − €80 = **€845 available**, while the posted balance stays €925. At completion, the bank posts one €65 payment: €925 − €65 = **€860**. Releasing the €80 hold removes a reservation; it is not €80 of income. Subtracting both €80 and €65 from the posted balance would count the same purchase twice.

### When to use this distinction

Use the statement's posted closing balance for period reconciliation. Use the provider's availability information when preparing a payment, and separately allow for known bills that the bank has not yet reflected. A high available balance does not mean every future commitment is already covered. A disappeared pending item alone does not prove that the merchant will never submit a final charge.

**Reconciliation** means comparing independent records, finding unmatched items and explaining the differences. Your own register is useful because it can be checked against receipts and confirmations rather than copied blindly from the statement. Think of matching luggage tags: the final number of bags is a check, but it does not tell you whether you have your bags.

For this separate case, return to the original posted records ending at €925; the hotel example is not included. Your personal register contains the opening balance and five movements shown earlier, but the bank's statement ends at **€780**. Compare the rows:

| Unmatched item | Evidence checked | Action |
|---|---|---|
| A €5 account fee appears only on the statement | The account agreement and fee notice confirm it is valid | Add the omitted fee to your personal register |
| A second €140 grocery debit appears only on the statement | The receipt and payment references are checked; the bank confirms a duplicate | Request correction and track the reversal |

First correct your own omission: €925 − €5 = **€920**. Then reconcile the statement: it contains the original €925 movements, the €5 fee and an extra €140 debit, so €925 − €5 − €140 = **€780**. The difference from your corrected register is €920 − €780 = **€140**, explained by the confirmed duplicate.

| Record | Before resolution | Adjustment | Comparable result |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Personal register | €925 | −€5 valid fee omitted | €920 |
| Bank statement | €780 | +€140 once the bank's reversal posts | €920 |

Until the reversal actually posts, the bank balance is still €780. Keep the €140 as an unresolved reconciling item, not imaginary spendable cash. If the refund arrives next month, preserve this month's issued statement and record the later correction at its actual posting date.

### A repeatable reconciliation

1. Fix the account and cutoff; bring forward the agreed opening balance.
2. Match each posted entry to a receipt, payment confirmation or other evidence, including its amount and reference.
3. List unmatched items. Separate timing differences, omissions in your register and suspected bank or merchant errors.
4. Correct your own records only when supported. Ask the provider about disputed items and keep the case reference.
5. Verify any later correction when it posts. Matching totals alone do not prove every row is correct.

If you spot a potentially unauthorised transaction, contact the provider promptly through a verified channel and retain the evidence. Reporting rules and protections depend on the jurisdiction and payment method. The [CFPB's guidance on missing money and unauthorised transactions](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-get-my-money-back-after-i-discover-an-unauthorized-transaction-or-money-missing-from-my-bank-account-en-1017/) describes the US case; do not treat its time limits as worldwide rules.

### When to use reconciliation

Use it when totals differ and when they agree. A missing €20 inflow and a missing €20 outflow can cancel in the total while leaving the record incomplete. The goal is an explained record, not a decorative green tick beside an unexplained number.

## Your own transfers move cash without creating income

A **transfer between your own accounts** changes where the money is recorded. It is an outflow from one account and an inflow to the other, but not new household income. First reconcile each account; then remove the internal movement when describing the combined cash flow.

Start from the corrected €920 current-account balance and €300 in savings. Assume the transfer has posted on both sides, with no fees, interest or other movements:

| Record | Current account | Savings account | Combined cash |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Before transfer | €920 | €300 | €1,220 |
| Transfer | −€200 | +€200 | €0 change |
| After transfer | €720 | €500 | €1,220 |

€920 − €200 = **€720** in the current account; €300 + €200 = **€500** in savings. Combined cash is €720 + €500 = **€1,220**, unchanged from €920 + €300. Calling the savings inflow “income” would overstate what you received from outside the household.

The earlier €60 purchase refund is also not another salary payment: it returns money from a purchase. In a personal budget, match it to the original expense category so you do not treat a reversal as newly earned pay. The bank statement still correctly records €60 coming in. The cash-entry view and the income-category view answer different questions.

### When to use this distinction

Use account-level in/out totals to reconcile that account, and a combined view to assess household saving or spending. Track transfers still in transit before concluding that money is missing, and account for any actual transfer fee separately. An account can rise because of your own transfer, a refund or borrowing without the same amount becoming new income.
