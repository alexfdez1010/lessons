You deposit \$100 in cash and the app adds \$100 to your balance. How could you check that balance after a salary payment, rent and groceries? Start by distinguishing the money the bank owes you from the record used to calculate it.

## Your balance is a claim

A **deposit** is money credited to a bank account. On an **on-demand account**, you can instruct the bank to pay or transfer it subject to the account’s terms. A term deposit can have different withdrawal conditions.

From your perspective, the deposit is an **asset**: a resource or claim you own. From the bank’s perspective, it is a **liability**: an amount it owes. The same \$100 is your claim and the bank’s obligation.

<Callout variant="info" title="The limit of the storage analogy">
A bank balance is not a receipt for particular notes kept aside for you. Nor is lending simply a process of passing those notes to another customer: making a bank loan can create a new deposit alongside the borrower’s debt. The [Bank of England explains that accounting](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/explainers/how-is-money-created). For this lesson, keep the two perspectives clear: your deposit is your asset and the bank’s liability.
</Callout>

### When it matters

If your app shows \$1,742.18, that is the amount of the recorded claim. To check whether it is correct, reconstruct the deposits, withdrawals and other entries that produced it.

## A Ledger: The Record of Who Has What

If your balance is just a promise, something has to keep score. That something is a **ledger**.

A **ledger** is simply a record of transactions — a running list of money coming in and money going out, with the resulting balance after each one. That's it. No magic, no blockchain (yet), no robes. A ledger is a list with a total that keeps updating.

<Callout variant="info" title="The scoreboard analogy">
A ledger is a **scoreboard**. Every play (transaction) nudges the score (balance) up or down, and the current total is always sitting there for everyone to read. Your bank statement is exactly this: a scoreboard for one account. So was the dog-eared *checkbook register* your grandparents kept in the kitchen drawer — same idea, fewer pixels.
</Callout>

The key insight is that the ledger doesn't just store the *current* balance — it stores the *history* of how you got there. That history is what lets anyone reconstruct, check, or dispute the total. Lose the history and "trust me, your balance is \$1,742.18" is all you've got.

## Debit, Credit, and the Running Balance

Here's the intuitive version, and we'll start here on purpose: on a personal account, **money in raises the balance, money out lowers it**, and the **running balance** is the total *after each entry*. Read the rows top to bottom, add the ins, subtract the outs, and the last number is where you stand.

Hit play and watch the ledger post one entry at a time. Notice the running balance recompute on every single row — that recomputation *is* the ledger doing its one job.

### Worked example

Walk it by hand so the animation isn't doing all the thinking for you. Start the month at **\$500**:

| Date  | Description | In (credit) | Out (debit) | Balance |
|-------|-------------|------------:|------------:|--------:|
| Jun 1 | Opening     |             |             |  500.00 |
| Jun 3 | Paycheck    |    1,200.00 |             | 1,700.00 |
| Jun 5 | Rent        |             |      900.00 |  800.00 |
| Jun 9 | Groceries   |             |      120.00 |  680.00 |

Paycheck adds: $500 + 1{,}200 = 1{,}700$. Rent subtracts: $1{,}700 - 900 = 800$. Groceries subtract: $800 - 120 = 680$. End the month at **\$680**. Every balance is just *the previous balance, plus this row's in, minus this row's out.*

<Reveal summary="Why does a deposit appear as a credit on the bank statement?">
Your deposit increases the bank’s liability to you, and liabilities increase with credits. In your own books, the same deposit is an asset; increasing that asset uses a debit. The entry depends on whose books you read and which type of account changes.
</Reveal>

<Callout variant="warning" title="Misconception: 'debit always means money out'">
Only on *your* informal mental model. In formal accounting, **debit** literally means *the left-hand entry* and **credit** the *right-hand entry* — directions in a book, not "out" and "in." Whether a debit raises or lowers a balance depends on the *type* of account. We're about to see why, so don't memorise "debit = bad" — it'll betray you the moment you read a real set of books.
</Callout>

## Double-entry and the perspective of the books

**Double-entry bookkeeping** records a transaction in at least two accounts within one set of books. Total **debits** must equal total **credits**. Debits and credits are accounting sides, not universal labels for money leaving and arriving.

### Worked example

You keep your own accounts and pay **\$900 rent** from your bank account:

| Account in your books | Debit | Credit | Effect |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Rent expense | \$900 | | Records the cost of rent |
| Bank deposit asset | | \$900 | Reduces the bank balance you own |

Total debits = \$900; total credits = \$900. Both entries are in **your** books. Your landlord has a separate set of books. If you and the landlord use the same bank, that bank can also record the transfer by reducing its deposit liability to you and increasing its liability to the landlord; that is a different viewpoint.

A balanced entry does not prove that it is correct. Recording a \$900 payment as \$90 on **both** sides still balances. The balance check detects unequal debits and credits; receipts, statements and reconciliation are needed to find other errors. Bank lending can also increase assets and liabilities together while the books remain balanced, so double-entry is not a rule that money can never be created.

### When it matters

Always name whose books you are reading and the type of account. That lets you distinguish a customer’s bank asset from the bank’s liability to that customer.

## From Ledger to Blockchain

A bank maintains an institutional ledger and gives customers statements of their accounts. You can compare those statements with payment records and query discrepancies.

A **public blockchain** distributes a transaction record among participants who validate updates under shared rules. It uses a different coordination and trust model; it is not simply the bank’s double-entry accounts copied to more machines. Some blockchains track account balances and others track unspent outputs, a distinction taught in Crypto Basics.

<Callout variant="info" title="Where this is heading">
The **crypto-basics** course uses the ledger idea to explain a shared transaction record. Carry forward the distinction between a record, the rights it represents and the rules used to validate changes. A blockchain need not use the same accounting structure as a bank.
</Callout>
