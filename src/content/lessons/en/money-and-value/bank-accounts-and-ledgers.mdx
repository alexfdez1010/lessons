You deposit \$100 in cash and the app adds \$100 to your balance. How could you check that balance after a salary payment, rent and groceries? Start by distinguishing the money the bank owes you from the record used to calculate it.

## Your balance is a claim

A **deposit** is money credited to a bank account. On an **on-demand account**, you can instruct the bank to pay or transfer it subject to the account’s terms. A term deposit can have different withdrawal conditions.

From your perspective, the deposit is an **asset**: a resource or claim you own. From the bank’s perspective, it is a **liability**: an amount it owes. The same \$100 is your claim and the bank’s obligation.

<Callout variant="info" title="The limit of the storage analogy">
A bank balance is not a receipt for particular notes kept aside for you. Nor is lending simply a process of passing those notes to another customer: making a bank loan can create a new deposit alongside the borrower’s debt. The [Bank of England explains that accounting](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/explainers/how-is-money-created). For this lesson, keep the two perspectives clear: your deposit is your asset and the bank’s liability.
</Callout>

### When it matters

If your app shows \$1,742.18, that is the amount of the recorded claim. To check whether it is correct, reconstruct the deposits, withdrawals and other entries that produced it.

## A ledger records the account's history

A **ledger** records entries in accounts. We will start with a **bank-account register**: a dated list of money coming in and going out, together with the balance after each entry. It shows one account's history; it is not, by itself, a complete double-entry accounting system.

The register works like a scoreboard with a record of every change. The current score alone cannot show whether an entry was omitted or counted twice. Keeping the entries lets you reconstruct the total and compare it with payment evidence.

For the independent example below, use **one account, US dollars and the period from 1 to 9 June**. The account starts with 500 already in it. This **opening balance** is an amount held at the start. Salary and payments during the period are **flows**: they change that amount. The **closing balance** is what remains at the cutoff.

## Rebuild the running balance from the same entries

For this positive bank-account balance, **money in raises the balance and money out lowers it**. The **running balance** is the total after each entry. Keep the opening amount once, then add the period's inflows and subtract its outflows.

### Trace the same account by hand

All amounts in the table are US dollars. There are no other entries in this example period:

| Date | Entry | Money in during the period | Money out during the period | Balance |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Jun 1 | Opening balance carried forward | | | 500.00 |
| Jun 3 | Paycheck | 1,200.00 | | 1,700.00 |
| Jun 5 | Rent | | 900.00 | 800.00 |
| Jun 9 | Groceries | | 120.00 | 680.00 |

1. **Bring forward 500.** This is the account's starting amount, not an extra receipt in the period.
2. **Post the paycheck:** $500+1{,}200=1{,}700$.
3. **Post the rent:** $1{,}700-900=800$.
4. **Post the groceries:** $800-120=680$.

Period inflows are **1,200**, not 1,700: the opening 500 was already there. Outflows are $900+120=1{,}020$, so the period increases the balance by $1{,}200-1{,}020=180$. Add that change to the opening balance:

$$500+180=680$$

The **closing balance on 9 June is 680**. The balance increased by 180; neither the opening 500 nor the closing 680 is the paycheck amount.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Debit and credit need a viewpoint">
In formal accounting, **debit** names the left-hand side and **credit** the right-hand side. Their effect depends on the account type. An increase in your bank-deposit asset is a debit in your books; an increase in the bank's deposit liability is a credit in the bank's books. The register's “Money in” and “Money out” columns do not replace those accounting rules.
</Callout>

## Double-entry and the perspective of the books

**Double-entry bookkeeping** records a transaction in at least two accounts within one set of books. Total **debits** must equal total **credits**. The running register above shows only the bank account; double-entry also records the corresponding change in another account. The [Open University demonstrates the rules for assets and liabilities](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=31734&section=3.1).

### Worked example

Return to the **900 rent payment on 5 June**. In your own books, its two entries are:

| Account in your books | Debit | Credit | Effect |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Rent expense | \$900 | | Records the cost of rent |
| Bank deposit asset | | \$900 | Reduces the bank balance you own |

Total debits = \$900; total credits = \$900. Both entries are in **your** books. Your landlord has a separate set of books. If you and the landlord use the same bank, that bank can also record the transfer by reducing its deposit liability to you and increasing its liability to the landlord; that is a different viewpoint.

A balanced entry does not prove that it is correct. Recording the 900 rent payment as 90 on **both** sides still balances. The balance check detects unequal debits and credits; receipts, statements and reconciliation are needed to find other errors. Bank lending can also increase assets and liabilities together while the books remain balanced, so double-entry is not a rule that money can never be created.

### When it matters

Always name whose books you are reading and the type of account. That lets you distinguish a customer’s bank asset from the bank’s liability to that customer.

## From Ledger to Blockchain

A bank maintains an institutional ledger and gives customers statements of their accounts. You can compare those statements with payment records and query discrepancies.

A **public blockchain** distributes a transaction record among participants who validate updates under shared rules. It uses a different coordination and trust model; it is not simply the bank’s double-entry accounts copied to more machines. Some blockchains track account balances and others track unspent outputs, a distinction taught in Crypto Basics.

<Callout variant="info" title="Where this is heading">
The **crypto-basics** course uses the ledger idea to explain a shared transaction record. Carry forward the distinction between a record, the rights it represents and the rules used to validate changes. A blockchain need not use the same accounting structure as a bank.
</Callout>
