You estimate a success probability of 62.5%. Does that make the chance of four future failures exactly $0.375^4$? It does if you fix that probability and assume independent trials. It need not if the same unknown probability is shared by the whole block.

The difference is small enough to hide in a spreadsheet and large enough to change a risk decision. We will derive it, using an invented binary-outcome model. A **success** is coded 1 and a **failure** 0; neither label by itself specifies a trading profit. The probability is assumed stable and common across the stated trials. Market regimes, unequal opportunities and changing loss sizes are outside this model.

## Move from a parameter posterior to future outcomes

A posterior describes uncertainty about a model parameter after observing data. A **posterior predictive distribution** describes new outcomes after averaging their conditional probabilities over that posterior. It is a forecast conditional on the model and data, not a distribution of the parameter itself.

Let $D$ be observed data, $p$ the unknown success probability and $\widetilde{Y}$ a future outcome or whole future block. If old and future observations are independent conditional on the shared parameter,

$$\Pr(\widetilde{Y}\in A\mid D)=\int_0^1\Pr(\widetilde{Y}\in A\mid p)\,\pi(p\mid D)\,dp$$

Here $\pi(p\mid D)$ is the posterior density, and $A$ is the event you want to predict. The integral is a weighted average of possible data-generating probabilities. A **plug-in forecast** instead selects one parameter value, often the posterior mean $\bar p=E[p\mid D]$, and uses $\Pr(\widetilde{Y}\in A\mid\bar p)$. Averaging a nonlinear event probability need not equal evaluating it at an average parameter. [Stan’s primary guide derives the predictive mixture and its two sources of uncertainty](https://mc-stan.org/docs/stan-users-guide/posterior-prediction.html).

### Start from an explicit update

The [conjugate-prior lesson](/bayesian-finance/conjugate-priors-and-updating) teaches the count update. Take a Beta(2,2) prior and observe three successes and one failure under the conditional Bernoulli model:

$$p\mid D\sim\operatorname{Beta}(2+3,2+1)=\operatorname{Beta}(5,3)$$

Its mean is $5/(5+3)=5/8=0.625$ and its concentration, the sum of its shape parameters, is 8. Its variance is

$$\operatorname{Var}(p\mid D)=\frac{5(3)}{8^2(9)}=\frac{15}{576}\approx0.0260417$$

The posterior is about an unknown probability. A future result still has its own random variation even if that probability were known. A credible interval for p does not contain a specified percentage of future 0/1 outcomes. [Stanford’s lecture derives the conditional Bernoulli likelihood and Beta update](https://web.stanford.edu/class/archive/stats/stats200/stats200.1172/Lecture20.pdf).

### When to use it

Name the future variable and event before integrating: one success, a count, an ordered prefix and a monetary loss are different targets. Keep both parameter uncertainty and new-outcome randomness when the decision depends on the full forecast. A posterior mean alone is sufficient for some events, as the next section shows.

For one future indicator Y, the success probability is linear in p:

$$\Pr(Y=1\mid D)=E[p\mid D]=\frac58,\qquad\Pr(Y=0\mid D)=\frac38$$

Thus the entire one-step predictive distribution is Bernoulli(5/8), exactly the same as the fixed-mean plug-in forecast. Its variance is $(5/8)(3/8)=15/64=0.234375$. Parameter uncertainty does **not** increase this one-step Bernoulli variance above that plug-in comparison.

The law of total variance explains the apparent puzzle:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid D)=E[p(1-p)\mid D]+\operatorname{Var}(p\mid D)$$

$$=\frac{15}{72}+\frac{15}{576}=\frac{120+15}{576}=\frac{15}{64}$$

The conditional-outcome part is about 0.208333 and the parameter part about 0.026042. Adding the latter to the plug-in variance would count some uncertainty twice. The plug-in variance is not the first term of that decomposition.

## A shared unknown probability creates predictive dependence

<MCQ
  client:visible

    "correct": false

  {

    "correct": false
  }

  explanation={"The unknown common probability is a shared source of predictive uncertainty."}

Assume two future indicators $Y_1,Y_2$ are independent **conditional on p**. Their conditional covariance is zero. After averaging over the same unknown p, they need not be independent. The law of total covariance gives

$$\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2\mid D)=E[0\mid D]+\operatorname{Cov}(p,p\mid D)=\operatorname{Var}(p\mid D)$$

For Beta(5,3), that covariance is $15/576$, and each marginal variance is $15/64$. Therefore

$$\operatorname{Corr}(Y_1,Y_2\mid D)=\frac{15/576}{15/64}=\frac19$$

A hypothetical failure changes what you would infer about the shared probability. After the first future failure, the updated shapes would be Beta(5,4), so the next failure probability is $4/9$, greater than the initial $3/8$. A success would instead produce Beta(6,3), making the next success probability $6/9$.

This is dependence under the predictive distribution, not a claim that one result physically causes the next. Under the assumed model p stays fixed; the information about it changes. With constant shared p, ordering does not affect the probability of a particular string with the same numbers of successes and failures. This property is **exchangeability**, which is weaker than independence.

## Predict the future count with the beta-binomial

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Both count forecasts have expected successes 2.5. Must they have the same variance?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "No; covariance can change the count variance",
    "correct": true
  },
  {
    "text": "Yes; a mean uniquely determines a count law",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "Yes; four Bernoulli marginals always imply binomial counts",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"Summing marginal variances omits the cross-covariance terms unless the indicators are independent."}
  pretest
/>


Let $K=Y_1+\cdots+Y_m$ count successes in m future trials. Conditional on p, K is binomial. With a Beta(a,b) posterior and one p shared by the block, averaging gives the **beta-binomial**:

$$\Pr(K=k\mid D)=\binom mk\frac{B(a+k,b+m-k)}{B(a,b)},\quad k=0,\ldots,m$$

The beta function is $B(a,b)=\int_0^1p^{a-1}(1-p)^{b-1}dp$. It normalizes the Beta density. The factor $\binom mk=m!/[k!(m-k)!]$ counts different orders with k successes. [SciPy’s official beta-binomial reference states this mass function](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.betabinom.html).

For a=5, b=3 and m=4, define the rising product $(a)_j=a(a+1)\cdots(a+j-1)$, with $(a)_0=1$. Then

$$\Pr(K=k\mid D)=\binom4k\frac{(5)_k(3)_{4-k}}{(8)_4},\qquad(8)_4=8(9)(10)(11)=7920$$

For exactly two successes, the numerator is $6[5(6)][3(4)]=2160$, giving $2160/7920=3/11\approx0.272727$. The fixed-mean binomial probability is $6(5/8)^2(3/8)^2=1350/4096\approx0.329590$.

Both distributions have mean $m\bar p=4(5/8)=2.5$. Their variances differ because the predictive indicators covary:

$$\operatorname{Var}(K\mid D)=m\bar p(1-\bar p)+m(m-1)\operatorname{Var}(p\mid D)$$

$$=4\frac{15}{64}+4(3)\frac{15}{576}=\frac{15}{16}+\frac5{16}=\frac54=1.25$$

The plug-in binomial variance is $15/16=0.9375$. More generally, if s=a+b, the variance ratio is $(s+m)/(s+1)$, equal to one at m=1. Here it is 12/9. Both endpoint counts become more likely, but middle counts can become less likely; no rule says every event probability increases.

### When to use it

Use the count distribution when order does not affect the event or payoff. Increasing concentration at the same mean makes the hypothetical posterior tighter: Beta(50,30) has the same 5/8 mean, pair correlation 1/81 and four-count variance $\frac{15}{16}(1+3/81)\approx0.972222$. This slider comparison constructs a different posterior; it is not evidence that data have arrived or that the chosen model is right.

### Give the event a defined monetary meaning

Suppose each of four invented exposures loses EUR 100 on failure and zero on success, with no offsets, recovery or compounding. Total loss is $100(4-K)$ EUR, expected loss is $100(4-2.5)=150$ EUR in both models, and a loss of EUR 400 occurs exactly when all four fail. The shared-parameter model assigns that loss probability 1/22; the plug-in model assigns 81/4096.

A hypothetical planning rule that requires the probability of a EUR 400 loss to stay below 3% would pass under the plug-in calculation and fail under this predictive model. That illustrates model sensitivity; it is not a recommendation or a calibrated market forecast. Expected loss alone would miss the difference.

### When to use it

Write a path event as an indicator before valuing it. “The next four fail” differs from “at least one run of four occurs anywhere in the next twenty.” The latter requires accounting for overlapping runs, for example with a state recursion or full-path simulation. A count alone also does not identify drawdown when payoff order and position sizing matter.

## Keep the shared parameter inside each simulated path

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Which simulation preserves a single unknown probability shared by four future trials?"}

For this finite Beta example, the integral and products are exact. For a more complex model, posterior predictive simulation follows two nested steps. For each independently generated replicate r:

1. Draw one $p^{(r)}$ from the posterior.
2. Hold it fixed while generating all m conditional Bernoulli outcomes of that path.
3. Calculate the count, loss or full-path event from those outcomes.
4. Repeat with a new posterior draw for the next replicate.

Drawing a fresh independent p for every future trial describes a different joint model. For this example it removes the shared-parameter covariance, producing independent Bernoulli outcomes with success probability 5/8. Holding p fixed at 5/8 does the same for the block distribution. Drawing p once per **replicate path** retains both layers of uncertainty.

For M independent replicate paths, estimate an event probability by

$$\widehat q=\frac1M\sum_{r=1}^M\mathbf1\{\text{event on path }r\}$$

At M=22,000 and exact q=1/22, the expected event count is 1,000. The model-based independent-replicate standard error is $\sqrt{q(1-q)/M}\approx0.00140435$, about 0.1404 percentage points. More simulation reduces this computational sampling error; it does not repair an unsuitable Bernoulli model. If parameter draws come from autocorrelated MCMC, M is not automatically the number of independent event estimates. [Stan explains predictive generation and event-probability estimation](https://mc-stan.org/docs/stan-users-guide/posterior-prediction.html).

### When to use it

Test model implications as well as code. Compare replicated counts, dispersion and path patterns with relevant data; examine prior sensitivity, changing rates, dependence left in residuals and out-of-sample behavior. Passing a predictive check does not establish that the model captures every future risk. [Stan’s predictive-check guide describes comparisons between model replications and observed statistics](https://mc-stan.org/docs/stan-users-guide/posterior-predictive-checks.html).

## Rebuild a forecast with its assumptions

A posterior describes a parameter; a predictive distribution describes future outcomes after averaging over it. A single Bernoulli prediction equals the fixed-mean forecast, while a block sharing that uncertain probability has covariance and a beta-binomial count. Define the exact count or path event, preserve the shared parameter inside a simulated path, and state the model limits beside the probability.
