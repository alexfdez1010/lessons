Lend \$1,000 for a year at 8% and the interest is \$80. Now leave it for a second year. Do you earn another \$80, or 8% of the new \$1,080 balance? That choice of base distinguishes **simple interest** from **compound interest**. We will keep the starting sum, rate and time identical so you can see exactly where the difference comes from.

## Interest is rent on money

Four quantities are enough to calculate interest: the amount, the payment for using it, the rate and the time.

When you lend money, you let another person use it for a time. **Interest** is the price charged for that use, rather like rent on a home. Depositing money in an interest-bearing bank account also makes you a lender to the bank. The analogy describes the payment for use; it does not make every deposit or loan equally safe.

- **Principal (P)** — the original chunk of money you lend or deposit. The "apartment" being rented out.
- **Interest** — the fee paid for the use of that money. The "rent."
- **Rate (r)** — the percentage of the principal charged per period, written as a decimal. 8% per year means $r = 0.08$.
- **Period / time (t)** — how long the money is on loan, measured in the rate's units. If $r$ is per year, $t$ is in years.

<Callout variant="info" title="Follow one contract">
For a single loan, interest paid by the borrower is interest received by the lender. A bank deposit and a bank loan are separate contracts and need not pay the same rate. The formulas below assume a constant rate and no repayments or extra deposits unless stated otherwise.
</Callout>

The only question left is *what the rent gets charged on* — and that's where simple and compound part ways.

## Simple interest: a flat rent

**Simple interest** calculates each period's interest on the original principal. With a fixed rate and no change in that principal, every period adds the same amount. If the interest is paid later, the amount owed grows in a straight line; accrued interest does not itself earn interest.

The interest you earn over the whole stretch is principal × rate × time:

$$\text{Interest} = P \cdot r \cdot t$$

And the final balance — the **future value (FV)**, meaning what the money is worth at the end — is the principal plus that interest:

$$\text{FV} = P(1 + r \cdot t)$$

### Worked example

You deposit **\$1,000** at **8%** simple interest and leave it for **10 years**.

First the interest:

$$\text{Interest} = 1000 \times 0.08 \times 10 = \$800$$

Then the final balance:

$$\text{FV} = 1000 \,(1 + 0.08 \times 10) = 1000 \times 1.8 = \$1{,}800$$

Each year you earn exactly $0.08 \times 1000$, i.e. **\$80**, ten times over, for \$800 total. Year 1 looks identical to year 10. Plot it and you get a ruler-straight line climbing \$80 a year — no curve, no acceleration, no drama.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Check what happens to the interest">
A deposit compounds when credited interest stays in the balance and earns interest itself. A bond coupon paid out and spent does not compound automatically. Many mortgages calculate interest on the outstanding principal and repay part of that principal each month; paying those interest charges is different from adding unpaid interest to the debt. Read the payment and reinvestment rules before choosing a formula.
</Callout>

### When it matters

A fixed-principal simple-interest calculation is useful when each period pays the same interest and that payment is not reinvested. For example, \$1,000 paying an \$80 annual coupon generates \$800 of coupons in ten years. Adding those payments to returned principal gives \$1,800 in total receipts; that does not mean a bond account balance rises by \$80 every year, and it ignores any interim price change.

## Compound interest: rent on the rent

**Compound interest** calculates interest on the principal **plus retained interest**. Last year's interest becomes part of the base for this year. At a constant positive rate, that growing base produces a curve that becomes steeper over time.

The future value after $t$ periods is:

$$\text{FV} = P(1 + r)^{t}$$

The exponent $t$ counts repeated multiplications by $(1 + r)$. One year gives $P(1 + r)$; two give $P(1 + r)(1 + r)$. Repeating the same growth factor is **exponential growth**.

### Worked example — same setup, very different ending

Take the *exact same* deposit: **\$1,000** at **8%**, this time **compounded** for **10 years**.

$$\text{FV} = 1000\,(1 + 0.08)^{10} = 1000 \times 1.08^{10} = 1000 \times 2.1589 \approx \$2{,}159$$

Walk the first couple of years to see the mechanism:

| End of year | Balance (compound) | Interest that year | Balance (simple) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | \$1,080.00 | \$80.00 | \$1,080 |
| 2 | \$1,166.40 | \$86.40 | \$1,160 |
| 3 | \$1,259.71 | \$93.31 | \$1,240 |
| 10 | \$2,158.92 | — | \$1,800 |

In year 2 the compound account earns **\$86.40**, not \$80 — that extra \$6.40 is 8% charged on year 1's \$80 of interest. Tiny at first, but it never stops compounding on itself.

After ten years, the compound balance is about **\$2,159**, compared with **\$1,800** under simple interest. The **\$359 difference** comes from reinvesting the interest, with no extra deposits. At the same fixed 8% for forty years, the balances would be about \$21,725 and \$4,200 respectively. Both figures depend on maintaining the stated rate for the entire period.

### When it matters

Use compounding when returns are retained and reinvested. For the \$1,000 at a constant 8% above, starting ten years earlier multiplies the eventual balance by 1.08^10 ≈ 2.16. That result depends on the assumed return and reinvestment; an investment can lose value, and a loan with repayments follows a different cash-flow path.

## How often it compounds changes the answer

Here's a subtlety that ambushes people: the headline rate isn't the whole story. **How often** interest gets added — the **compounding frequency** — quietly changes the final balance, even when the advertised rate is identical. The more often interest is credited, the sooner it starts earning *its own* interest, so the same "12% a year" ends up worth slightly more.

If a nominal annual rate $r$ is compounded $n$ times per year, the formula stretches to:

$$\text{FV} = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{n t}$$

Each period now earns a smaller slice ($r/n$), but there are more of them ($n \cdot t$), and the extra trips through the compounding machine win out.

### Worked example — same 12%, four schedules

Put **\$1,000** in at a **12%** nominal annual rate for **1 year**, and just change how often it compounds:

| Compounding | Periods/year (n) | Growth factor | Value after 1 year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual | 1 | $(1 + 0.12)^1$ | \$1,120.00 |
| Quarterly | 4 | $(1 + 0.03)^4$ | \$1,125.51 |
| Monthly | 12 | $(1 + 0.01)^{12}$ | \$1,126.83 |
| Daily | 365 | $(1 + 0.12/365)^{365}$ | \$1,127.47 |

At 12% nominal, daily compounding gives an **effective annual rate of about 12.747%**. That is the percentage change in the retained balance over one year. Increasing the frequency raises the result for this fixed positive nominal rate, with smaller increments at each step.

## Continuous compounding: the speed limit

If quarterly beats annual, and daily beats monthly, where does it end? What if interest compounded every *second*? Every *nanosecond*? You might expect the balance to rocket off to infinity — but it doesn't. It politely bumps into a ceiling.

As the number of compounding periods $n$ grows toward infinity, the formula converges to a clean limit involving Euler's number $e \approx 2.71828$. This is **continuous compounding** — interest credited at every instant:

$$\text{FV} = P \cdot e^{r t}$$

### Worked example

Our **\$1,000** at **12%** compounded **continuously** for **1 year**:

$$\text{FV} = 1000 \times e^{0.12 \times 1} = 1000 \times 1.12750 = \$1{,}127.50$$

Now line continuous up against the schedules from before:

| Compounding | Value after 1 year |
|---|---|
| Annual | \$1,120.00 |
| Quarterly | \$1,125.51 |
| Monthly | \$1,126.83 |
| Daily | \$1,127.47 |
| **Continuous** | **\$1,127.50** |

Daily compounding already lands within **three cents** of the continuous ceiling. The jump from annual to daily was worth \$7.47; squeezing all the way to *infinitely often* buys you another **3 cents**. The lesson: more frequent compounding helps, but it **doesn't run away** — it crawls toward a hard limit set by $e^{rt}$ and stops. Frequency has rapidly diminishing returns.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why bother with continuous?">
You'll rarely meet a real account compounding "continuously" — banks credit interest on fixed schedules. But $e^{rt}$ is mathematically the cleanest growth model, so it's the workhorse of finance theory: option pricing (Black–Scholes), bond math, and growth-rate calculations all lean on it because the exponent behaves so nicely.
</Callout>
