Your \$1,000 savings balance grows to **\$1,050** in a year. Over the same year, the basket of goods that cost \$1,000 rises to **\$1,030**. You can buy more than before, but the improvement is smaller than 5%. To measure it, compare the growth of your money with the growth of prices over the same period.

## A bigger number isn't more money

Two things move at once in the real world, and they pull in opposite directions.

**Inflation** is a broad rise in the prices of goods and services over time. At 3% inflation, a representative basket costing \$100 becomes a \$103 basket. A price index tracks this change across many purchases; your own expenses can rise at a different rate.

**Purchasing power** means how much a sum of money can buy. If prices rise while the sum stays unchanged, it buys a smaller share of the basket. We need both the account balance and the price level to measure the change.

So when your money earns interest, two races are happening:

| What's racing | Direction | Effect on you |
|---|---|---|
| Your **balance** (nominal interest) | Grows by the nominal rate | More dollars in the account |
| **Prices** (inflation) | Grow by the inflation rate | Each dollar buys less |

You only get *ahead* if your balance outruns prices. The gap between the two is what you can genuinely spend more on — and that gap has a name.

<Callout variant="info" title="Nominal vs real, in one breath">
**Nominal** = the number you're paid, in plain dollars, ignoring inflation. **Real** = what's left after inflation, measured in *buying power*. Nominal answers "how many more dollars?"; real answers "how much more *stuff*?" The second is the one that pays for your groceries.
</Callout>

## The real rate: stripping out inflation

The **real interest rate** adjusts the nominal return for inflation. Here nominal means before the inflation adjustment, whether the annual interest figure is already effective or not. First put the return and inflation on the same time basis; then compare them. Subtraction gives an approximation, while division gives the exact result.

The shortcut is just subtraction:

$$r_{\text{real}} \approx r_{\text{nominal}} - \pi$$

where $\pi$ (pi, the Greek letter economists use) is the inflation rate. It's fast, it's close enough for everyday rates, and it's what most people do in their heads.

The *exact* version is the **Fisher equation**, named after economist Irving Fisher. The logic: your money grows by a factor of $(1 + r_{\text{nominal}})$, but prices grow by a factor of $(1 + \pi)$, so your *real* growth factor is one divided by the other:

$$1 + r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + r_{\text{nominal}}}{1 + \pi}$$

Solving for the real rate:

$$r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + r_{\text{nominal}}}{1 + \pi} - 1$$

### Worked example

You earn **5%** nominal in a year when inflation is **3%**.

The quick shortcut:

$$r_{\text{real}} \approx 5\% - 3\% = 2\%$$

The exact Fisher calculation:

$$r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1.05}{1.03} - 1 = 1.01942 - 1 = 0.01942 \approx 1.94\%$$

So the shortcut said 2% and the truth is **1.94%** — a difference of six hundredths of a percentage point. For a quick estimate at these rates, subtraction is close. The chart compares the two input rates with the exact real rate:

<Callout variant="tip" title="When subtraction overstates the result">
With 5% nominal return and 3% inflation, the shortcut gives 2%, but the exact result is 2% ÷ 1.03 ≈ 1.94%. In general, real = (nominal − inflation) ÷ (1 + inflation). With positive inflation, division shrinks the magnitude of the difference: a positive result becomes smaller, while a negative result becomes less negative. The exact rate is therefore not always below the shortcut.
</Callout>

## When real goes negative

A **negative real rate** means that the ending balance buys a smaller share of the basket than the starting balance did. It can occur even when the balance rises: prices may rise faster.

### Worked example

You leave money in a savings account paying **3%**. Inflation that year is **5%**.

The shortcut:

$$r_{\text{real}} \approx 3\% - 5\% = -2\%$$

The exact Fisher version:

$$r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1.03}{1.05} - 1 = 0.98095 - 1 = -0.01905 \approx -1.9\%$$

Put real numbers on it. Deposit **\$1,000**. After the year your statement proudly shows **\$1,030** — you "made" \$30. But the basket of goods that cost \$1,000 last year now costs **\$1,050**. So your \$1,030 can't even buy last year's basket; you're short \$20. In purchasing-power terms you went *backwards* by about 1.9%. The balance rose; your real wealth fell.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Count money and purchasing power separately">
A fixed cash balance buys less over a period in which prices rise. That is a loss of purchasing power even if no dollars leave the account. The calculation describes the measured period; an annual inflation rate does not say that prices rose every day. Cash can still serve a purpose when money is needed soon.
</Callout>

### When it matters

Use the real rate to compare purchasing power, and assess liquidity and investment risk separately. A cash reserve can lose purchasing power during inflation while still being useful for near-term payments. A higher advertised return does not establish that an alternative is safer or suitable for that reserve.

## Why the approximation drifts

The shortcut omits a multiplication term. Its error depends on the size of inflation and the real return, so use the exact calculation when that error could affect the comparison.

Look again at the Fisher equation. Multiplying out $(1 + r_{\text{real}})(1 + \pi) = 1 + r_{\text{nominal}}$ gives:

$$r_{\text{nominal}} = r_{\text{real}} + \pi + (r_{\text{real}} \times \pi)$$

The product of the real return and inflation is the cross-term. In our 5% and 3% example, it is about 0.01942 × 0.03 = 0.000583, or **0.0583 percentage points**. Adding it to the exact real return and inflation recovers the nominal return.

### Worked example

Suppose **50%** nominal interest in a year of **40%** inflation — big numbers, the kind you'd see in a high-inflation economy.

The shortcut:

$$r_{\text{real}} \approx 50\% - 40\% = 10\%$$

The exact Fisher version:

$$r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1.50}{1.40} - 1 = 1.0714 - 1 = 0.0714 \approx 7.1\%$$

The shortcut gives 10%, while the exact result is about **7.14%**: it overstates the return by **2.86 percentage points**. The table compares the two methods in several cases. The final column is a difference between rates, measured in percentage points.

| Nominal | Inflation | Shortcut (nom − infl) | Exact Fisher | Gap in percentage points |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 2% | 3.0% | 2.94% | 0.06 |
| 5% | 3% | 2.0% | 1.94% | 0.06 |
| 10% | 8% | 2.0% | 1.85% | 0.15 |
| 50% | 40% | 10.0% | 7.1% | 2.9 |

### When it matters

Subtraction is useful for a quick estimate at low rates. Use the exact ratio for a precise calculation, high inflation or close comparisons. Keep both rates over the same period and in the same currency; expected inflation estimates a future real return, while observed inflation measures a past one.

## Real returns everywhere

The same calculation applies to savings, pay rises and investment returns. An advertised percentage alone usually does not say how much purchasing power changes: you also need the relevant inflation rate, period and currency.

| Where you meet a rate | The quoted (nominal) number | What you actually care about (real) |
|---|---|---|
| **Savings account** | "Earn 4% APY!" | 4% minus inflation — maybe 1% real, maybe negative |
| **Bonds** | "This bond yields 6%" | 6% minus expected inflation over its life |
| **Wages** | "You got a 3% raise" | A 3% raise in 4% inflation is a real *pay cut* |
| **Crypto staking / DeFi** | "6% on this stablecoin" | 6% minus inflation — about 1% real if inflation is 5% |

A 3% pay rise with 4% inflation changes purchasing power by 1.03 / 1.04 − 1 ≈ **−0.96%**. For a dollar-denominated stablecoin position that actually earns 6%, 5% dollar-price inflation leaves 1.06 / 1.05 − 1 ≈ **0.95% real**, before fees and taxes. The peg does not remove issuer, platform or depegging risk; spending in another currency adds an exchange-rate comparison.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Match the period and the currency">
Ask whether the return is before or after inflation, and which inflation measure matches the spending you want to compare. Adjust the return actually earned, after any costs you are including. An annual yield and one month of inflation do not describe the same interval.
</Callout>
