Your balance rises from 1000 to 1050 USD. The basket you wanted rises from 1000 to 1030. You have more dollars and can buy more of that basket, but the improvement is less than 5%. Measuring it takes two growth factors over the same dates, not just the number on the statement.

All figures below are invented teaching cases. Unless stated otherwise, money and prices use the same currency and period, the return is effective for that period, and there are no outside cash flows, taxes or fees. We add a deliberately specified tax rule later.

## Purchasing power compares two factors

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"Money grows 5% while the same basket costs 3% more. What should you compare to measure purchasing-power growth?"}
  options={[{"text": "The money factor 1.05 divided by the price factor 1.03", "correct": true}, {"text": "The money balance plus the new basket price"}, {"text": "The two percentages multiplied and called a cash balance"}, {"text": "The money factor divided by the original balance again"}]}
  explanation={"Purchasing power is measured in basket units. Its growth factor is the money growth factor divided by the basket-price growth factor."}
/>

A **nominal return** measures growth in money before adjusting for prices. A **real return** measures the change in purchasing power against a stated price measure. **Inflation** is an increase in that price measure; a **price index** summarises prices for a defined population and basket. Your personal spending need not match it.

Let $i$ be the effective nominal return and $\pi$ the price-index change over the same interval. Decimal $0.05$ means 5%. The money factor is $1+i$ and the price factor is $1+\pi$. For an initial balance $W_0$ and initial basket price $P_0$, affordable basket units change from $W_0/P_0$ to $W_0(1+i)/[P_0(1+\pi)]$. Divide the latter by the former:

$$
1+r=\frac{1+i}{1+\pi},\qquad
r=\frac{1+i}{1+\pi}-1=\frac{i-\pi}{1+\pi}.
$$

Here $r$ is the real return. The price factor must be positive; a −100% price change would make this division undefined. These are measured purchasing-power ratios, not a claim that the return was risk-free. The [Czech National Bank](https://www.cnb.cz/en/faq/What-are-nominal-and-real-interest-rates) gives the exact ratio as well as the subtraction approximation.

### From balances to baskets

Start with 1000 USD and a basket costing 1000. After 5% money growth and 3% price growth, the balance is 1050 and the basket costs 1030. Affordable basket units move from 1 to $1050/1030=1.019417476$. Thus

$$
r=\frac{1.05}{1.03}-1=0.019417476\approx1.941748\%.
$$

The ending balance expressed in initial-price purchasing-power units is $1050/1.03=1019.417476$ USD. That is not another bank balance to add to 1050; it is a different unit of measurement for the same ending resources.

### When to use it

State the price index or basket, currency, dates and whether the money return is before or after included costs. A real-return calculation does not establish liquidity, credit quality or suitability of the investment.

## Subtraction is an approximation with a signed error

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="At 3% money growth and 5% inflation, the shortcut gives −2%. Must the exact result be more negative?" text="No. The exact return is −0.02/1.05 = −1.904762%. Subtraction is below the exact result here; the approximation error has a sign." />

The shortcut $r\approx i-\pi$ is useful for a rough estimate. Multiplying the exact factors shows what it drops:

$$
1+i=(1+r)(1+\pi),\qquad i=r+\pi+r\pi.
$$

The **cross-term** is $r\pi$. Therefore the subtraction result minus the exact real return is also $r\pi$. Its sign matters. With positive inflation, dividing $i-\pi$ by $1+\pi$ shrinks the magnitude: positive returns become smaller than the shortcut, negative returns less negative. Under deflation that denominator is below one, so the magnitude can increase instead.

### Small and large rates

At 5% nominal and 3% inflation, subtraction gives 2%, while the exact result is 1.941748%. The difference is **0.058252 percentage points**. A percentage-point difference subtracts two rates; it is not a dollar amount or the same as a relative percentage error.

At 50% nominal and 40% inflation,

$$
r=\frac{1.50}{1.40}-1=7.142857\%,\qquad
(50-40)\%-7.142857\%=2.857143\text{ percentage points}.
$$

The second row is a nominal gain with a real loss: a 1000 balance becomes 1030 while the basket becomes 1050. The shortfall to buy a whole original basket is 20 USD, but the real return is $1030/1050-1=-1.904762\%$, not a 2% exact loss.

### When to use it

Use subtraction for a deliberately rough estimate. Use the factor ratio for precise comparisons, high inflation, deflation or close results. Report the error's sign and units instead of saying the shortcut always overstates every return.

## Match effective returns and inflation over the same dates

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"A quoted 12% annual rate credits 1% monthly. Annual inflation is 5%. Which inputs have matching dates for an exact annual real return?"}
  options={[{"text": "The twelve-month money factor 1.01^12 and annual price factor 1.05", "correct": true}, {"text": "The monthly money rate 1% and annual inflation rate 5% directly"}, {"text": "The annual quote 12% and six-month price change without conversion"}, {"text": "The first monthly balance and the final annual basket price alone"}]}
  explanation={"Build effective factors for the same twelve-month interval before dividing."}
/>

“Nominal” is used in two different comparisons. Before inflation, a return can still be an **effective** return after compounding. Separately, an **annual nominal quote** $j$ may specify interest $j/m$ per credit period. You must convert that quote to the actual period's growth factor before adjusting for prices.

### Annual quote, monthly credits, annual inflation

Suppose a hypothetical account quotes $j=12\%$ annually, credits $j/12=1\%$ each month and retains interest for twelve complete periods. Its effective annual factor is

$$
1+i_{\mathrm{year}}=(1+0.12/12)^{12}=1.126825030,
$$

so the effective annual money return is 12.682503%. If the price index rises from 100 to 105 over those same twelve months, annual inflation is 5% and

$$
r_{\mathrm{year}}=\frac{1.126825030}{1.05}-1=7.316670\%.
$$

Using $12\%-5\%=7\%$ misses both compounding and the exact inflation ratio. A uniform-equivalent monthly inflation factor is $1.05^{1/12}$, not $1+0.05/12$. This equivalent factor reproduces the annual index change; it does not say that every actual month had that inflation.

<EffectivePeriodBridge client:visible  />

### A six-month measurement needs six-month prices

With six monthly credits, the money factor is $(1.01)^6=1.061520151$. If the index is observed to rise from 100 to 102 over those **same six months**, then

$$
r_{\mathrm{six\ months}}=\frac{1.061520151}{1.02}-1=4.070603\%.
$$

A full-year 5% index change does not, by itself, tell you the actual change over those six months. Use the matching endpoints. For the same reason, a dollar return cannot be deflated directly by a euro-price basket without first expressing both in a common currency.

### When to use it

Write down the credit frequency, effective return period and price-index endpoints. Actual dated interest accrual has further conventions, covered in the [next lesson](/interest-and-yield/day-count-and-interest-accrual). A day count, a compounding rule and a price adjustment are separate choices.

## Deflation can outweigh a negative nominal return

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="Can a negative money return coexist with rising purchasing power?" text="Yes. If money falls 1% and the fixed basket price falls 3%, 0.99/0.97−1 = +2.061856%. The balance shrinks while it buys more of that basket." />

**Deflation** means the relevant price index falls: $-1<\pi<0$. Prices falling is different from inflation slowing from +3% to +2%; the latter still means prices rise over the second interval.

Suppose money falls 1% and the basket price falls 3% over one year. Start with 1000 USD and a 1000-USD basket. End with 990 USD and a 970-USD basket:

$$
r=\frac{0.99}{0.97}-1=2.061856\%.
$$

The nominal balance fell, but it now buys $990/970=1.020618557$ baskets instead of one. Holding money fixed with 3% deflation would instead give $1/0.97-1=3.092784\%$ more basket units, not exactly 3%.

<FisherPurchasingPower client:visible nominal={-1} inflation={-3} />

Equal money and price factors give zero real change, whether both rise or both fall. That equality says nothing about payment access, default or other risks. A negative real return means fewer units of the specified basket, not necessarily a negative bank balance.

### When to use it

Check both signs before making a conclusion from the balance alone. “Positive nominal” does not guarantee a real gain; “negative nominal” does not guarantee a real loss.

## Apply an explicit tax rule before deflating the money

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"A fictional tax takes 20% of the positive 50 USD nominal gain at the end. What cash remains from an initial 1000 USD?"}
  options={[{"text": "1040 USD, before the separate price adjustment", "correct": true}, {"text": "1050 USD, because inflation cancels every tax"}, {"text": "1010 USD, because the whole gain is paid as tax"}, {"text": "840 USD, because the tax applies to the entire balance"}]}
  explanation={"The stated rule taxes the 50 gain: tax 10, cash 1000+50−10=1040. It is not a jurisdictional rule."}
/>

For this section only, invent a tax equal to **20% of positive nominal interest**, collected once at the period's end. There are no allowances, loss credits, fees or other payments. This is a teaching assumption, not a country's tax rule.

Earn 5% on 1000 USD. Gross nominal interest is 50, tax is $0.20(50)=10$ and the ending balance is $1000+50-10=1040$. With 3% inflation,

$$
r_{\mathrm{after\ tax}}=\frac{1+0.05(1-0.20)}{1.03}-1
=\frac{1.04}{1.03}-1=0.970874\%.
$$

The before-tax real return was 1.941748%. Multiplying it by $1-0.20$ gives 1.553398%, which is **not** this model's after-tax result: the invented tax applies to nominal interest, not the real gain.

For general signed input $i$ within this viewer, the rule is $i_{\mathrm{net}}=i-t\max(i,0)$: take the larger of $i$ and zero as the tax base. A negative nominal change receives no credit under this particular assumption. Then divide $1+i_{\mathrm{net}}$ by the price factor.

<FisherPurchasingPower client:visible mode="tax" />

Change the example to 5% nominal, 4% inflation and a 30% tax on positive interest. Net nominal growth becomes $0.05(0.70)=3.5\%$ and real growth is $1.035/1.04-1=-0.480769\%$. The gross before-tax real return was positive, $1.05/1.04-1=0.961538\%$, while the net real return is negative.

### When to use it

Identify which money gain is actually taxed, the timing and any included costs before calculating purchasing power. Different tax bases, timing, credits or fees change the answer. Keep this hypothetical arithmetic separate from jurisdiction-specific tax advice.

## A price index is not every household's spending basket

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="Two people earn the same 5% money return. Must their own fixed baskets show the same real return?" text="No. In the invented cases, basket A rises 8% and basket B 2%. Real returns are 1.05/1.08−1 = −2.777778% and 1.05/1.02−1 = +2.941176%, despite the same bank return." />

The [BLS CPI FAQ](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/questions-and-answers.htm) explains that an index reflects its defined population and methodology, rather than each household's experience. A price index also does not measure every aspect of living standards. A larger spending bill can reflect buying more units even when unit prices are unchanged.

### Two fixed baskets under identical prices

Invent two goods: food at 10 USD per unit and transport at 20. Food rises 10% to 11; transport stays at 20. Basket A contains eight food units and one transport unit; B contains two food units and four transport units.

A 5% money return has real value $1.05/1.08-1=-2.777778\%$ against A and $1.05/1.02-1=2.941176\%$ against B. The bank result did not change. The measured purchasing-power comparison did, because food represents 80% versus 20% of the initial basket cost.

This fixed-quantity example is not an implementation of official CPI. It holds quantities and quality constant and excludes substitution. Selecting another measurement basket does not magically create another monetary profit or prescribe what to buy.

### When to use it

Choose an index suited to the spending question and explain its scope. Distinguish price changes from changes in quantities, quality, lifestyle and taxes. A single national inflation figure is useful for its purpose without becoming everyone's personal inflation rate.

## A forecast input is different from a realised result

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"A fixed 5% money return faces two equally likely inflation outcomes, 0% or 10%. Does using mean inflation of 5% automatically give expected real return?"}
  options={[{"text": "No: calculate the real return in each scenario, then average using its probability", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes: every nonlinear ratio equals the ratio of the mean inputs"}, {"text": "Yes: expected inflation is always the inflation subsequently observed"}, {"text": "No: real returns cannot be calculated until all investment risks disappear"}]}
  explanation={"A forecast is not realised data, and averaging does not generally commute with a nonlinear ratio."}
/>

A calculation using observed money growth and observed prices is **ex post**: after the interval. Before it ends, a forecast inflation input produces an **ex ante** estimate or scenario. The [St. Louis Fed's explanation](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/blog/2022/05/constructing-ex-ante-real-interest-rates-on-fred/) makes this distinction. Neither label removes uncertainty about the future input.

### Feeding a mean into a ratio can change the target

Stipulate 5% nominal growth in both of two equally likely invented inflation scenarios:

<table tabIndex={0} aria-label="Worked comparison table 3" className="block w-full max-w-full overflow-x-auto text-left text-sm focus-visible:outline focus-visible:outline-2 focus-visible:outline-offset-2 focus-visible:outline-brand-600">
  <thead><tr><th scope="col">Scenario probability</th><th scope="col">Inflation</th><th scope="col">Real return</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><th scope="row">50%</th><td>0%</td><td>$1.05/1.00-1=5\%$</td></tr>
    <tr><th scope="row">50%</th><td>10%</td><td>$1.05/1.10-1=-4.545455\%$</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Mean inflation is 5%, so inserting that single mean gives $1.05/1.05-1=0\%$. But the average real return across these scenarios is

$$
0.5(5\%)+0.5(-4.545455\%)=0.227273\%.
$$

These differ because dividing by prices is nonlinear. A ratio using one forecast number need not equal the average real return over possible outcomes. If nominal returns also vary, their joint behaviour with inflation matters too. No probabilities in this example were estimated from market data.

Move inflation from the initial forecast input of 5% to the two scenario values, 0% and 10%. The island calculates each conditional ratio; it does not generate an inflation distribution or identify which scenario will occur.

### When to use it

Label the input as observed, assumed or forecast, and name the horizon. For an expected-return question under uncertainty, specify the scenario distribution and average the corresponding real outcomes; do not silently reinterpret a mean-inflation proxy as a realised fact or a general expected-return identity.
