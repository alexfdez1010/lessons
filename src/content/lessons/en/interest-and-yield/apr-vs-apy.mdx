One account pays **5% effective per year**. Another pays **5% nominal per year, compounded monthly**. Put \$1,000 in each, leave every interest payment in the account, and the second ends the year about \$1.16 ahead. The two labels describe different calculations.

To compare them, first find the rate for one period. Then work out what happens when the balance earns that rate repeatedly for a year.

A **nominal annual rate** scales a periodic rate up by multiplication. If an account pays 1% each month, its nominal annual rate is 1% × 12 = **12%**. The multiplication describes the rate; it does not yet grow a balance through twelve months.

The **effective annual rate** measures the year's growth with compounding included. In US deposit disclosures, **APY** means *annual percentage yield*. For a fixed rate, a full year and no withdrawals, it tells you how much interest the deposit earns relative to the amount deposited. Fees and taxes can reduce what you keep.

<Callout variant="info" title="APR needs its own definition">
**APR**, or *annual percentage rate*, is a borrowing disclosure whose calculation depends on the product and jurisdiction. It can include required loan fees. It is not a universal synonym for a nominal annual interest rate. In this lesson's calculations, **r is the nominal rate before fees**; do not feed an unfamiliar regulatory APR into the formula without checking its definition.
</Callout>

## From a monthly rate to an annual yield

Start with \$1,000 earning **1% per month**, with no deposits, withdrawals or fees:

| Month | Starting balance | Interest at 1% | Ending balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | \$1,000.00 | \$10.00 | \$1,010.00 |
| 2 | \$1,010.00 | \$10.10 | \$1,020.10 |
| 3 | \$1,020.10 | \$10.20 | \$1,030.30 |

The extra ten cents in month 2 are 1% of the previous month's \$10 interest. Repeating that multiplication twelve times gives \$1,000 × 1.01¹² = **\$1,126.83**. The annual yield is \$126.83 ÷ \$1,000 ≈ **12.68%**.

If the nominal annual rate is $r$ and there are $n$ equal compounding periods per year:

$$\text{Effective annual rate} = \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^n - 1$$

For $r = 0.12$ and $n = 12$:

$$\left(1 + \frac{0.12}{12}\right)^{12} - 1 = 1.126825 - 1 \approx 12.68\%$$

The **0.68 percentage point** difference from 12% is the annual effect of interest earning interest within the year. That adds \$6.83 to a \$1,000 deposit.

### When to use it

Use this conversion to compare **interest-only growth** on the same balance and over the same year. A loan with repayments or fees needs its full cash-flow schedule as well; its total interest bill is not the effective rate multiplied by its original principal.

## Changing the compounding frequency

Keep the nominal rate fixed at **12%** and retain the interest:

| Schedule | Periods per year | Effective annual rate | \$1,000 after one year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual | 1 | 12.00% | \$1,120.00 |
| Quarterly | 4 | 12.55% | \$1,125.51 |
| Monthly | 12 | 12.68% | \$1,126.83 |
| Daily | 365 | 12.747% | \$1,127.47 |
| Continuous limit | ∞ | 12.750% | \$1,127.50 |

For this positive, fixed nominal rate, more frequent compounding increases the yield, with smaller and smaller increments. The continuous limit is $e^{0.12} - 1 \approx 12.750\%$, where $e \approx 2.71828$. Daily compounding already lands within three cents of that limit on \$1,000.

With annual compounding, $(1 + r)^1 - 1 = r$: nominal and effective rates coincide. They also coincide when **r = 0**, whatever the frequency. Equality alone therefore does not prove that an account compounds annually.

## Comparing offers on the same basis

Account A advertises **5.00% nominal, compounded daily**. Account B advertises **5.05% APY**. Neither charges fees and both keep the stated rate for a full year.

- A: $(1 + 0.05/365)^{365} - 1 \approx 5.127\%$, so \$1,000 becomes about **\$1,051.27**.
- B: 5.05% is already effective, so \$1,000 becomes **\$1,050.50**.

A earns about **\$0.77 more**, despite its smaller headline number. The conclusion applies to these conditions; withdrawal restrictions, changing rates or fees could change the comparison.

## Loan APR, fees and repayments

US loan APR can include fees as well as interest. **APY is a deposit-yield term**, not a universal name for the amount a borrower pays. In Spain, **TIN** is the nominal interest rate and **TAE** is the annual equivalent disclosure, which includes relevant costs under the applicable rules. APR, APY, TIN and TAE are therefore not interchangeable translations.

A simple fee example shows why frequency alone is insufficient. A one-year loan pays you **\$1,000**, requires **\$1,100** back at the end, and charges a **\$20 upfront fee**. You have \$980 available, but must return \$1,100. For these two cash flows, the effective cost is:

$$\frac{1100}{980} - 1 \approx 12.24\%$$

The interest alone was 10% of the stated principal. The fee raises the cost relative to the money you could use. This two-payment calculation illustrates the mechanism; a regulatory disclosure must follow its own prescribed method.

If instead you repay part of the principal every month, the outstanding balance falls. To calculate the interest bill, apply the contractual rate to the outstanding balance in each period and follow the repayment schedule. Treating the whole original loan as untouched for a year describes a different loan.
