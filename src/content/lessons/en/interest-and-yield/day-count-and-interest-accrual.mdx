You lend €10,000 at a fixed 6% annual simple rate from 31 December to 31 January. “One month” seems enough information—until one calculation charges for 31 actual days and another uses 30 convention days. The rate and the dates are the same; the time measurement differs.

The [simple-interest lesson](/interest-and-yield/simple-vs-compound) used a term measured in years. Here we construct that term from actual dates. We will compare **Actual/365 Fixed** with one precisely defined **plain 30E/360 Eurobond algorithm**. These are teaching choices, not universal rules for deposits, loans or bonds. All cash amounts are invented euros, before fees and tax.

**Accrued interest** is interest accumulated over an elapsed accrual period, whether or not it has been paid yet. The **accrual period** has contractual boundaries. A **day-count convention** translates those dates into a **year fraction**, the number that plays the role of time in an interest formula.

Counting is like measuring a board: decide where the measurement begins and ends before choosing the ruler. In every example and visual here, **include the start date and exclude the end date**. This local boundary convention prevents the shared date of adjacent periods being counted twice. Check the actual contract rather than assuming every product uses these boundaries.

### Count the main interval explicitly

For **2025-12-31 to 2026-01-31**:

| Dates | Included? | Calendar days counted |
|---|---|---:|
|31 December 2025 | Yes, the starting date |1 |
|1 January through 30 January 2026 | Yes |30 |
|31 January 2026 | No, the ending boundary |0 |
| Total | — | **31** |

The next period could begin on 31 January, counting that date once in the next interval. Counting both endpoints in both periods would create overlap. Calendar-day counting also includes weekends; “calendar days” is not “business days.” A clock changing for daylight saving does not remove a civil date from an accrual period.

### When to use it

Identify the accrual boundaries before reading a payment date or a phrase such as “monthly interest.” Dates on which money is paid can differ from dates through which interest accumulates. A day count supplies a time fraction; it does not specify the principal, annual rate, compounding method or payment schedule by itself.

## Actual/365 Fixed counts real days over 365

For **Actual/365 Fixed**, sometimes written **Act/365F**, let $D$ be the actual calendar days between the stated boundaries:

$$f_{365F}=\frac{D}{365}$$

The “actual” part describes the numerator. The “fixed” part describes the denominator. It does not mean that every calendar year has 365 days. The [Strata day-count specification defines Actual/365 Fixed this way](https://strata.opengamma.io/apidocs/com/opengamma/strata/basics/date/DayCounts.html#ACT_365F).

### Convert 31 days to interest

Use the main interval, €10,000 constant principal and a fixed **6% annual simple-accrual rate**. We explicitly choose

$$I=P\,r\,f$$

Here $P$ is principal in euros, $r=0.06$ is the annual simple rate as a decimal and $f$ is the year fraction. This chosen method does not add accrued interest back to principal.

$$f_{365F}=\frac{31}{365}\approx 0.0849315068$$

$$I=10{,}000(0.06)\frac{31}{365}\approx 50.95890411$$

Interest rounds to **€50.96** if the contract rounds the period total to cents. Principal plus this interest is **€10,050.96**, before any other amounts. The annual rate is not divided by 12 unless the selected time rule justifies a one-twelfth fraction.

### When to use it

Use this calculation when the agreed method specifies Actual/365 Fixed and simple accrual with the supplied rate and principal. Other “Actual” conventions can use different denominators or require a coupon schedule. Do not silently replace 365 with 366 or treat Actual/365F as a shorthand for every convention with actual days.

## Plain 30E/360 uses adjusted endpoint components

For this lesson, **plain 30E/360, Eurobond basis**, means this exact algorithm: replace an endpoint day-of-month of 31 with 30 at **each** endpoint, leaving all other day numbers unchanged. This is the plain algorithm associated with the 2006 ISDA definition 4.16(g), as distinguished from **30E/360 ISDA**, whose end-of-February rules differ. The [Strata documentation lists both algorithms separately](https://strata.opengamma.io/day_counts/). Names have changed across definitions and editions, so the stated algorithm matters more than a casual “thirty over three-sixty.”

Let $Y_i,M_i,D_i$ be each endpoint's year, month and day component, and define $D_i'=\min(D_i, 30)$. The convention numerator is

$$N_{30E}=360(Y_2-Y_1)+30(M_2-M_1)+(D_2'-D_1')$$

The year fraction is

$$f_{30E}=\frac{N_{30E}}{360}$$

The adjusted components are ingredients in this formula, not a claim that a Gregorian date such as 30 February exists.

### Work across the year boundary

For 31 December 2025 to 31 January 2026, both 31 day components become 30:

$$N_{30E}=360(2026-2025)+30(1-12)+(30-30)=360-330=30$$

$$f_{30E}=\frac{30}{360}=\frac 1{12}$$

With the same €10,000 and 6% annual simple rate, interest = €10,000 ×0.06 ×30/360 = **€50.00**. Compare the €50.96 under Actual/365F: both are correct for their respective assumed conventions. One is not a rounding error in the other.

### A convention day is not a real elapsed day

In the valid interval **30 January 2026 to 31 January 2026**, one calendar day passes. Plain 30E clamps both endpoint components to 30, giving $N_{30E}=30-30=0$ and zero interest under this selected simple formula. This does not say that time stopped; it shows that this numerator is a contractual measuring rule.

February 28 or 29 remains 28 or 29 under our plain algorithm. Do not import a February month-end adjustment from a different variant. In particular, the same-looking labels **30E/360** and **30E/360 ISDA** are not interchangeable without reading the definition. [FpML's official scheme records their distinct 2006 definition references](https://www.fpml.org/spec/fpml-4-2-11-tr-5/html/fpml-4-2-schemes.html).

### When to use it

Use the named variant and definition version specified for the instrument. The example's simple clamp rule needs only two dates; other variants can require maturity status or schedule information. Do not select whichever convention produces the preferred payment.

A leap day is an extra date in the calendar, not permission to swap a fixed denominator. Compare a full February in a common year and a leap year, keeping €10,000 and the 6% annual simple rate:

| Interval | Actual days | Actual/365F fraction | Simple interest | Plain 30E/360 numerator and interest |
|---|---:|---|---:|---|
|2023-02-01 to 2023-03-01 |28 |28/365 | €600 ×28/365 ≈ **€46.03** |30; €600 ×30/360 = **€50.00** |
|2024-02-01 to 2024-03-01 |29 |29/365 | €600 ×29/365 ≈ **€47.67** |30; €600 ×30/360 = **€50.00** |

For the leap interval, the exact Actual/365F year fraction is 29 ÷365 ≈ **0.07945205**. The Actual/365F interest differs by one daily accrual, €600 ÷365 ≈ **€1.6438356**, before period-total rounding. The displayed rounded period interests differ by €1.64.

### A calendar anniversary need not produce a fraction of one

From 1 January 2024 to 1 January 2025 there are 366 actual days. Actual/365F gives 366/365 ≈ **1.00273973**, so the selected simple accrual is €10,000 ×0.06 ×366/365 ≈ **€601.64**. Plain 30E/360 gives 360/360 =1 and **€600.00**. This does not redefine the calendar year. It applies two different conversion rules to its dates.

### When to use it

Check periods spanning February and year boundaries explicitly. A rule that divides some days by 366 is a different convention, not an automatic correction to Actual/365 Fixed. If a contract names Actual/Actual or a schedule-dependent method, use that method's complete definition rather than adapting this one informally.

The formula $I=Prf$ above is **fixed-rate simple accrual** with constant principal. A day-count fraction does not choose compounding for you. An effective-rate growth formula, daily capitalization, a changing rate or a changing balance needs additional terms. See the earlier [simple and compound interest comparison](/interest-and-yield/simple-vs-compound) for that separate choice.

### Split the period when the principal changes

Keep Actual/365F and 6%, but suppose the principal falls from €10,000 to €8,000 at the start of 15 January 2026. Each subperiod keeps its own principal:

| Start included, end excluded | Actual days | Principal | Simple interest before rounding |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
|31 December 2025 to 15 January 2026 |15 | €10,000 | €10,000 ×0.06 ×15/365 ≈ €24.65753425 |
|15 January 2026 to 31 January 2026 |16 | €8,000 | €8,000 ×0.06 ×16/365 ≈ €21.04109589 |
| Total |31 | — | **€45.69863014**, rounding to €45.70 |

Using the original €10,000 for all 31 days would incorrectly give €50.96 under this changed-principal scenario. The shared 15 January belongs to the second interval and is not counted twice. A changing rate similarly requires the applicable rate for each segment under the specified method.

### Distinguish a payment move from an accrual extension

Suppose two invented contracts both pay on 2 February, but one keeps an accrual end of 31 January while the other explicitly extends it to 2 February. Starting 31 December, with unchanged €10,000, 6% and Actual/365F:

- End 31 January: 31 days; €600 ×31/365 ≈ **€50.96**
- End 2 February: 33 days; €600 ×33/365 ≈ **€54.25**

The payment date alone cannot select between those answers. The [example note terms filed with the SEC list several day-count choices and separate contractual payment provisions](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/797463/000079746308000007/exhibit4e3312008.htm); that document illustrates specified terms, not a rule for all accounts or current product recommendations.

### State when rounding occurs

For a separate €1,000 principal at 5% simple annual interest, daily accrual on Actual/365F is €50 ÷365 ≈ **€0.1369863**. If a hypothetical contract rounds each daily amount to €0.14 and then adds 31 of them, the result is **€4.34**. If it adds unrounded amounts and rounds the period total, €50 ×31/365 ≈ €4.2465753 becomes **€4.25**. Neither rounding policy should be silently substituted for the other.

### When to use it

To reconcile an interest amount, collect the accrual boundaries, full day-count variant, principal history, rate and its accrual method, capitalization rules and rounding policy. Confirm how any business-day adjustment affects accrual boundaries rather than inferring it from payment timing. You now have the time fraction; the contract supplies the rest of the calculation.
