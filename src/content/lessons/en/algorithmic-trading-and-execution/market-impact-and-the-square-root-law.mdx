A buy order finishes when the reference price is 100.60. Its average fill was 100.40, against an arrival benchmark of 100. Which number belongs in its execution bill? Charging every share 0.60 would confuse the price at completion with the price actually paid.

**Market impact** concerns the price response associated with trading. It is one ingredient in execution cost, alongside spread, fees, delay and other price changes. An observed price move also contains news and other traders' activity. We will keep a measured association, a hypothetical impact model and an actual cash bill separate. Every numerical case below is invented.

## Measure the endpoint and the fills separately

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"A filled buy ends with reference price 100.60, but average fill is 100.40 against arrival 100. Which price determines its fill bill?"}
 options={[{"text": "The quantity-weighted 100.40 average, with fees recorded separately", "correct": true}, {"text": "The 100.60 endpoint charged to every filled share"}, {"text": "Whichever of the two prices makes the strategy look cheaper"}]}
 explanation={"The endpoint response and actual average fills measure different quantities. An attribution model is a separate question."}
/>

A **parent order**, also called a metaorder, is the whole intended purchase or sale. **Child orders** are the individual instructions used to execute it. Let $Q$ be the filled share quantity, $P_0$ the arrival benchmark and $\bar P=\sum_i q_iP_i/Q$ the share-weighted average fill. All quantities in this first case fill; there is no unfilled-order opportunity cost.

Write $\varepsilon=+1$ for a buy and $-1$ for a sell. Two useful signed returns are

$$
I_{\mathrm{end}}=\varepsilon\frac{P_{\mathrm{end}}-P_0}{P_0},\qquad
c_{\mathrm{fill}}=\varepsilon\frac{\bar P-P_0}{P_0}.
$$

A positive $c_{\mathrm{fill}}$ is adverse execution relative to arrival: buys paid more or sells received less. A **basis point** is $0.0001$ in return units, so multiply a return by 10,000 to express it in basis points. The monetary fill shortfall, excluding fees, is

$$
C=\varepsilon Q(\bar P-P_0)=P_0Q c_{\mathrm{fill}}.
$$

Buy 100,000 shares with $P_0=100$, average fill 100.40 and completion reference 100.60 USD per share:

For a sell at average 99.60 and completion reference 99.40, the sign convention gives the same adverse 40-basis-point average cost and 60-basis-point completion move. Receiving less is a cost to the seller. A favourable average sale at 100.20 would instead give a negative shortfall of −20 basis points.

**Pitfall:** treating every arrival-to-completion move as the causal effect of this order. News, market exposure, the signal motivating the trade and overlapping orders can move the benchmark too. Attribution needs a comparison model or identification strategy; the cash arithmetic alone does not provide one.

### When to use it

Specify benchmark, side, price source, averaging weights and measurement time before comparing estimates. Empirical studies may use completion impact, a maximum during execution or average fills. “Peak” is a shorthand in some stylised monotone paths, not proof that the final price is the largest observed price.

## A square-root endpoint fit is not an average execution bill

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"Keep the stipulated cumulative square-root fill path and double Q. What happens to total USD impact cost?"}
 options={[{"text": "It multiplies by 2√2, because average impact also rises by √2", "correct": true}, {"text": "It multiplies only by √2, because quantity is not part of cash cost"}, {"text": "It falls per share because the response curve is concave"}]}
 explanation={"Cash cost equals arrival price × quantity × average impact. Concavity of an endpoint response does not make total cost concave."}
/>

An empirical approximation often used for the magnitude of a parent order's completion response is

$$
I_{\mathrm{end}}(Q)\approx Y\sigma_D\sqrt{Q/V_D}.
$$

Here $\sigma_D$ is daily return volatility as a decimal, $V_D$ a specified daily-volume denominator, $Q$ order quantity in the same units and $Y$ a fitted dimensionless coefficient. A 2% daily volatility means 0.02; it is not 2 currency units. The output is a return. Multiplying by $P_0$ converts it to a price change, not yet a total execution cost.

[Zarinelli and co-authors](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152) study completion responses and find limits to the square-root fit, including dependence on duration and participation. It is a useful approximation to calibrate and test, not a universal price law or an exact description of every execution path. Their historical sample is not a forecast for today's instrument.

With $Y=1$, $\sigma_D=0.02$, $V_D=1{,}000{,}000$ shares and $Q=100{,}000$ shares,

$$
I_{\mathrm{end}}=0.02\sqrt{0.10}\approx0.00632456\approx63.24555320\text{ basis points}.
$$

At $P_0=100$, the corresponding completion price change is about 0.63245553 USD per share. It does not follow that every share paid that change.

### Add a path assumption before averaging

For the next calculation **separately assume** that each infinitesimal fill at cumulative quantity $q$ pays impact return $I(q)=Y\sigma_D\sqrt{q/V_D}$, relative to an otherwise constant arrival price. There is no spread, news, decay or other fill concession. An empirical endpoint formula alone does not imply this trajectory.

The average over the shares is the area under that path divided by $Q$:

$$
\bar I(Q)=\frac1Q\int_0^QY\sigma_D\sqrt{q/V_D}\,dq
=\frac23Y\sigma_D\sqrt{Q/V_D}=\frac23I(Q).
$$

The integral uses $\int\sqrt q\,dq=(2/3)q^{3/2}$. Thus this invented execution has average impact **42.16370214 basis points**, and cash cost

$$
C(Q)=P_0Q\bar I(Q)\approx100(100{,}000)(0.004216370214)=42{,}163.70\text{ USD}.
$$

Doubling quantity multiplies both return measures by $\sqrt2$, but total cash cost by $2\sqrt2$. The function $I(Q)$ is **concave**: its slope decreases. The total cost $C(Q)$ is proportional to $Q^{3/2}$ and is **convex**. Average cost per share rises; it does not fall because the response curve flattens. In this coherent path model, $dC/dQ=P_0I(Q)$ also rises with quantity.

**Pitfall:** comparing a square-root curve with an arbitrary linear slope and calling one “reality”. A linear comparison must be calibrated to the same observation or declared coefficient. Even then, neither shape is established as universally correct. The mathematical slope of $\sqrt Q$ is infinite at zero while its value tends to zero; that does not predict an infinite bill for a tiny order or remove the spread floor.

### When to use it

Ask whether the fitted coefficient describes an endpoint return, average fill slippage or total currency cost. The factor two thirds belongs to the explicitly stipulated fill path. It is neither a universal average/peak ratio nor a universal long-run permanent-impact fraction.

## Daily-volume fraction and execution participation use different denominators

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"A parent is 10 % of daily volume. Must it also be 10 % of market volume during its own execution?"}
 options={[{"text": "No: participation uses a separate window denominator", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes: daily fraction and participation are the same name for one ratio"}, {"text": "No: participation must always exceed 100 % for large orders"}]}
 explanation={"With a window equal to 20 % of daily volume, the order participates at 50 %, including its own fills."}
/>

**Daily-volume fraction** is $x=Q/V_D$. **Participation** in a specified execution window is $\rho=Q/V_{\mathrm{window}}$, where the window denominator here is total market volume **including our own fills**. A broker using an excluding-own-volume convention would have to label and convert it.

Take the same 100,000-share order and 1,000,000-share daily-volume reference. It is 10% of daily volume. If total volume while it trades is 200,000 shares, participation is $100{,}000/200{,}000=50\%$. If the window contains 500,000 shares instead, participation is 20%. The order's daily fraction stayed 10%.

<ImpactCostLab client:visible mode="horizon" labels={{"title": "Same daily fraction, different participation", "caption": "Invented daily reference 1,000,000 shares. Window market volume includes the order itself, so controls keep order quantity no greater than window volume. Daily-volume fraction and window participation are different percentages; the window is a volume amount, not necessarily a clock-time fraction."}} />

Daily volume may be a trailing average known at arrival, a forecast or a realised daily total used later in a study. Those are different data inputs. A live forecast using tomorrow's realised denominator would leak information. State the window and whether the denominator includes this order; participation under our definition cannot exceed 100%.

### Do not mix horizon conventions silently

The daily formula uses a daily calibration. Replacing $V_D$ with one quarter of daily volume while retaining daily volatility changes the quantity being computed. Under the **additional hypothetical assumptions** of constant volume rate and Brownian volatility scaling, quarter-day volatility is $0.02\sqrt{0.25}=0.01$ and volume is 250,000 shares. Then

$$
0.01\sqrt{100{,}000/250{,}000}=0.00632456,
$$

the same number as the daily normalisation. This cancellation is algebra under those assumptions, not proof that duration is irrelevant in actual executions. Intraday volume patterns, participation, liquidity, volatility and signal decay can break that simplification. [The impact-surface study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2152) explicitly examines duration and participation rather than relying only on order size.

### When to use it

Use aligned quantity units, volatility horizon and a coefficient calibrated to the chosen definition. A dollar-volume ratio can be suitable if both numerator and denominator use consistent valuation conventions; dividing share quantity by dollar volume is dimensionally wrong.

## A post-trade residual is not identified permanent impact

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"A price is still 30 basis points above arrival an hour after a buy. Does that observation alone identify 30 basis points of permanent impact?"}
 options={[{"text": "No: transient response, news and correlated flow can remain", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes: one hour is a universal separation point"}, {"text": "Yes: every move remaining after completion is permanently caused by this order"}]}
 explanation={"A measurement horizon and an attribution model are necessary. In our toy curve, 10 of the 30 bp remain transient at minute 60."}
/>

A response can **decay** after trading stops. Execution models distinguish an immediately paid temporary concession, a transient state that relaxes over time, and a permanent component stipulated to persist. These are model components. Information can influence prices, but a delayed price observation does not by itself identify a lasting causal information effect.

Use a deliberately simple post-trade response curve, in basis points:

$$
r(t)=20+40\,2^{-t/30},\qquad t\text{ in minutes after completion}.
$$

The chosen persistent component is 20 basis points and the transient part starts at 40. Its **half-life** is 30 minutes: every 30 minutes the remaining transient part halves.

At minute 60, 30 basis points remain. They are not all permanent even in this model: 10 are still transient. At a finite observation time, a lower response could also reflect news or correlated selling. A plateau in raw averages can reflect later correlated orders. [Brokmann and co-authors](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.3390) explain why separating correlated flow and predictability changes the inferred decay; that separation itself depends on assumptions.

**Pitfall:** expecting a later reversal to refund earlier execution. A completed buy at average 100.40 still spent that cash. A later mark of 100.20 changes the position's marked value; it does not rewrite the historical fill. Neither waiting a fixed number of minutes nor hiding an order guarantees that impact disappears.

### When to use it

For transaction-cost analysis, retain the entire time profile, market adjustment and overlapping-order assumptions. For execution planning, distinguish a model's recovery time from actual available liquidity. Do not label every movement that remains after an arbitrary wait “permanent”.

## Slicing changes cost only when it changes the execution conditions

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"You split exactly the same cumulative execution path into four accounting labels. What resets the impact state?"}
 options={[{"text": "Nothing: labels alone do not change the path or its integral", "correct": true}, {"text": "The fourth label automatically erases all previous trades"}, {"text": "Concavity mathematically guarantees independent liquidity for every child order"}]}
 explanation={"A reset needs an additional mechanism and enough actual time. It cannot be created by applying the parent formula to each label independently."}
/>

Suppose the 100,000-share parent follows the cumulative square-root fill path above. Writing four child-order labels on that **same trajectory** does not reset the market. Each segment costs the area over its cumulative interval:

$$
C_j=P_0\int_{q_{j-1}}^{q_j}I(q)\,dq,\qquad \sum_jC_j=P_0\int_0^QI(q)\,dq.
$$

Their sum is still 42,163.70 USD. Applying $I(Q/4)$ independently to each connected segment silently adds a reset that the model did not contain.

Now stipulate **full recovery to the same unchanged baseline between four equal batches**, identical future liquidity, no spread or extra fees, and enough time. Each batch then starts the fill-impact path at zero. Average impact becomes $42.16370214/\sqrt4=21.08185107$ basis points. That different hypothetical environment costs 21,081.85 USD in impact alone.

If waiting carries a separately assumed 10-basis-point expected charge per extra batch, three waits add 30 basis points. Reset impact plus that charge becomes **51.08185107 basis points**, or **51,081.85 USD**. Waiting is worse than the connected 42.16370214-basis-point case under this chosen scoring model. The charge is an invented expected cost, not a volatility estimate or a law of delay.

<ImpactCostLab client:visible mode="slicing" labels={{"title": "Accounting slices versus an assumed full reset", "caption": "Invented 100,000-share order, arrival 100 USD and the stipulated square-root fill path. Renaming the same cumulative trajectory leaves its average impact unchanged. The reset comparison additionally assumes full independent recovery, unchanged baseline and identical liquidity between batches. The apparent zero-impact limit with infinitely many fully independent batches relies on unlimited time and repeated free recovery. Deadlines, spread, fees, stochastic prices, information decay and limited replenishment can invalidate it. Concavity alone is not a reset mechanism.

### Linear price concessions can produce convex monetary cost

A separate temporary-rate model makes the units explicit. Trade at rate $v$ shares per minute for $\Delta t$ minutes, with average price concession per share $h(v)=\eta v$. Then

$$
n=v\Delta t,\qquad C_{\mathrm{temp}}=n h(v)=\eta v^2\Delta t=\eta n^2/\Delta t.
$$

Here $\eta$ has units USD·minute/share². Set $\eta=0.001$, $n=100$ shares. Trading over one minute gives $v=100$, concession 0.10 USD/share and cost 10 USD. Over two minutes it gives $v=50$, concession 0.05 and cost 5 USD. The **per-share concession is linear in rate**; monetary cost is quadratic in rate for fixed duration. You do not need superlinear per-share impact for this convex cost.

<ImpactCostLab client:visible mode="rate" labels={{"title": "Linear concession per share, quadratic rate cost", "caption": "Separate invented temporary-rate model: exactly 100 shares, eta=0.001 USD·minute/share², constant rate over the chosen actual duration, no spread, permanent response, drift or timing-risk charge. Accounting labels partition that same rate and duration; they do not change cost. The curve is shown only for durations from 1 to 10 minutes."}} />

This distinction appears in [Almgren and Chriss, sections 1.3–1.5](https://smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/optliq.pdf). Merely splitting the same one-minute rate into four accounting intervals still costs $4[0.001(100)^2(0.25)]=10$ USD. Spending more actual time at a lower rate changes the modelled cost, while creating timing exposure that this impact-only number excludes.

### When to use it

Specify replenishment, transient-state dynamics, rate limits, completion deadline and the objective before comparing schedules. The next lesson's algorithms decide schedules; this lesson supplies the quantities they must keep consistent.

## Capacity depends on the cost definition and the objective

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"Your model has a positive-net-edge region ending at break-even. Is its boundary necessarily the maximum expected dollar-profit trade?"}
 options={[{"text": "No: the quantity multiplying net edge changes the dollar objective", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes: a zero per-unit net edge maximises total profit"}, {"text": "Yes: available funding can never alter a model optimum"}]}
 explanation={"Break-even gives zero expected profit here. The unconstrained maximum is at 4/9 of that quantity, conditional on the stated path and fixed edge."}
/>

Let $a$ be a fixed assumed gross edge **per traded dollar**, not a fixed pot of profit. Let $c_0$ be other one-way costs. In the stipulated square-root fill-path model, $\kappa=(2/3)Y\sigma_D$ and expected net edge is

$$
n(x)=a-c_0-\kappa\sqrt x,\qquad x=Q/V_D.
$$

The inputs must refer to the same trade direction, horizon and notional. A round trip needs both legs and their assumptions; subtracting one-way costs from a round-trip edge is not this model.

Use $a=30$ basis points, $c_0=5$ basis points, $Y=1$ and $\sigma_D=2\%$. Thus $a-c_0=0.0025$ and $\kappa=0.01333333\ldots$. Average-cost break-even occurs at

$$
x_{\mathrm{BE}}=\left(\frac{a-c_0}{\kappa}\right)^2
=0.03515625=3.515625\%.
$$

At that point expected net edge is zero. Below it the stipulated net edge is positive, not guaranteed realised profit. With no other cost, the average-cost threshold would be 5.0625%. A **different** convention charging the full endpoint impact as cost would give 2.25% for the 30-basis-point edge; neither convention can silently stand in for the other.

### Positive net edge is not maximum expected total profit

Under the same assumptions, total expected profit is

$$
\Pi(Q)=P_0Q\left[a-c_0-\kappa\sqrt{Q/V_D}\right].
$$

Differentiating gives $d\Pi/dQ=P_0[a-c_0-(3/2)\kappa\sqrt{Q/V_D}]$. For positive available edge and unconstrained continuous quantity,

$$
x_* =\left(\frac{2(a-c_0)}{3\kappa}\right)^2=\frac49x_{\mathrm{BE}}=1.5625\%.
$$

At $V_D=1{,}000{,}000$ shares, this is 15,625 shares. Average impact is $16.66666667$ basis points, net edge $30-5-16.66666667=8.33333333$ basis points, and expected profit is $100(15{,}625)(0.0008333333\ldots)\approx1{,}302.08$ USD. At the larger break-even quantity, total expected profit is zero.

If $a\le c_0$, no positive quantity has positive net edge in this model; cash/no trade is feasible unless a mandate says otherwise. Funding, inventory, risk and liquidity constraints can change the feasible optimum. Estimated alpha and impact parameters can also be wrong. The square relationship between available edge and threshold is conditional algebra, not a universal capacity forecast.

### When to use it

Choose whether the objective is positive expected return per unit, maximum expected currency profit, completion of a required order or a risk-adjusted score. Recalibrate costs and test adverse scenarios before treating a threshold as a deployable size.
