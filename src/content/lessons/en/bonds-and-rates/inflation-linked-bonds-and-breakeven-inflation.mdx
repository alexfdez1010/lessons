A bond can adjust its payments for inflation and still fall in market price. It can return at least its original principal at maturity and still leave a buyer with a loss. Those statements fit together once we track **the index rule, the price paid and the date of the outcome**.

An **inflation-linked bond** adjusts specified cash flows using a price index and contractual rules. We will use US Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, **TIPS**, for the named instrument mechanics. The UK comparison has its own terms. All numerical cases are invented, amounts use USD unless stated, and issuer payments are assumed to occur as contracted. Taxes, fees and currency conversion enter only when discussed explicitly.

## 1. Apply the index ratio to principal and coupon cash

Think of the original principal as a fixed number of baskets. The index ratio changes the currency price assigned to those baskets. It does not change the contractual coupon rate into “coupon plus inflation” paid as one extra cash transfer.

Let F be **original face**, c the annual coupon rate as a decimal, I₀ the original reference index and I_t the reference index for a payment date. The **index ratio** K_t is dimensionless:

$$
K_t=\frac{I_t}{I_0},\qquad A_t=F K_t,\qquad C_t=\frac c2 F K_t.
$$

A_t is adjusted principal before a maturity floor; C_t is one regular half-year coupon. [Treasury’s payment instructions multiply original face by the payment-date ratio, then by half the fixed annual coupon rate](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/auctions/tips-cpi-data/).

### A fixed rate produces different cash coupons

For F = 1,000, c = 2 %, I₀ = 100 and I_t = 103:

- K_t = 103 / 100 = **1.03**.
- Adjusted principal = 1,000 × 1.03 = **1,030**.
- Half-year coupon = 1,030 × 0.02 / 2 = **10.30**.

If the next payment-date reference is 101, the next coupon is 1,000 × 1.01 × 0.02 / 2 = **10.10**. The annual coupon rate stayed 2 %. Neither 30 nor the earlier rise in adjusted principal was a separate cash coupon paid to the holder.

### When to use it

Use the payment-date ratio, original face and stated frequency to construct the scheduled cash flow. Different indexed instruments can adjust different payments. The original face is also different from **market price**, the cash value at which the bond trades, and from the buyer’s full settlement outlay. The [previous settlement lesson](/bonds-and-rates/accrued-interest-clean-and-dirty-prices) explains that price conventions and accrued interest need their own reconciliation.

## 2. Follow the reference lag rather than today's shopping bill

TIPS use a specified version of the **Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, CPI-U, not seasonally adjusted**. Seasonal adjustment removes estimated seasonal patterns; the contractual series here does not do that. It is not a personal household’s cost index.

The **reference lag** means a payment-date calculation draws on earlier monthly observations. For TIPS, the first day of a month uses the CPI for the third preceding month. April 1 therefore uses January’s index; May 1 uses February’s. Other April dates interpolate between those anchors. The [current Treasury rule specifies the lag, interpolation and published truncation/rounding](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-II/subchapter-A/part-356/appendix-Appendix%20B%20to%20Part%20356).

### An invented April 16 calculation

Suppose January’s index is 100 and February’s is 102. April has 30 days. With d the April calendar day, the interpolation before official rounding is

$$
I_d=100+\frac{d-1}{30}(102-100).
$$

On April 16, the weight is (16 − 1) / 30 = **0.5**, so reference index = 100 + 0.5 × 2 = **101**. With original reference 100, K = 1.01. For our 1,000 face and 2 % annual coupon, a payment on that illustrative date would use adjusted principal 1,010 and coupon **10.10**.

April 30 is not the next anchor: its weight is 29/30, giving reference **101.933333** before rounding. February’s full 102 becomes the May 1 anchor. The day count matters even when the monthly labels look close enough to tempt a shortcut.

### When to use it

Read the index version, base date, lag and rounding method before estimating cash. Official TIPS reference CPI and ratios use specified six-decimal truncation followed by five-decimal rounding; our visual isolates interpolation before those publication steps. Use the published instrument ratio for an actual payment. A recent grocery-price rise need not enter this payment yet, and the chosen CPI need not match your spending basket.

## 3. Identify what the maturity floor actually protects

A minimum refund tied to the original ticket does not reimburse every resale price. [TIPS repay the larger of adjusted principal and original principal at maturity](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/tips/). The protection attaches to that maturity principal payment, not to every interim adjusted amount, coupon or secondary-market purchase price.

In our model the maturity principal M and final coupon are

$$
M=F\max(K_T,1),\qquad C_T=\frac c2 F K_T.
$$

For F = 1,000, c = 2 % and final K_T = 0.95, adjusted principal is 950. Maturity principal is **1,000**, including a 50 floor addition. The final coupon is still 950 × 0.02 / 2 = **9.50**, not 10. The final combined receipt is **1,009.50**.

### A buyer can pay more than that protected amount

Suppose exactly one final half-year remains. Buy just after a coupon, with purchase index ratio 1.10 and a real clean quote of 102 per 100 original face. “Real clean” is the quote before index scaling and accrued interest. There is no accrued interest at our chosen date:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\mathrm{cash}}&=1000\times1.10\times\frac{102}{100}=1122,\\
\text{Final gain}&=1009.50-1122=-112.50,\\
R_{\mathrm{halfyear}}&=\frac{-112.50}{1122}\approx-10.026738\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

There are no other receipts, reinvestment, fees or taxes in this final-half-year case. The percentage is **not annualized**. The original-principal floor was honored and the buyer still lost cash.

### When to use it

Use the floor clause to model the exact protected payment. Do not transfer it across countries: [the UK DMO states that index-linked gilts do not have this deflation floor](https://www.dmo.gov.uk/help/glossary). They reference the UK Retail Prices Index, RPI; legacy and newer designs can have different lags. [The DMO distinguishes the newer three-month design from earlier issues](https://www.dmo.gov.uk/data/gilt-market/index-linked-gilts/). Check the particular issue rather than treating “inflation-linked” as one worldwide contract.

## 4. Discount real cash flows and retain real-rate price risk

<MCQ

A **real yield** discounts payments expressed in units of the chosen inflation index. A **coupon rate** determines payment amounts from face. They answer different questions, just as nominal coupon and nominal yield did earlier.

To isolate real-rate sensitivity, now use a simplified **exactly indexed bond with no maturity floor**, at a coupon date. The floor option is omitted entirely from this pricing model; it is not silently valued at zero for every real TIPS. Index lag, taxes, fees and rounding are also omitted. Let y_r be a nominal annual real yield compounded twice yearly, and N the number of remaining half-year payments:

$$
P_{\mathrm{real}}=
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{Fc/2}{(1+y_r/2)^j}
+\frac{F}{(1+y_r/2)^N}.
$$

“Nominal annual” describes the rate-quotation convention here, not nominal purchasing units. The real yield is divided by two for each half-year. This section’s rate convention differs from the annual-effective rates in the next comparison.

### Reprice two remaining payments

For face 1,000 and 2 % annual coupon, the remaining real-unit cash flows are 10 at half a year and 1,010 at one year. At y_r = 2 %, price is 10/1.01 + 1,010/1.01² = **1,000**. At y_r = 4 %:

$$
P_{\mathrm{real}}=\frac{10}{1.02}+\frac{1010}{1.02^2}
\approx 9.803922+970.780469=980.584391.
$$

That is a **1.941561 % price decline** with unchanged real-unit cash flows. Hold the current valuation index ratio fixed at 1.05: nominal cash values at that instant are 1,000 × 1.05 = **1,050** and 980.584391 × 1.05 = **1,029.613610**. Nothing in this instantaneous comparison assumes that the index falls.

### When to use it

Use a real-yield curve to examine real discount-rate exposure, with appropriate instrument adjustments. A bond’s inflation indexation does not fix its resale price when real yields change. Positive coupon rates also do not prevent negative quoted real yields: [Treasury permits negative real-yield bids for TIPS](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/tips/tips-negative/). In our unfloored example, y_r = −1 % gives real-unit price **1,030.226509**, above face. A quoted yield is a valuation rate for specified cash flows, not an unconditional personal purchasing-power outcome.

## 5. Derive an exact breakeven for a matched ideal comparison

A **breakeven inflation rate** names the inflation assumption at which two stipulated alternatives tie. To derive one cleanly, use two ideal zero-coupon investments: same issuer, currency, initial outlay, horizon and annual-effective rate convention; one terminal payment each; exactly matched indexation with no binding floor, lag, taxes or costs. This is a comparison model, not a claim that coupon-paying TIPS have one universal exact yield-subtraction rule.

With initial wealth W, nominal yield n, real yield r, constant annual inflation π and common horizon T years:

$$
V_N=W(1+n)^T,\qquad
V_L=W(1+r)^T(1+\pi)^T.
$$

Equality of terminal cash, with all factors positive, gives

$$
1+\pi^*=\frac{1+n}{1+r},\qquad
\pi^*=\frac{n-r}{1+r}.
$$

The familiar n − r drops the denominator adjustment. It is an approximation for this annual-effective comparison, not the exact result.

### Four percent versus one percent

For W = 1,000, n = 4 %, r = 1 % and T = 1, the nominal alternative pays **1,040**. Exact breakeven is 1.04/1.01 − 1 = **2.970297 %**, rather than exactly 3 %. At stipulated inflation 3 %, linked cash is 1,000 × 1.01 × 1.03 = **1,040.30**: the extra 0.30 is the cross-product term that simple subtraction missed.

### When to use it

Use the exact factor comparison when these matched assumptions describe the decision. With coupon bonds, payment dates, purchase prices, reinvestment, index lag, maturity-floor effects and taxes can alter the realized tie. Matching maturity alone does not make every cash-flow pattern identical. Selling early also introduces the sale price. Even the correct ideal threshold is a **conditional indifference calculation**, not a prediction of future inflation or a personal recommendation.

## 6. Read market compensation without turning it into a forecast

Market **inflation compensation** is commonly quoted as a matched-convention nominal Treasury yield minus a comparable TIPS yield. That observed yield difference is a quote definition. It differs from deriving an exact terminal-wealth threshold for our ideal annual-effective zeros.

The [Federal Reserve’s model discussion separates expected inflation, inflation-risk compensation and a TIPS liquidity component](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/tips-from-tips-update-and-discussions-20190521.html). A risk premium compensates exposure; liquidity concerns the ease and cost of trading. Under the note’s stated additive model convention,

$$
b\approx E[\pi]+\mathrm{IRP}-\mathrm{LP}.
$$

IRP is the inflation-risk premium. LP is the additional TIPS yield attributed to its relative liquidity component. Components are estimated under assumptions; the decomposition is not an exact universal law and IRP need not always be positive.

### The same spread fits different component stories

An invented nominal quote 4 % minus TIPS quote 1.5 % gives **2.5 percentage points**. The assumed components 2.2 + 0.5 − 0.2 = 2.5 fit it. So do 2.2 + 0.8 − 0.5 = 2.5. If only LP rises from 0.2 to 0.5, holding the first example’s other components fixed, the spread falls to **2.2 points** without changing expected inflation 2.2.

### When to use it

Use the spread as evidence to investigate alongside surveys, liquidity conditions and the chosen curve conventions. Check currency, index, maturity, compounding, timing and whether you compare fitted curves or individual bonds. For an actual holding decision, also price the relevant cash flows, floor and costs. Tax timing, reinvested coupons, personal inflation and home-currency conversion can make a spending outcome differ from a quoted real yield. A market price contains useful information and still leaves multiple explanations open.
