A bond screen says 98.4. Your holding has face value 1,000, yet the pre-fee settlement amount is 998.917127. Neither multiplication nor the broker has necessarily gone wrong: the quote may exclude the coupon interest accrued between payment dates.

Earlier examples bought just after a coupon date. This lesson opens the space between those dates. **Settlement** is the exchange of the securities and payment; the **trade date** is when the deal is agreed. Accrual and entitlement must follow the actual instrument's settlement conventions, not an assumed universal delay.

We use invented, regular, fixed nominal coupon bonds, no default, fees or taxes, and the precise payment rights stated in each example. Amounts use one unspecified currency throughout. These are arithmetic teaching models, not live prices or a recommendation.

## A quote per 100 face is not your cash outlay

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A clean quote is 98.4 per 100 face and you buy face 1,000. A price quote can be expressed as a percentage of **face value**, the principal base specified by the bond. A quote of 100 means 100 per 100 face, or at par. A quote of 98.4 means 98.4% of face. Check the screen: not every market uses the same quotation format or excludes accrued interest.

A **clean price** excludes the stated accrued-interest adjustment. A **dirty price**, also called full price in this context, includes it. Neither word says anything about honesty, credit quality or cleanliness of the issuer's accounts. [LSEG's fixed-income glossary defines clean, dirty and day-count terms](https://www.lseg.com/content/dam/ftse-russell/en_us/documents/policy-documents/glossary-of-terms-ftse-fixed-income.pdf).

Keep the units consistent:

$$
P_{\mathrm{clean,cash}}=F\frac{q_{\mathrm{clean}}}{100},\qquad
P_{\mathrm{dirty,cash}}=P_{\mathrm{clean,cash}}+AI_{\mathrm{cash}}.
$$

Here $F$ is the holding's face amount, $q$ is a quote per 100 face and $AI$ is signed accrued interest. If accrued interest is instead quoted per 100 face:

$$
q_{\mathrm{dirty}}=q_{\mathrm{clean}}+AI_{\mathrm{per 100}},\qquad
AI_{\mathrm{per 100}}=100\frac{AI_{\mathrm{cash}}}{F}.
$$

For face 1,000, quote 98.4 gives clean cash 984. If signed accrued cash is 14.917127, dirty cash is 998.917127. The accrued quote is 1.4917127 per 100 and dirty quote 99.8917127. Adding 14.917127 directly to 98.4 would mix cash with quotation units.

### When to use the distinction

Use it to budget the settlement payment and reconcile a contract note. Separately add any commissions, taxes or other invoice items. Do not add accrual again if the quoted figure already includes it. In a consistent valuation model, the dirty price values the cash flows to which the buyer is entitled at settlement; clean price is that value less the agreed accrual adjustment. Clean does not mean the position's full economic value.

## Accrual uses a coupon-period fraction and the settlement date

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A bond pays 6% a year in two equal coupons. If half of one regular coupon period has passed, is accrued interest 6% of face?" hint="First find one period's coupon, then the elapsed share of that period." text="No. Each regular coupon is face× 6%/2. Half of that coupon is face× 1.5%. Multiplying the full annual coupon by a half-period fraction would double-count the frequency." />

Before the next coupon detaches, the usual cum-coupon model has the buyer receive the whole next coupon. The purchase's accrued-interest adjustment compensates the seller for the elapsed part of that coupon period. **Cum-coupon** means the next coupon is included in the transferred payment rights.

For a regular period with equal coupons:

$$
C=F\frac{c}{m},\qquad AI=C\frac{d}{D}.
$$

$c$ is the annual coupon rate, $m$ the number of equal coupons per year, $d$ elapsed days and $D$ days in that period **under the specified day-count convention**. It is a linear contractual allocation of a coupon, not compound interest on the buyer's purchase price. [CFA Institute explains between-coupon pricing and common actual/actual and 30/360 conventions](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/fixed-income-bond-valuation-prices-and-yields).

### Work actual calendar days and a stated 30/360 comparison

Take face 1,000, annual fixed coupon 6%, two equal payments per year. The regular coupon period is 1 January to 1 July 2026; settlement is 1 April 2026, the next coupon belongs to the buyer, and we count from the start date up to but excluding settlement.

Under the **actual/actual coupon-period rule specified here**, count 90 actual days elapsed and 181 actual days in the full period. Therefore:

$$
C=1000\times0.06/2=30,
$$
$$
AI_{\mathrm{Act/Act}}=30\times90/181=14.917127\ldots,
$$
$$
P_{\mathrm{dirty}}=984+14.917127\ldots=998.917127\ldots.
$$

For a separate **regular 30/360 example on those same dates**, use 30-day months: elapsed 90 days and full period 180. No end-of-month or February adjustment is needed for these first-of-month dates. Then $AI=30\times90/180=15$, and dirty cash is 999. Different contracts can use different variants; this example is not an algorithm for all 30/360 or actual/actual cases.

Both cases schedule the same coupon 30. Only its settlement allocation differs. A convention is a rule for this calculation, not a claim that the calendar year literally has 360 days. The year, leap years, irregular coupons and specific instrument terms can matter. Using trade date when settlement differs can also change $d$ and payment entitlement.

### When to use the fraction

Read the security's coupon calendar, settlement date and precise day-count rule. Check whether you are inside an ex-coupon period before applying the positive-accrual formula. Do not replace every denominator with 365 or multiply an annual rate by $d/D$ when $D$ describes just one half-year coupon period.

## Receiving the whole coupon is not earning the whole coupon

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="You buy just before a coupon, paying its positive accrued interest. Does receiving the whole coupon make that whole amount a gain since your purchase?" options={[{text:'No; part was already paid to the seller through the dirty price',correct:true},{text:'Yes; settlement accrual is unrelated to the cash you paid'},{text:'Yes; a coupon payment is always a guaranteed profit'},{text:'No; buyers never receive a full scheduled coupon'}]} explanation="A receipt is a cash inflow. Gain also subtracts the actual outlay and reflects the remaining bond's value, fees, taxes and any other flows." />

Buying a cake with most slices already allocated does not create extra cake. The buyer may receive the next entire coupon, but the dirty outlay includes the seller's accrued share. Receiving cash and earning a return are different calculations.

Use the same regular period of 181 days and coupon 30. For this invented comparison, settle after 175 days, with six days left. Set clean cash at 1,000 before purchase and again immediately after the next coupon; assume full punctual payment and no other flows, costs or market changes. This clean-price equality is stipulated to isolate the allocation; it is not a prediction that real clean prices stay fixed.

$$
AI=30\frac{175}{181}=29.005525\ldots,
$$
$$
\text{Purchase outlay}=1000+AI=1029.005525\ldots.
$$

At the coupon date the buyer receives 30 and the remaining bond is worth 1,000 under our assumption. Final combined value is 1,030:

$$
\text{Gain}=1030-1029.005525\ldots
=30\frac{6}{181}=0.994475\ldots.
$$

The six-day holding-period return is approximately $0.994475/1029.005525=0.0966443\%$, without annualisation. It is neither the full coupon 30 nor a 6% holding-period return. A change in the clean price, default, fees or taxes would change that result.

### When to compare outcomes

Compare changes in **dirty position value plus actual cash distributions**, subtracting the dirty purchase outlay and relevant costs. A scheduled coupon mechanically leaving a position does not itself create or destroy that amount of combined wealth, holding other valuation inputs fixed. Do not treat a clean-price chart as a total-return series, or an accrued-interest line as a separate extra return to add twice.

## Payment entitlement can change the sign, and some bonds have no periodic coupon

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="Must dirty price always exceed clean price?" hint="That requires the signed accrued adjustment to be positive. Must the buyer always receive the next coupon?" text="No. Some ex-coupon conventions leave that coupon with the seller and use a negative accrual or rebate adjustment. A regular zero-coupon bond has no periodic coupon to allocate. The instrument's rights and pricing conventions decide." />

**Ex-coupon** means the upcoming coupon is excluded from the transferred rights under that instrument's rules. Do not infer entitlement just from being an owner on payment day. The UK Debt Management Office describes an explicit **conventional gilt** arrangement in which settlement during the relevant ex-dividend period leaves the upcoming coupon with the seller and negative accrued interest, or rebate interest, reduces the purchase price. Its rules are specific to those securities, and even settlement restrictions can differ near final maturity. [Read the DMO's current explanation of gilt entitlement and rebate interest](https://dmo.gov.uk/responsibilities/gilt-market/about-gilts/).

### Work one invented ex-coupon allocation

To illustrate that signed adjustment, suppose a non-final regular coupon period has $C=30$, $D=181$ and $d=178$. **Stipulate** that the seller retains the whole upcoming coupon, and that this model subtracts the full coupon from ordinary accrued interest:

$$
AI_{\mathrm{ex}}=C\left(\frac{d}{D}-1\right)
=-C\frac{D-d}{D}=-30\frac{3}{181}=-0.497238\ldots.
$$

With clean cash 984, dirty cash is $984-0.497238\ldots=983.502762\ldots$. The buyer receives **zero of this next coupon**; the seller receives 30. The rebate allocates the remaining three days under this stated model. A negative adjustment is not an issuer paying negative interest, and it is not a universal ex-coupon formula for every instrument.

<BondAccruedPrice client:visible title="A stipulated ex-coupon adjustment is negative" elapsedDays={178} nextCouponEntitled={false} />

For a regular **zero-coupon bond**, set $c=0$, so this periodic-coupon accrued interest is zero. A clean cash price 984 then gives dirty cash 984 before costs in this convention. That does not mean its market value cannot change, its discount cannot accrete economically, or tax rules cannot recognise income before payment. Those are different questions.

### When to stop using the simple model

Check actual instrument documentation for entitlement dates, business-day adjustments, irregular first or last coupons, inflation-linked or floating amounts, default and flat trading, and fees or taxes. Our controls compare hypothetical assumptions; they do not determine which rights you can elect on a real trade. Full cash-flow valuation must include only the payments transferred to the buyer and the conventions applicable at settlement.
