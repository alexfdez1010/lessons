A company offers to pay interest and repay a stated amount in three years. Buying its bond makes you a creditor with a contract. The contract tells you what is owed. It does not, by itself, tell you what will actually arrive, what you could sell for tomorrow or what the money will buy when you receive it.

We will start with a deliberately simple model: a **fixed-rate nominal bullet bond**, with no conversion into shares and no early-redemption option. Fixed-rate means the coupon rate is specified and stays unchanged; nominal means payments are stated in currency units without an inflation adjustment; bullet means the principal is scheduled as one payment at maturity. Other structures come later in this lesson.

All numerical examples are invented, exclude fees and taxes, and use the stated currency. A scheduled-payment example assumes full payment on time. The actual-outcome cases state different assumptions explicitly.

## A bond makes you a creditor

Imagine lending a food-truck business \$1,000 under an agreement to receive \$50 interest a year and \$1,000 principal after three years. You have a claim for payment, not a share of the truck's future profits. A private loan is only an analogy: a **bond** is a debt security with its own legal terms, ownership records and transfer arrangements.

The **issuer** is the borrower that issues the bond, such as a company or government. The **bondholder** is the creditor entitled to the specified claim. In a **new issue**, the issuer raises money. If you buy an already-issued bond from another investor, your purchase payment goes to that seller; the debt claim changes hands. Buying an existing \$1,000-face bond for \$950 does not make the issuer promise to repay \$950 instead.

A bond's claim differs from an ordinary share's residual ownership claim. The shareholder's result depends on dividends, sale value and what remains for owners. A plain nonconvertible bond instead specifies debt payments. Its market price can rise or fall, and its actual recovery can be smaller than the amount owed. “Creditor” describes the claim, not a safety rating.

### When to use the distinction

Identify whose obligation you hold and which legal entity owes it. A familiar group name is not enough if the bond is issued by a particular subsidiary. Read the bond's terms rather than treating every security with the same brand as the same claim. [FINRA introduces the issuer–creditor relationship and the variety of debt terms](https://www.finra.org/investors/investing/investment-products/bonds).

## Read the payment terms and build the schedule

<ThinkReveal client:visible
  prompt="You pay 1,080 for a bond with face value 1,000. If it performs in full, does face repayment alone return your whole purchase price?"
  hint="Separate the contract's principal from your market outlay."
  text="No. Its scheduled principal is 1,000, not 1,080. Coupons may add income, but the premium purchase price is not itself protected by the face amount. The scheduled rate of return depends on the price and all payment dates."
/>

**Face value**, also called **par value**, is the reference principal amount written in the terms. **Principal** is the debt amount to be repaid under the repayment provisions. In our bullet example, the outstanding principal equals face until its scheduled repayment; amortizing and inflation-linked bonds can work differently. Denominations vary by market and issue: \$1,000 here is an example, not the definition of a bond.

The **coupon rate** is the annual contractual interest rate applied to the specified principal base. **Coupon frequency** tells you how many payments occur each year. Annual, semiannual and other schedules exist; the actual contract decides. The **maturity date** is when the stated final repayment is due. A bond originally issued for three years has less than three years remaining if you buy it later.

For a fixed coupon rate $c$, unchanged face $F$ and $m$ equal payments per year:

$$
C=\frac{Fc}{m}.
$$

A \$1,000-face bond with a 5% annual coupon paid twice a year has $C=1000(0.05)/2=25$ per six months. If three full years remain just after a coupon date, there are $3(2)=6$ coupons. Assume all are paid and there is no early redemption:

Changing the controls builds another hypothetical schedule; it does not reset the coupon of a bond you already own.

The coupons sum to $6(25)=150$. Scheduled principal is paid **once**, adding \$1,000: total cash \$1,150. If you paid \$1,080, your simple net cash amount over the schedule is $1150-1080=70$, assuming no costs or reinvestment. That money sum is not an annualized return and does not account for when individual coupons arrived.

For this fixed-rate model, changing your purchase price does not change the coupon. Paying \$950, \$1,000 or \$1,080 still gives \$25 per coupon if the contract performs. The coupon rate is therefore not generally the investor's yield or realized return. [The next lesson](/bonds-and-rates/bond-prices-and-yields) values the scheduled payments and distinguishes promised yield from actual outcomes.

### When to use the schedule

List the principal base, coupon formula, frequency, remaining dates, currency and early-redemption terms before making a cash plan. A **call option** lets an issuer redeem early under specified conditions; an **amortizing** bond repays principal in portions. Neither fits our unchanged bullet schedule. A contractual calendar can support planning, but a spending need also requires considering whether payments arrive and what happens if you need to sell first.

## Credit risk and seniority determine recovery

<MCQ client:visible pretest

    { text: "The government must reimburse any loss on a corporate bond" },

**Credit risk** is the risk that the issuer does not meet its payment obligations. A **default** is a failure covered by the debt terms, such as missing a due payment. **Recovery** is what the creditor actually receives after a default or restructuring; its amount, form and timing may differ from the original promise.

**Seniority** is the priority of one claim relative to others. A **secured** bond has specified collateral supporting its claim. An **unsecured** bond does not have that particular collateral claim; senior unsecured claims may rank ahead of **subordinated** claims. The bondholder is not necessarily first among all creditors. Legal entities, collateral value, other obligations, costs and applicable law matter. Secured does not mean the collateral will cover the whole debt.

### Work a deliberately simple priority example

Suppose one company has \$600 available for these claims **after all other costs and claims**, a senior debt claim of \$500 and a subordinated claim of \$500. Assume the stated strict priority and no other rights:

The senior claim uses \$500, leaving $600-500=100$ for the subordinated claim. Its recovery fraction is $100/500=20\%$, despite both being debt. If only \$300 were available, even the senior claim would recover just $300/500=60\%$ in the same simplified ordering. These are illustrations, not a universal bankruptcy waterfall. [Investor.gov describes secured, senior unsecured and subordinated priority and other competing creditors](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/what-are).

For a separate one-year investor example, pay \$1,000 for a bond promising \$50 interest and \$1,000 principal. The issuer defaults, pays **no interest**, and the investor receives \$750 recovery at year end. Same currency, no other receipts or costs:

$$
R_{\mathrm{actual}}=\frac{750-1000}{1000}=-25\%.
$$

### When to use credit and priority

Read the actual issuer, security and seniority provisions and how repayment could be funded. A rating is an opinion about credit risk, not insurance against loss. A bond of one issuer is not automatically safer than an ordinary share of a different issuer. Even within one issuer, priority does not prescribe the investor's return: purchase price, cash received and timing still matter. Holding until maturity does not remove default risk.

## Sale price, currency and inflation affect the investor

<ThinkReveal client:visible
  prompt="The issuer pays every promised dollar at maturity. Does that establish that every holder made a positive return in their spending currency?"
  hint="What price did the holder pay, and what happened to exchange rates and purchasing power?"
  text="No. A premium purchase price, currency movements and inflation can change the result. Earlier sale also uses the sale price rather than a future promised principal amount. Full issuer performance answers one part of the outcome, not all of it."
/>

### Selling early replaces the future claim with a market price

**Interest-rate risk** means changes in required market yields can change a bond's price. **Liquidity risk** means you may not be able to sell promptly at the price you expect. Credit conditions can change prices too. A future maturity payment is not today's guaranteed resale price.

Buy an annual-coupon bond for \$1,000. One year later, immediately after receiving a \$50 coupon, sell it for \$940. Assume no other cash adjustments or costs:

$$
R_{\mathrm{sale}}=\frac{50+940-1000}{1000}=-1\%.
$$

  homeCurrency="USD" foreignCurrency="USD"
  scopeLabel="Invented one-year holding: 50 coupon paid at the end, then sale for 940 just after the coupon, no other cash adjustments, fees or taxes. The sale price is a stated scenario, not a prediction from the coupon rate." />

Receiving the coupon did not prevent a loss. The later contractual maturity amount is no longer a receipt for this investor after the sale. The [SEC interest-rate-risk bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-86) explains why prices of otherwise unchanged fixed-rate bonds generally fall when required yields rise. That relationship does not mean every observed price change has a single cause.

### Full payment in one currency can mean a loss in another

Suppose you spend €1,000 when **€1 buys \$1** and buy a one-year \$1,000-face bond at par. The issuer fully pays \$50 interest plus \$1,000 principal at year end. At that time each dollar converts to **€0.90**, and all cash arrives at the end. No separate contract offsets the currency movement. Ignore conversion charges:

$$
\text{final euros}=1050(0.90)=945,\qquad
 R_{\mathrm{EUR}}=\frac{945}{1000}-1=-5.5\%.
$$

The USD cash return is +5%, while the EUR return is −5.5%. Currency denomination and the investor's spending currency are different pieces of information. [Investor.gov explains how exchange-rate movements can reverse an international investment's home-currency result](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/international-investing).

### Nominal repayment is different from purchasing power

**Inflation** is an increase in a price index; it reduces what a given amount buys relative to that index. A **real return** adjusts a nominal return for the same period's price change:

$$
1+R_{\mathrm{real}}=\frac{1+R_{\mathrm{nominal}}}{1+\pi}.
$$

If euro prices in the same one-year example rise 5%, the final €945 buys what €900 bought initially:

$$
\text{start-date purchasing power}=\frac{945}{1.05}=900,\qquad
 R_{\mathrm{real,EUR}}=\frac{900}{1000}-1=-10\%.
$$

Set the currency factor to one and inflation to zero to isolate the +5% nominal cash result. Then change one assumption at a time. This is an outcome reconciliation, not a model predicting currencies or inflation. The price index is the one stipulated for the investor's home spending comparison; personal spending can differ from that index.

### When to use the distinction

Specify the intended holding horizon, possible sale needs, payment currency and spending currency. A promise in fixed dollars is not a promise of fixed purchasing power or a fixed euro value. A bond fund also has its own portfolio and share price; it is not the same contract as one bond held to a stated maturity. These observations identify the risks to measure rather than recommending a particular allocation.

## The simple model does not describe every bond

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A security is called a bond. Must it have equal fixed coupons and one unchanged principal repayment?"

    { text: "Yes; every bond in every market must follow the same four numbers" },
    { text: "Yes; a different coupon frequency automatically turns it into equity" },
    { text: "No; bonds never have any contractual repayment obligation" },
  ]}
  explanation="Our fixed nominal bullet model is a starting case. Other debt structures can have floating coupons, index-linked principal, amortization, early redemption or conversion rights."
/>

A **floating-rate bond** changes its coupon according to a stated reference and reset rule. An **inflation-linked bond** adjusts specified payments using an index and contractual rules; it does not necessarily protect against every investor's personal inflation or a premium purchase price. For example, [US TIPS adjust principal with an inflation index and have a specific original-principal floor at maturity](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/tips/). That rule is specific to those securities, not a universal feature of bonds or a guarantee that any secondary-market purchase price will be recovered.

A **convertible bond** provides a contractual route to exchange the debt claim for shares; the conversion terms and who controls the choice matter.

An issuer can be a government, local authority, company or another entity. Those labels do not establish a universal yield ranking. Maturity, currency, credit, tax treatment, liquidity and contractual options differ. A higher quoted yield is not a free bonus; ask what assumptions, prices and risks explain it. It need not reflect credit risk alone, and a government label does not eliminate price, currency or purchasing-power risk.

A **zero-coupon bond** has no periodic coupon payments. In our noncallable one-year example, it promises a single \$1,000 at maturity. At a \$950 purchase price, assuming full payment:

$$
R_{\mathrm{zero}}=\frac{1000}{950}-1\approx 5.2632\%.
$$

At a \$1,020 price, the same promised \$1,000 would instead give $1000/1020-1\approx-1.9608\%$ over the year before costs. No coupon does not mean no interest-rate or credit risk; nor does the word zero guarantee a discounted purchase price. Our positive-yield example uses a discount, while a negative scheduled yield can accompany a price above the redemption amount.

### When to use the model

Start with the contract: identify the issuer, currency, principal rules, coupon formula, dates, seniority and options. Apply the fixed nominal bullet arithmetic only when its assumptions fit. The later pricing, yield and risk lessons add detail to this foundation; they do not replace reading the claim itself.
