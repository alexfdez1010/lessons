At one date, a three-month rate is 5.4% and a ten-year rate 4.1%. The difference is easy to calculate. Explaining it requires more information: which securities, which yield definition, which currency, and what assumptions separate expected future rates from the price of bearing risk?

A **yield curve**, or **term structure of interest rates**, relates a specified interest-rate measure to remaining maturity at a stated observation time. Moving across the curve selects another maturity in that snapshot. Following a single bond through successive dates would instead be a time series. The curve's shape describes relative yields; it does not by itself reveal one unique economic story or guarantee an investment return.

All numerical curves and decompositions below are invented. They use matching currency, annual rate conventions and the stated payment model. No graph is a live quote, mortgage offer or calibrated recession forecast.

Think of the curve as a photograph of a selected market at one time. A dot labelled ten years is a rate for that remaining term under the chart's methodology. It is not the rate today's three-month investment will necessarily earn ten years later.

Using one issuer can reduce differences between borrowers, but it does not freeze every risk. Longer and shorter claims can have different default exposure over their horizons, coupon schedules, collateral, liquidity, tax treatment or embedded options. Curves can also be estimated for a defined benchmark group rather than one legal issuer. Read the selected universe and method.

### Coupon-bond YTM, spot rates and par rates answer different questions

A **coupon-bond yield to maturity (YTM)** is the single discount rate fitting that particular bond's scheduled coupons and redemption to its price, under a stated compounding convention. It is not generally a separate market discount rate for every coupon date and does not guarantee actual receipts.

A **spot rate**, also called a zero-coupon rate, prices one payment at one maturity. In our annual-effective, fully performing model, a payment $F$ at year $t$ has price:

$$
P_t=\frac{F}{(1+z_t)^t}.
$$

A **par yield** is the coupon rate that would make a specified hypothetical coupon bond price equal its face value, given the discount curve and payment conventions. Actual coupon-bond YTMs, fitted spot rates and par yields can differ at the same maturity. The [ECB methodology explains that it estimates spot curves and derives par and forward curves](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/stats/financial_markets_and_interest_rates/euro_area_yield_curves/shared/pdf/technical_notes.pdf).

### Work the two-year par rate from two spot rates

Assume one-year spot rate 4% and two-year spot rate 6%, annual coupons, face 100, valuation on a coupon date and full payment. Define the discount factors for one unit at each date:

$$
d_1=\frac{1}{1.04}=0.961538\ldots,\qquad
 d_2=\frac{1}{1.06^2}=0.889996\ldots.
$$

A two-year par bond with annual coupon cash $C$ must satisfy:

$$
100=Cd_1+(100+C)d_2,
$$

$$
C=\frac{100(1-d_2)}{d_1+d_2}=5.941209\ldots.
$$

The par rate is below the two-year spot rate here because the first coupon is discounted at the lower one-year spot rate. They are related outputs of the same model, not interchangeable labels. A headline chart may contain fitted par yields rather than the YTMs of the exact bonds you own. Its documentation decides.

### When to use the definition

Check nominal versus inflation-linked rates, compounding, payment frequency, observation time and security-selection rules before interpreting a spread or discounting cash flows. A benchmark curve helps organize valuation; an individual bond may require adjustments for its own credit, liquidity or options. Calling a government curve a risk-free reference is a modeling convention for a particular purpose, not a statement that every holding-period price, currency or purchasing-power risk disappears.

## Describe shapes and calculate a specific spread

<ThinkReveal client:visible
  prompt="If the ten-year-minus-three-month spread is negative, must every segment from three months to thirty years slope downward?"
  hint="The subtraction contains two selected points. What does it say about the points between and beyond them?"

An **upward-sloping** curve has higher selected yields at longer maturities; it is often called normal. A **flat** curve has similar yields over the maturities being described. An **inverted** comparison has the longer selected yield below the shorter one. These are descriptions of rates. Normal is not a health certificate, and flat does not logically mean a transition is about to occur.

The **ten-year-minus-three-month spread** is:

$$
S_{10y,3m}=y_{10y}-y_{3m}.
$$

A **percentage point** is the difference between percentage rates. One **basis point** is 0.01 percentage point. In these matching-convention examples:

### When to use the shape

Use the pair-specific spread for a defined comparison and the full curve for changes in slope, humps and maturity-specific moves. Keep the yield basis consistent: a bill discount quote and an annual-effective bond yield are not automatically comparable inputs. A label alone does not determine the expected path of rates, the term premium or a borrowing decision.

## Expectations and a possibly negative term premium both matter

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="An upward-sloping curve is observed. Does that prove the estimated term premium is positive?"
  options={[
    { text: "No; an expected-rate component can outweigh a negative premium", correct: true },
    { text: "Yes; a term premium is required by law to be positive" },
    { text: "Yes; the long-minus-short spread equals the premium exactly" },
    { text: "No; the curve can never contain information related to expectations" },
  ]}
  explanation="Yield differences combine components. A curve's slope cannot uniquely identify their separate values. Term-premium estimates can be negative."
/>

A useful **schematic decomposition** of a long nominal yield is the expected average future short rate plus a **term premium**. The term premium is the component associated with bearing longer-term interest-rate risk in that model. It is estimated, not directly observed. A negative estimate is possible, for example when investors value a longer bond's hedging or safe-asset properties. It is not a negative coupon, a negative default probability or proof of an arbitrage.

In a simplified annual-rate approximation:

$$
y_n\approx\overline{\mathbb E[r_{\mathrm{short}}]}+TP_n,
$$

$$
y_n-y_{\mathrm{short,today}}\approx
 \left(\overline{\mathbb E[r_{\mathrm{short}}]}-y_{\mathrm{short,today}}\right)+TP_n.
$$

The expectation term is not the average of today's observed maturities. The equations summarize a model and omit convexity and other adjustments; they are not an exact pricing identity for every coupon-bond YTM. [The New York Fed explains that term premia require estimation](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/data-indicators/term-premia-tabs), while [the Federal Reserve discusses why term premia may be low or negative](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/bernanke20130301a.htm).

Inversion therefore does not require expected rate cuts in every model explanation.

This is why neither “healthy normal premium” nor “flat means everyone expects cuts” follows from the shape alone.

Move the component controls one at a time. They are assumptions supplied by you. The graphic does not infer expectations or term premia from the selected long yield. Different statistical models can estimate different decompositions, especially when assumptions and data change.

### When to use the decomposition

Use it to organize hypotheses about a curve move and identify what extra evidence would distinguish them. For example, a rise in a long yield can reflect changed expected short rates, changed risk compensation or both; it need not prove stronger expected growth. Nominal expected rates themselves involve inflation and real-rate expectations. Report the model and its uncertainty rather than turning an estimated component into a directly observed fact.

## Historical signals need a model, a horizon and other evidence

<ThinkReveal client:visible
  prompt="A hypothetical forecasting model reports a 30% chance of recession within twelve months after using a spread. What does that still allow?"
  hint="Use the same stated binary event and horizon."
  text="It still assigns 70% to no recession within that horizon. The 30% is the model's conditional probability, not a certain start date, a 30% fall in output or proof that the model is calibrated. A different model, sample or spread may give another probability."
/>

Some US Treasury spread measures have historically been informative about subsequent recessions. That association is empirical and depends on the chosen spread, sample, horizon and forecasting method. It is neither a law for every country nor proof that inversion causes a recession. Expected policy changes, inflation expectations, risk premia and other forces can affect the curve; policy and the economy can also respond to each other.

A hypothetical model probability of 30% over twelve months implies 70% for the complementary event in the same binary setup. Those figures are meaningful only as that model's forecast. They do not establish its accuracy. Changing a forecasting horizon or adding credit and macroeconomic variables can change the result. [Federal Reserve research compares spread-based recession models and their limitations](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/predicting-recession-probabilities-using-the-slope-of-the-yield-curve-20180301.html). No recession frequency or forecast is calculated from our invented curves.

### A Treasury spread is not a retail loan offer

The example of short yield 5.4% and long yield 4.1% contains no mortgage contract. It cannot establish that a particular long fixed mortgage is cheaper or better than a short reset period. Actual offers have borrower and collateral terms, payment schedules, fees, prepayment features and rate-reset rules. A mortgage's balance also normally changes through principal repayments. Those cash flows and risks differ from a Treasury benchmark.

The same caution applies to a bank's profits: subtracting two benchmark yields does not account for its actual funding, credit losses, hedging, capital or operating costs. The curve is one market input; it does not complete the decision.

### When to use the signal

State the specific spread, curve basis, data date, model and horizon. Look for out-of-sample evidence and consider other information. A steepening after inversion might come from short yields falling, long yields rising or both, with different underlying components. Describe what the data show before adding a causal story or choosing a financial contract.
