Two investors buy the same two-year bond for 100. Both receive the promised coupons and principal. One finishes with 110; the other with 110.25. The bond did not secretly change its terms: one investor earned interest on the first coupon. A yield quotation describes a particular cash-flow calculation; your eventual wealth also depends on what actually happens to the money.

We will use **plain fixed-rate bonds with no early-redemption option**, bought immediately after a coupon date. Worked examples use annual payments, no accrued interest, fees or taxes, and invented prices. We distinguish scheduled payments from actual receipts throughout.

## The coupon is a promise tied to face value

The **issuer** is the government or company borrowing. **Face value**, also called **par value**, is the principal scheduled for repayment at maturity. The **coupon rate** states scheduled annual interest as a percentage of face value. For our fixed-rate bond:

$$\text{Annual coupon}=100\times0.05=5$$

If the bond has two years remaining, its scheduled receipts are **5 after one year** and **105 after two years**: the last coupon of 5 plus principal of 100. Paying 90, 100 or 110 to buy it does not rewrite that schedule.

A fixed coupon resembles a fixed-rent contract: the agreed payments stay the same even if the contract's market value changes. The analogy has a limit. A contract specifies what is owed; it does not make the payer able to pay. **Default** means failing to fulfil an obligation such as paying interest or principal on time. The [SEC's corporate-bond guide explains that distinction](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/what-are).

### When it matters

Holding until maturity avoids having to accept an intermediate market sale price. It does **not** remove default risk or ensure that repayment keeps its purchasing power. Also, not every bond has a fixed coupon: floating-rate and inflation-linked bonds have different rules. The calculations here keep our fixed nominal schedule unchanged.

## A higher required yield lowers the price of the same schedule

A **yield** is a rate related to what you pay and the bond's cash flows; always identify which yield measure is being quoted. A **required yield** is the rate used here to discount the scheduled receipts to today's price. **Discounting** asks what price today corresponds to a stated future receipt and rate.

For the one-year payment of 105:

$$P=\frac{105}{1+y}$$

At a required yield of 5%, price = 105 ÷ 1.05 = **100**. At 10%, price = 105 ÷ 1.10 ≈ **95.4545**. Check the second price from the buyer's side: (105 − 95.4545) ÷ 95.4545 ≈ **10%** over the year, if the 105 is received as scheduled.

| Same promised receipt in one year | Required yield | Price today |
|---|---:|---:|
| 105 | 3% | About 101.94 |
| 105 | 5% | 100.00 |
| 105 | 10% | About 95.45 |

### The perpetual-bond shortcut

A **perpetual bond**, or consol, has no scheduled principal repayment and pays a coupon indefinitely. In the simplified model of a fixed annual coupon $C$ discounted forever at one constant positive rate $y$:

$$P=\frac{C}{y}$$

An annual coupon of 50 gives 50 ÷ 0.05 = **1,000** at 5% required yield. At 6.25%, 50 ÷ 0.0625 = **800**. The price change is (800 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = **−20%**. This formula is for the perpetuity, not our two-year bond with a final principal payment.

### When it matters

The inverse relationship compares **the same bond at the same valuation time with an unchanged cash-flow schedule**, varying its discount yield. It is not a rule that every pair of bonds, or every move between two dates, differs only in interest rates. Credit risk, liquidity, time remaining and contractual features also affect market quotations. A price drop can produce a higher *promised-flow* yield while the chance of receiving those flows worsens. The [SEC illustrates fixed-rate bond price sensitivity](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-86).

## Par, premium and discount describe price relative to face

**At par** means price equals face value. **At a premium** means price is above face; **at a discount**, below it. Prices are often quoted per 100 of face, so a quote of 98 corresponds to 980 for face value 1,000, before any accrued interest and transaction costs.

For a plain bond redeeming at face, with positive fixed coupons and the same payment convention, a required yield equal to the coupon rate prices it at par. A higher required yield prices it below par; a lower one prices it above par. [TreasuryDirect gives this price–yield comparison for Treasury notes and bonds](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/understanding-pricing/).

Our two-year bond promises 5 and then 105. The price at annual yield $y$ is:

$$P=\frac{5}{1+y}+\frac{105}{(1+y)^2}$$

| Required annual yield | Calculated price | Relative to face 100 |
|---|---:|---|
| 3% | About 103.83 | Premium |
| 5% | 100.00 | Par |
| 7% | About 96.38 | Discount |

At 7%, the two present values are 5 ÷ 1.07 ≈ **4.6729** and 105 ÷ 1.07² ≈ **91.7111**. Their sum is **96.3840**, rounded to 96.38. The coupon remains 5.

### When it matters

A premium is not automatically overpayment: larger scheduled coupons can justify a price above face. A discount is not automatically a bargain: smaller coupons or greater credit risk can accompany the lower price. Today's premium or discount alone does not tell you the rates that prevailed when a previous owner bought the bond.

## Current yield measures coupon income against price

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Two bonds pay the same annual coupon of 60. One costs 900 and the other 1,200. Which has the larger current yield?"}
  options={[
  {

  {
    "text": "The bond costing 1,200",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "Both have the same ratio",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"Current yield divides the same coupon by the amount paid. A smaller denominator gives the larger coupon-income ratio."}
  pretest
/>

**Current yield** is annual scheduled coupon cash divided by current price:

$$\text{Current yield}=\frac{\text{Annual coupon}}{\text{Price}}$$

With face value 1,000 and a 6% fixed coupon, annual coupon cash is 1,000 × 0.06 = **60**. At a price of 900, current yield = 60 ÷ 900 ≈ **6.67%**. At a price of 1,200, it is 60 ÷ 1,200 = **5%**. The coupon rate remains 6% in both calculations.

| Measure | Denominator | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Coupon rate | Face value | What annual coupon does the contract schedule? |
| Current yield | Current purchase price | How much annual coupon is scheduled per unit paid? |

Current yield leaves out the timing of principal repayment, the difference between price and redemption value, coupon reinvestment, costs and default losses. A 6.67% current yield is not a promise of 6.67% annual growth in your wealth. For a finite bond, you also need the remaining term and full schedule to calculate yield to maturity.

### When to use it

Use current yield to compare scheduled coupon income relative to price. Do not use it as a complete measure of a holding-period result. It can exceed the coupon rate simply because the bond trades below face, without any increase in coupon cash.

## YTM is the IRR of the scheduled cash flows

<MCQ

    "correct": false

  {
    "text": "A coupon rate reset whenever its price changes",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"YTM is calculated from the price and scheduled cash flows. Actual receipts, reinvestment and costs can lead to a different realised result."}
  pretest
/>

**Yield to maturity (YTM)** is the discount rate that equates the present value of scheduled coupons and principal with the price. It is the **internal rate of return (IRR)** of those scheduled cash flows: the rate that makes their discounted receipts equal the initial outlay. It is not an inflation-adjusted “real return”.

For an annual coupon $C$, face $F$, $n$ whole years remaining and a price $P$ on a coupon date, annual YTM $y$ solves:

$$P=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{C}{(1+y)^t}+\frac{F}{(1+y)^n}$$

The sum includes each annual coupon; the last term adds the face repayment at year $n$. With more than one payment per year, solve using the appropriate periods and state the annual quotation convention. Our examples and chart use annual payments so an annual periodic rate is directly comparable.

### Solve a rate by checking the cash flows

For price 100, annual coupon 5 and two years remaining, try 5%:

$$\frac{5}{1.05}+\frac{105}{1.05^2}\approx4.7619+95.2381=100$$

Therefore 5% is the scheduled-flow YTM. At price about 96.383964, 7% works instead:

$$\frac{5}{1.07}+\frac{105}{1.07^2}\approx4.6729+91.7111=96.3840$$

For the plain positive-coupon bond under these conventions:

| Price position | Ordering of annual rate measures |
|---|---|
| Discount | Coupon rate &lt; current yield &lt; scheduled YTM |
| Par | Coupon rate = current yield = scheduled YTM |
| Premium | Coupon rate &gt; current yield &gt; scheduled YTM |

That ordering compares **quoted measures of the scheduled cash flows**, not guaranteed realised returns. At a discount, the YTM calculation includes principal scheduled above the price paid. If the issuer does not repay that principal, the actual outcome changes.

### When to use YTM

Use it as a common scheduled-flow yardstick alongside credit risk, liquidity, currency, costs and redemption terms. Two bonds with different coupons can have similar YTMs because their prices differ, but the market does not guarantee that their realised returns or risks match. Callable bonds need additional early-redemption analysis. [FINRA distinguishes yield measures and total return](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/bond-yield-return).

## Reinvestment, default and an early sale change the outcome

An IRR discounts dated cash flows. **Computing the bond's cash-flow IRR does not itself require reinvesting coupons.** If you pay 100 and receive 5 after one year and 105 after two, those cash flows have a 5% IRR whether you spend, retain or reinvest the first coupon. What you do with it changes the wealth accumulated outside the bond.

A **compound terminal-wealth rate** asks a different question: what constant yearly growth turns the initial investment into the wealth retained at the end? To interpret the original YTM as that compound growth rate, the scheduled payments must arrive, the bond must be held to the relevant endpoint, and the coupons must earn the matching reinvestment rate through that endpoint, before excluded costs and taxes. The [CFA Institute explains the sources of realised fixed-rate bond returns](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/interest-rate-risk-and-return).

### Same bond, different treatment of the first coupon

Keep every payment; nothing is spent or added from outside:

| Treatment of year-one coupon | Value of that coupon at year two | Final bond payment | Terminal wealth |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Kept as cash earning 0% | 5.00 | 105.00 | 110.00 |
| Reinvested for one year at 5% | 5 × 1.05 = 5.25 | 105.00 | 110.25 |

With no reinvestment earnings, terminal wealth is **110**. The constant annual growth in retained wealth is:

$$\left(\frac{110}{100}\right)^{1/2}-1\approx0.04880885=4.8809\%$$

With the first coupon reinvested at 5%, terminal wealth is **110.25**:

$$\left(\frac{110.25}{100}\right)^{1/2}-1=0.05=5\%$$

Both bond payment schedules still have a 5% cash-flow IRR. The two terminal-wealth calculations differ because one includes 0.25 of reinvestment income. **Reinvestment risk** is uncertainty about the rate available for money received before your final horizon.

### Default changes the received cash-flow IRR

Suppose you paid 100 and received the first coupon of 5, but at year two the issuer defaults. In this invented outcome you receive only **60 in final recovery**, with no last coupon and no later payments. **Recovery** is the amount actually returned after the default; it need not equal face value.

The actual flows are now −100, +5 and +60. Their IRR is **−20%**, which can be checked directly:

$$\frac{5}{0.8}+\frac{60}{0.8^2}=6.25+93.75=100$$

The old 5% YTM remains the quotation for the *promised* schedule. It did not protect the principal or describe the received schedule after default.

### A sale and purchasing power need their own checks

If instead the issuer pays the first coupon of 5 and you sell after one year for 96, total proceeds are 101. With no costs, the one-year holding-period return is (5 + 96 − 100) ÷ 100 = **1%**. You never reach the original maturity schedule; the sale price is part of your actual result.

Finally, all the quoted yields above are **nominal**, measured in money rather than purchasing power. Inflation can reduce what the proceeds buy even if every payment arrives. Adjust the realised return using the relevant price change over the same period and currency when purchasing power is the question.

### When to use these distinctions

For promised cash-flow comparison, calculate YTM. For an outcome after the event, use actual receipts and dates. For an accumulation goal, include reinvestment and retained terminal wealth. State the measurement before calling a return “earned”: the same bond can support different valid numbers answering different questions.
