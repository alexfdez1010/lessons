A reserve account starts at 100 USD. A rule closes the activity if the reserve reaches 0 or 300. “What is the chance of closing at zero within two steps?” differs from “What is the chance of closing there eventually?” Both differ from “How long until either exit?”

A stopped Markov chain answers these questions within an explicit model. We will use **invented independent steps of +100 or −100 USD**, with up probability $p=0.6$ and down probability $q=0.4$ while the account is active. There are no costs, withdrawals, jumps or changing probabilities. This is fixed monetary sizing, not a fixed percentage of wealth, a trading recommendation or an estimated market edge.

## Make the stopping rule part of the state model

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="If the account closes at either 0 or 300, what should its stopped-state model do after it first reaches 300?" options={[{text:'Keep recording the stopped outcome 300 forever',correct:true},{text:'Continue trading and later count a fall to zero as the original stop'},{text:'Delete the successful path from the probability distribution'}]} explanation="An absorbing state records an outcome after stopping. The model freezes the stopped result; it does not claim that all assets in the real world stop changing value." />

Measure reserves in units of 100 USD, so states are 0, 1, 2 and 3. An **absorbing state** cannot be left: its transition probability to itself is one. States 0 and 3 are absorbing **because the policy stops the process there**. States 1 and 2 are active.

The **first-passage time** to the stopping set is $\tau=\inf\{n\ge0:X_n\in\{0,3\}\}$. It counts steps until the first boundary hit. Starting already at a boundary gives $\tau=0$. The first upper hit and the first lower hit are competing outcomes; after the first stop, the account cannot go on to earn the other outcome in this stopped model.

Rows mean current state and columns next state. In natural order $0,1,2,3$:

$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0&0\\
0.4&0&0.6&0\\
0&0.4&0&0.6\\
0&0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

From state 1, one down step reaches 0 with probability 0.4; one up reaches 2 with probability 0.6. From 2, down reaches 1 and up reaches 3. Every row sums to one. The two active states can return to each other, so counting only one-step exits would miss later stopping paths.

<AbsorbingReserveChain client:visible horizon={1} title="One step from reserve state 1" />

**One-step check from state 1.** Lower-stop probability is 0.4, upper-stop probability is 0, and survival is 0.6, all in state 2. They sum to one. “60% up probability” does not mean a 60% chance of reaching 300 before 0 from every starting reserve.

<FillBlank client:visible text="From state 1, reaching the upper boundary needs at least {{2|1|0}} steps: 1 → 2 → 3." explanation="One step can only reach state 0 or 2. The first possible upper hit is at step 2." />

### When to use it

Represent a risk limit, target, default state or contract barrier only after stating exactly what stops and what is recorded afterwards. The first-passage event concerns the whole path, but a stopped chain can encode it in the current state. A snapshot of an **unstopped** process being at a level at date $n$ need not equal the chance it has ever touched that level.

## Separate cumulative hits from first hits at a date

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A path reaches zero at step 1 and remains stopped there. Does it create a new first hit at step 2?" options={[{text:'No: it contributes to cumulative hits by step 2, but its first hit was at step 1',correct:true},{text:'Yes: each later observation at zero is another first-passage event'},{text:'No: it must disappear from all later cumulative probabilities'}]} explanation="Absorbing occupancy accumulates earlier hits. First-hit probability at an exact date is the increase in that cumulative probability." />

Let $L_n$ and $U_n$ be probabilities of having stopped at the lower and upper boundaries **by** step $n$. Let $a_n$ and $b_n$ be probabilities of still being active in states 1 and 2. Starting at 1 gives $(L_0,a_0,b_0,U_0)=(0,1,0,0)$.

One forward step gives:

$$
L_{n+1}=L_n+qa_n,\quad a_{n+1}=qb_n,\quad b_{n+1}=pa_n,\quad U_{n+1}=U_n+pb_n.
$$

The added terms $qa_n$ and $pb_n$ are **new** lower and upper hits on that next step. Thus $P(\tau=n,\text{ lower})=L_n-L_{n-1}$ for $n\ge1$, and similarly for the upper stop.

At step 2, upper absorption is $0.6\times0.6=0.36$, lower absorption remains 0.4, and survival is $0.6\times0.4=0.24$. The lower first-hit probability at step 3 is $0.24\times0.4=0.096$. Cumulative lower hits then rise to $0.4+0.096=0.496$.

<AbsorbingReserveChain client:visible horizon={2} title="Move the horizon and distinguish first hits from accumulated hits" />

In a stopped chain, $(P^n)_{1,0}=L_n$ because all earlier lower hits stay at 0. It is **not** generally the probability of first hitting exactly at $n$. At step 2, $(P^2)_{1,0}=0.4$ while the first lower-hit probability is zero.

Add 0.096 to earlier cumulative lower hits 0.4." />

### When to use it

Use cumulative probabilities for “breach by the reporting horizon” and first-hit masses for timing of closure or cash needs. Keep the still-active probability: here 24% are unresolved at step 2. Finite-horizon results do not automatically equal eventual results.

## Eventual stopping probabilities solve boundary equations

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="At two steps, 24% remain active. Can the 36% upper-hit probability already be called the eventual upper-hit probability?" options={[{text:'No: some unresolved paths can hit the upper boundary later',correct:true},{text:'Yes: all surviving paths must eventually hit only the lower boundary'},{text:'Yes: any two-step calculation is automatically an infinite-horizon one'}]} explanation="A finite horizon leaves unresolved paths. Eventual outcomes must account for all later returns between active states." />

Let $h_i$ be the probability of reaching 3 before 0 from state $i$. Boundary values are $h_0=0$ and $h_3=1$. Condition on the first step:

$$
h_1=0.4(0)+0.6h_2,\qquad h_2=0.4h_1+0.6(1).
$$

Substitute the second equation into the first:

$$
h_1=0.6(0.4h_1+0.6)=0.24h_1+0.36,
\qquad 0.76h_1=0.36,
\qquad h_1=\frac9{19}\approx0.473684.
$$

Then $h_2=0.4(9/19)+0.6=15/19\approx0.789474$. Eventual lower probabilities are $1-h_1=10/19$ and $1-h_2=4/19$. Starting closer to the lower stop matters even with $p>0.5$.

The calculation is a small **linear system**: unknowns appear only to the first power. In matrix form, reorder states as active $1,2$ then absorbing $0,3$:

$$
P_{\text{canonical}}=\begin{pmatrix}Q&R\\0&I\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&0.6\\0.4&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
R=\begin{pmatrix}0.4&0\\0&0.6\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$Q$ records moves **within** active states; its rows need not sum to one because probability exits through $R$. $I$ is the identity matrix, ones on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere: absorbing states keep their own outcomes.

If $B$ records eventual absorption probabilities, first-step conditioning gives $B=R+QB$, hence $(I-Q)B=R$. When the inverse exists under the conditions below, $B=(I-Q)^{-1}R=NR$.

<AbsorbingMatrixWorkbench client:visible title="Multiply expected visits by one-step exits" />

**One matrix product entry.** We will derive $N$ in the next section. Its first row is $(25/19,15/19)$. The column of $R$ leading to state 3 is $(0,0.6)^\top$. Multiply corresponding entries and add:

$$
B_{1,3}=\frac{25}{19}(0)+\frac{15}{19}\left(\frac35\right)=\frac9{19}.
$$

These are expected visits multiplied by the chance of exiting to that boundary on a visit. The full result, columns ordered $0,3$, is:

$$
B=\frac1{19}\begin{pmatrix}10&9\\4&15\end{pmatrix}.
$$

<MCQ client:visible question="Why do rows of Q sum to less than one here, while rows of B sum to one?" options={[{text:'Q omits exits; B includes all eventual stopping outcomes because absorption is certain in this finite model',correct:true},{text:'Q lost probability through an arithmetic mistake'},{text:'B counts expected visits, so its rows must always exceed one'}]} explanation="The missing Q mass goes to the absorbing columns of R. Eventual stopping at either boundary has total probability one here; B contains those probabilities, unlike N’s expected visit counts." />

### When to use it

Use boundary equations to check a matrix result and keep the state ordering explicit. A matrix of eventual probabilities does not answer “by next Friday”. The two-boundary model also differs from a walk with only a lower floor and no upper target, such as the separate [Risk of Ruin](/risk-of-ruin) model.

## Expected visits turn into expected stopping time

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An entry of the fundamental matrix is 25/19. Does exceeding one make it an invalid probability?" options={[{text:'No: it is an expected visit count, which can exceed one',correct:true},{text:'Yes: every matrix in a Markov model must contain only probabilities'},{text:'No: it is a guaranteed number of visits on every path'}]} explanation="Q and B contain probabilities. N contains expected counts before stopping, including the initial active visit." />

The **fundamental matrix** counts visits before absorption:

$$
N=(I-Q)^{-1}=I+Q+Q^2+\cdots.
$$

The identity term counts time 0: starting in state 1 is already one visit to 1. Later powers add probabilities of being in each active state after one, two or more steps before stopping. Summing their expectations gives visit counts. This interpretation and $t=N\mathbf1$ are developed in [Grinstead and Snell's probability text, section 11.2](https://math.dartmouth.edu/~prob/prob/prob.pdf).

**Compute the inverse rather than treating it as a spell.** For a $2\times2$ matrix $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $ad-bc\ne0$:

$$
A^{-1}=\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Here $I-Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&-0.6\\-0.4&1\end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $1-0.24=0.76=19/25$. Therefore:

$$
N=\frac1{0.76}\begin{pmatrix}1&0.6\\0.4&1\end{pmatrix}
=\frac1{19}\begin{pmatrix}25&15\\10&25\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Starting at 1, expected visits to 1 are $25/19$ and to 2 are $15/19$. Each visit to an active state at times $0,\ldots,\tau-1$ precedes one transition, so adding the row gives expected steps to **either** stop:

$$
t=N\mathbf1=\frac1{19}\begin{pmatrix}40\\35\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus $E_1[\tau]=40/19\approx2.105263$ steps and $E_2[\tau]=35/19\approx1.842105$. Individual stopping times are whole numbers; their expectations need not be.

<AbsorbingMatrixWorkbench client:visible initialMode="time" title="Sum the selected row for expected time to either boundary" />

**Independent check with first-step equations.** Set $t_0=t_3=0$. You always spend one step before seeing the next state:

$$
t_1=1+0.6t_2,\qquad t_2=1+0.4t_1.
$$

Substitution gives $t_1=1.6+0.24t_1$, so $t_1=1.6/0.76=40/19$. These times are not the expected time **conditional on success**. Nor is $1/h_1$ a valid generic replacement for them.

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="Why should the identity matrix be included in N rather than starting the series at Q?" hint="Count active observations before a path that stops on its very first step." text="That path has one active visit at time 0 and takes one step to stop. Omitting I loses the initial visit and subtracts one from the corresponding expected stopping time." />

### When to use it

Use expected stopping time for modelled duration and planning, with the time unit stated. It is not a maximum waiting time, deadline guarantee or a conditional duration given a particular boundary. Costs per active step can be weighted by expected visit counts, but require their own stated units and discounting if relevant.

## Check reachability before inverting

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A model contains absorbing states 0 and 3, but states 1 and 2 only alternate forever. Does the mere existence of absorbing states make N = (I−Q)⁻¹ valid for states 1 and 2?" options={[{text:'No: those active states cannot reach the stopping set',correct:true},{text:'Yes: any matrix with an absorbing state has that finite inverse'},{text:'Yes: a stationary distribution must force exit from every state'}]} explanation="The active block must be transient relative to the chosen stopping set. A closed active cycle violates that condition." />

For the finite absorbing-chain formulas above, every active state must have a route of positive probability to a stopping state, with the assumed fixed transition rule. Then active-state mass tends to zero, the expected visit series is finite, and $I-Q$ is invertible. Equivalently here, $Q^n$ tends to zero: no active-state mass remains in the infinite-horizon limit. The route condition is usually the easier practical check in a small finite model.

Now replace the active rules with $1\to2$ and $2\to1$, each with probability one, and no exits:

$$
Q_{\text{cycle}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=0,\qquad
I-Q_{\text{cycle}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its determinant is $1-1=0$. From either active start, boundary-hit probability is zero at every finite horizon and eventually; $\tau=\infty$ almost surely. There is no finite expected stopping time and no valid fundamental inverse for this chosen stopping set. Silently applying a numerical pseudoinverse would not repair the stopping problem.

<AbsorbingMatrixWorkbench client:visible closedCycle={true} initialMode="visits" title="A closed active cycle withholds the fundamental inverse" />

An absorbing chain also differs from the irreducible mixing chains studied earlier. In our exit model, any mixture supported on states 0 and 3 is stationary. The eventual mixture **depends on the start** through $B$; Markov alone does not imply a unique long-run mix independent of the initial state. Constant transition probabilities mean **time homogeneity**, whereas stationarity of a process also concerns its initial law and joint distributions.

<MCQ client:visible question="What changes if withdrawals, jump sizes or transition probabilities depend on information not included in the current state?" options={[{text:'Reassess the state definition and transition model before reusing these probabilities and durations',correct:true},{text:'Nothing: the old matrix remains exact whenever it has rows summing to one'},{text:'The old expected time automatically becomes a guaranteed calendar deadline'}]} explanation="A stochastic matrix can be internally valid yet be a poor model of the actual financial process. State sufficiency, time homogeneity, barriers and costs are substantive assumptions." />

### When to use it

Check transition estimates, state completeness, stopping rules and reachability before relying on a matrix output. A finite model with certain eventual absorption is not a claim that a real account must stop within a fixed number of trades. Barrier changes or a restart policy define a different process.
