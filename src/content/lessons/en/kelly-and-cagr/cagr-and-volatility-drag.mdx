An average return and a compound growth rate answer different questions. The previous lesson establishes that distinction. Here we measure the gap without giving an approximation more authority than it has.

All numerical paths are invented. Unless stated otherwise, periods are one year, all investment distributions are reinvested, and there are no outside contributions or withdrawals. The returns supplied already determine the wealth calculation; there is no additional fee in our arithmetic. Applying the examples to a product requires checking which fees, taxes and distributions its return measure includes.

## Reconcile the actual path before reporting CAGR

Write $R_t$ for the simple return in year t, so its **gross return factor** is $1+R_t$. A 50% gain multiplies wealth by 1.5. A 30% loss multiplies it by 0.7. With positive gross factors and no outside flows,

$$\frac{V_n}{V_0}=\prod_{t=1}^{n}(1+R_t),\qquad C=\left(\frac{V_n}{V_0}\right)^{1/n}-1$$

Here C is **CAGR**, the compound annual growth rate. It is the constant annual simple rate that reproduces the endpoint. Define the average **log growth** separately:

$$\gamma=\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\ln(1+R_t),\qquad C=e^\gamma-1$$

The logarithm converts multiplication into addition. It does not turn a log rate into the same numerical simple rate. For +30% and −10%, the factor is $1.3(0.9)=1.17$, so $\gamma=\ln(1.17)/2\approx0.078502$ but $C=\sqrt{1.17}-1\approx8.166538\%$.

$$A=\frac{0.5-0.3+0.4-0.2+0.6-0.169}{6}=0.1385$$

### When to use it

Use CAGR to describe an endpoint over a known number of years. A geometric mean of daily returns is per day until annualized using an explicit time convention. CAGR conceals interim losses and is not a money-weighted return. With outside flows, use the distinctions and dated methods in [the preceding lesson](/kelly-and-cagr/arithmetic-vs-geometric-mean).

## A specified two-return family gives an exact variance effect

<MCQ

    "correct": false

  {
    "text": "Yes; variance can always be deducted exactly",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"AM–GM compares summaries of one list. Ranking different lists requires more than those two moments."}
  pretest
/>


The arithmetic mean A is the ordinary average of the simple returns. For the same list of positive gross factors, AM–GM gives $C\le A$, with equality only when all returns are identical. Equivalently, log is concave: averaging logarithms gives at most the logarithm of the average gross factor. This compares two summaries of the **same list**; it does not rank every pair of funds by volatility.

To isolate a precise effect, specify two equally frequent returns $A-d$ and $A+d$. Here d is a nonnegative symmetric spread. Their population variance is $v=d^2$; this descriptive variance divides the two squared deviations by two, not by one. Assume $d<1+A$, so both gross factors are positive:

$$(1+C)^2=(1+A-d)(1+A+d)=(1+A)^2-d^2$$

$$C=\sqrt{(1+A)^2-d^2}-1$$

For A=10% and d=20%, the returns are −10% and +30%. Their product is 1.17, so C≈8.166538%. With the same A and d=40%, the returns are −30% and +50%; their product is $0.7(1.5)=1.05$, giving C≈2.469508%. In this family, increasing d lowers exact geometric growth.

The exact gap can also be written without subtracting nearly equal square roots:

$$A-C=\frac{d^2}{(1+A)+\sqrt{(1+A)^2-d^2}}$$

### When to use it

Use this family for a controlled comparison or a sensitivity exercise. Do not infer an arbitrary fund’s exact CAGR from only its mean and variance: skewness and near-total losses matter too. For example, lists (−82%, +53%, +53%) and (−42%, −42%, +108%) both average 8%. Their variances are 0.405 and 0.5, but their products are 0.421362 and 0.699712. The higher-variance list has the higher geometric mean. Both remain below their own arithmetic mean.

## The usual variance shortcut needs two approximation steps

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"When is C≈A−v/2 safe to label an exact formula for arbitrary annual simple returns?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "Never; it omits terms and assumptions",
    "correct": true
  },
  {
    "text": "Whenever the sample has two observations",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "Whenever the average return is positive",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"The shortcut is an approximation; the exact product remains available for a supplied list."}
  pretest
/>


Let $v=(1/n)\sum(R_t-A)^2$. Expanding the logarithm around the mean A and neglecting higher central moments gives

$$\gamma\approx\ln(1+A)-\frac{v}{2(1+A)^2}$$

This requires returns concentrated sufficiently near A relative to the distance $1+A$ from total loss. Large downside moves and higher moments can make the neglected terms important. Exponentiating and retaining only the first-order effect of this small variance correction gives

$$C\approx A-\frac{v}{2(1+A)}$$

If A is also small relative to one, replace the denominator $1+A$ by one:

$$C\approx A-\frac v2$$

This last expression is the familiar **variance-penalty shortcut**. It is not an exact simple-return identity. Expanding log around zero instead gives $\gamma\approx A-(v+A^2)/2$, because the second raw moment is $v+A^2$, not v alone. Dropping terms and switching between C and γ without saying so mixes approximations.

For A=10%, d=20%, v=0.04, the centered simple-rate estimate is $0.1-0.04/(2.2)=8.181818\%$. The additional shortcut gives $0.1-0.04/2=8\%$, while exact C≈8.166538%. At A=50%, d=20%, exact C is $\sqrt{2.21}-1\approx48.660687\%$, the centered estimate is 48.666667%, and the small-mean shortcut gives 48%. The second approximation is visibly less suitable at that larger mean.

### A continuous-time identity uses different inputs

In the specified diffusion $dV/V=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW$, the log drift is exactly $\mu-\sigma^2/2$. Here μ and σ are instantaneous model coefficients with matched time units; μ is not the realized arithmetic mean of a finite annual list. The Brownian term still affects finite realized log growth. An exponential converts a per-year log rate to its equivalent annual simple rate. [Columbia’s primary notes derive lognormal level and log growth using a different symbol convention: their log drift μ corresponds to our μ−σ²/2](https://www.columbia.edu/~ks20/FE-Notes/4700-07-Notes-GBM.pdf).

### When to use it

Use a labeled approximation for a quick sensitivity estimate, and calculate the actual product when the return list is available. Use diffusion identities only inside that model. Neither a historical variance nor a fitted diffusion guarantees a future CAGR.

## Break-even volatility is model-specific

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"A=8% and d=40% in the equally frequent symmetric family. What should decide the sign of exact growth?"}

  explanation={"The exact product is 1.0064, above one, even though the shortcut equals zero."}
  pretest
/>


For the symmetric family with nonnegative A, exact zero geometric growth requires the two-period product to equal one:

$$(1+A)^2-d^2=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_*=\sqrt{2A+A^2}$$

The shortcut C≈A−d²/2 instead gives an approximate candidate $\sqrt{2A}$. At A=8%, these are $\sqrt{0.1664}\approx40.792156\%$ and $\sqrt{0.16}=40\%$.

Check the disputed point directly. With d=40%, the two returns are −32% and +48%. Their product is $0.68(1.48)=1.0064$, giving $C=\sqrt{1.0064}-1\approx0.319490\%$, which is positive. Only at the exact family boundary does C reach zero. A negative mean has no zero-growth solution for nonnegative d in this family: $C\le A<0$ already.

<VolatilityGrowthLab
  client:visible
  mean={0.08}
  spread={0.4}
  title={"Compare zero-growth boundaries"}
/>


At d=1+A, the lower gross factor is zero. One period loses 100%, subsequent percentage gains on zero wealth cannot restore it, and geometric return is −100%. Logarithmic growth tends to minus infinity; there is no finite logarithm to report. A simple return below −100% can arise in a liability or leveraged-account setting, but is outside this positive-wealth log framework. The viewer accepts the total-loss endpoint and explains it without a numeric infinity.

### When to use it

Use a break-even boundary as a statement about an explicit payoff family or stochastic model. Do not turn it into a universal volatility cutoff for an asset class. The two-point threshold is not the exact binary Kelly zero of a different payoff model, nor a prediction that a particular market must lose money.

### When to use it

Pair endpoint growth with a path-risk measure when interim solvency or withdrawals matter. A lower drawdown here does not establish a universal benefit from a proposed rebalancing or hedging strategy. Those interventions change returns, risks and costs and need their own comparison.

## Expected wealth and long-run log growth ask different questions

Now specify a probability model rather than a known historical list: each period independently gains 50% or loses 50%, each with probability one half. These probabilities are known for this invented exercise. The expected gross factor is $0.5(1.5)+0.5(0.5)=1$. Independence therefore gives $E[V_n]=100(1)^n=100$ from initial wealth 100.

After two periods, wealth is 225 with probability 1/4, 75 with probability 1/2, and 25 with probability 1/4. The expectation is $225/4+75/2+25/4=100$. It does not equal the endpoint 75 of a list containing one gain and one loss.

The expected log growth per period is $[\ln(1.5)+\ln(0.5)]/2=\ln(0.75)/2<0$. Under this IID model, the law of large numbers for the finite log returns makes realized average log growth approach that value almost surely. Its limiting equivalent simple rate is $\sqrt{0.75}-1\approx-13.397460\%$. This asymptotic statement does not say that every finite path loses: two gains produce 225.

Choose the objective: expected terminal wealth, a quantile, drawdown risk and expected log growth need not favor the same choice. A negative average log does not justify declaring every real-world asset with a given estimated volatility a guaranteed loser.

## Read the return convention and reset interval

<MCQ
  client:visible

“Average annual return” does not automatically mean arithmetic average. For example, US SEC Form N-1A specifies a compound-rate calculation for its standardized average annual total return, with stated reinvestment and charge assumptions. Inspect the actual measure and methodology. [See Item 26(b)(1), the formula and its instructions](https://www.sec.gov/files/form-n-1a.pdf).

Daily leverage is a different issue. An idealized strategy that exactly delivers twice each daily simple index return, with no financing costs, fees or tracking error, multiplies factors $1+2R_t$. Two days of +10%, −10% leave the index at $1.1(0.9)-1=-1\%$ and that ideal strategy at $1.2(0.8)-1=-4\%$. Twice the index’s two-day return would be −2%, a different calculation.

Two +10% days instead give index growth $1.1^2-1=21\%$ and ideal daily-double growth $1.2^2-1=44\%$, above twice 21%. So “daily leverage always decays below the horizon multiple” is false even before considering actual product implementation. [SEC investor guidance explains daily reset objectives and why returns over longer holding periods can differ](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alerts/sec).

Check the return period, reset frequency, compounding convention, funding, fees, distributions and downside domain before comparing a product with an unleveraged benchmark. The examples are ideal factor calculations, not promised tracking or suitability advice. Diversification, hedging and rebalancing may change the entire return distribution; a variance shortcut alone cannot promise their net growth benefit.
