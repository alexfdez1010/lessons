A favourable coin wins 60% of the time and pays even money. You stake 20% of your current bankroll, then see win, loss, loss, win. Starting with €1,000, you finish with €921.60. That fraction really is Kelly-optimal under the stated model. Optimal expected growth has not promised a profitable four-bet path.

The previous lessons separated arithmetic averages from compounding. Here you will derive the binary Kelly fraction, calculate its exact expected log growth, and find where over-betting turns that growth negative. You will also keep three different ideas separate: a finite losing path, wealth tending toward zero in the limit, and a practical threshold that forces you to stop.

## Start with the payoff and the admissible stake

Let $p$ be the probability of winning, $q=1-p$ the probability of losing, and $b$ the **net payoff odds**: a win earns $b$ units of profit per unit staked, with the stake also returned. A loss forfeits the stake. You stake a fixed fraction $f$ of the **current bankroll**, then resize it after every outcome.

| Outcome | Probability | Change in wealth | Wealth multiplier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Win | $p$ | Gain $b$ times the stake | $1+bf$ |
| Loss | $q$ | Lose the stake | $1-f$ |

For this model assume $0<p<1$, $b>0$, no leverage or shorting, and $0\le f<1$. The repeated outcomes are independent with the same known probabilities and payoffs. Stakes can be divided arbitrarily, unused cash earns zero, and there are no costs, taxes, minimum stakes or forced liquidations. Those assumptions are part of the result.

### Worked finite path

At even money, $b=1$. With $f=0.2$, a win multiplies wealth by 1.2 and a loss by 0.8:

| Bet | Outcome | Calculation | Wealth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start | — | Initial bankroll | €1,000.00 |
| 1 | Win | €1,000 × 1.2 | €1,200.00 |
| 2 | Loss | €1,200 × 0.8 | €960.00 |
| 3 | Loss | €960 × 0.8 | €768.00 |
| 4 | Win | €768 × 1.2 | **€921.60** |

The second loss stakes 20% of €960, not 20% of the original €1,000. For a fixed fraction and fixed payoffs, the final product depends on the counts of wins and losses, while their order changes intermediate drawdowns and can matter when there are stopping rules.

At $f=1$, a loss makes wealth exactly zero. The probability of surviving $n$ independent all-in bets is $p^n$, which tends to zero as $n$ increases. For $0<f<1$, every win and loss multiplier is strictly positive: **no finite sequence makes idealised wealth exactly zero**. That does not make the drawdowns affordable.

### When to use it

Write down what a win and a loss actually do before using a sizing formula. A market position with variable losses, transaction costs, overlapping exposures or liquidation conditions does not automatically match this two-outcome bet.

## Expected log growth is not the return on every bet

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A calculation gives positive expected log growth per bet. Which claim does that support under the repeated-bet assumptions?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Positive asymptotic log growth', correct: true },
    { text: 'A profit on every individual bet' },
    { text: 'A profit after every finite sequence' },
    { text: 'A guaranteed maximum drawdown' },
  ]}
  explanation="An expected log-growth objective describes a distribution and its long-run rate under the model. It does not remove losing bets or constrain every finite path."
/>

Wealth multiplies across bets. Taking natural logarithms turns the product into a sum:

$$\ln\frac{W_n}{W_0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln M_i$$

Here $M_i$ is the realised wealth multiplier. For the binary bet, **expected log growth per bet** is:

$$g(f)=p\ln(1+bf)+(1-p)\ln(1-f)$$

Under the stated independent, identically distributed assumptions, the average realised log growth converges to $g(f)$ with probability one as the number of bets tends to infinity. This is a law-of-large-numbers result, not a schedule of guaranteed gains. [David Aldous, Berkeley, on the Kelly criterion](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/Real_World/kelly.html)

The number $g$ is in **log units per bet**. The corresponding ordinary geometric growth-factor equivalent is:

$$r_{\text{geo}}=\exp(g)-1$$

### Worked units and a conditional wealth calculation

For $p=0.6$, $b=1$ and $f=0.2$:

$$g(0.2)=0.6\ln(1.2)+0.4\ln(0.8)$$

$$=0.6(0.18232156)+0.4(-0.22314355)\approx0.02013551$$

Thus $\exp(0.02013551)-1\approx0.02033960$, or **2.03396% per bet as a geometric equivalent**. On an actual bet the bankroll still either gains 20% or loses 20%; it never receives a guaranteed 2.03396% payment.

If a specific 100-bet run contains exactly 60 wins and 40 losses, its final wealth is:

$$W_{100}=1000(1.2)^{60}(0.8)^{40}=1000\exp(100g(0.2))\approx7489.87$$

That is **€7,489.87 conditional on those outcome counts**, not the guaranteed outcome of 100 bets. The finite-run arithmetic expectation is a different quantity: $E[W_{100}]=1000(1.04)^{100}$, because the expected one-bet multiplier is 0.6 × 1.2 + 0.4 × 0.8 = 1.04. Do not swap expected wealth, expected log wealth and the outcome of one path.

### When to use it

Use expected log growth to compare repeated proportional staking policies within the model. For a finite decision, also inspect possible losses, the investment horizon and any requirement to preserve a minimum wealth level. Maximising an expectation does not settle those constraints.

## Derive the binary Kelly fraction

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="At a fixed 60% win probability, improving net win odds from one-to-one to three-to-one does what to the exact optimal stake?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Raises the optimal fraction', correct: true },
    { text: 'Lowers the optimal fraction' },
    { text: 'Keeps the fraction unchanged' },
  ]}
  explanation="Holding win probability fixed, a better payoff increases the edge. The slogan 'edge divided by odds' does not mean larger odds always require a smaller fraction."
/>

Differentiate the exact objective and set its derivative to zero:

$$g'(f)=\frac{pb}{1+bf}-\frac{q}{1-f}=0$$

$$pb(1-f)=q(1+bf)$$

$$pb-q=bf(p+q)=bf$$

Therefore the interior optimum is:

$$f^*=\frac{pb-q}{b}=p-\frac{q}{b}$$

The numerator $pb-q$ is expected profit per unit staked, often called the **edge**. Strict concavity confirms the stationary point is a maximum:

$$g''(f)=-\frac{pb^2}{(1+bf)^2}-\frac{q}{(1-f)^2}<0$$

With the no-shorting constraint, use $f^*=0$ when the edge is zero or negative. A negative unconstrained formula is not permission to take the opposite side: that other bet has its own available odds and costs. The assumptions exclude $p=1$; if a loss were genuinely impossible, the boundary problem would differ.

### Worked sizing examples

| Win probability | Net win odds | Edge | Constrained Kelly fraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.60 | 1 | 0.60 × 1 − 0.40 = 0.20 | 0.20 ÷ 1 = **20%** |
| 0.55 | 1 | 0.55 − 0.45 = 0.10 | **10%** |
| 0.40 | 3 | 0.40 × 3 − 0.60 = 0.60 | 0.60 ÷ 3 = **20%** |
| 0.60 | 3 | 0.60 × 3 − 0.40 = 1.40 | 1.40 ÷ 3 ≈ **46.67%** |
| 0.40 | 1 | 0.40 − 0.60 = −0.20 | **0%**, since shorting is excluded |

For even money, $f^*=2p-1$. A 60% win probability is not “winning twice as often as losing”: its win-to-loss probability ratio is 0.6 ÷ 0.4 = **1.5**. Win probability and stake fraction answer different questions.

### When to use it

The formula is exact for the stated binary payoff distribution and objective. Two bets with the same Kelly fraction can still have different growth, variance and drawdown profiles. The table's 60/40 even-money bet and 40/60 three-to-one bet both have a 20% optimum; their full growth curves are different.

## Find the exact zero instead of assuming twice Kelly

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Two binary bets have the same 20% Kelly optimum but different win odds. Must both return to zero expected log growth at a 40% stake?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; their exact curves can differ', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; doubling Kelly is an exact identity' },
    { text: 'Yes; the win probability cannot matter' },
  ]}
  explanation="The exact binary log-growth function is not a symmetric quadratic. Its second zero depends on the probabilities and payoffs."
/>

With a positive edge, $g$ starts at zero, rises to its unique maximum, then falls. Since $q>0$, it tends to negative infinity as $f$ approaches one. There is a unique positive root beyond Kelly, found by solving:

$$p\ln(1+bf_0)+q\ln(1-f_0)=0,\qquad f_0>f^*$$

A numerical method such as bisection brackets a positive value of $g$ near the peak and a negative value nearer one, repeatedly halves the interval and keeps the part containing the crossing.

### Worked threshold for the 60/40 even-money bet

$$g(0.4)=0.6\ln(1.4)+0.4\ln(0.6)$$

$$=0.6(0.33647224)+0.4(-0.51082562)\approx-0.00244691$$

Twice Kelly is **already negative**, not exactly zero. The geometric-equivalent rate there is $\exp(-0.00244691)-1\approx-0.24439\%$. The positive zero is approximately **$f_0=0.38939068$**, or **38.9391%** of bankroll.

| Stake fraction | Exact expected log growth | Geometric-equivalent return |
|---|---|---|
| 10%, half Kelly | 0.01504190 | About +1.51556% |
| 20%, full Kelly | 0.02013551 | About +2.03396% |
| 38.9391%, positive root | Approximately zero | Approximately zero |
| 40%, twice Kelly | −0.00244691 | About −0.24439% |
| 50% | −0.03397981 | About −3.34090% |

### A counterexample on the other side

For $p=0.4$ and $b=3$, Kelly is also 20%, yet:

$$g(0.4)=0.4\ln(2.2)+0.6\ln(0.6)\approx0.00888757>0$$

Its positive zero is **41.8624%**, above twice Kelly. A stake between 40% and 41.8624% still has positive expected log growth in this different model, although it over-bets the optimum.

### Where the twice-Kelly shortcut comes from

For small stakes, a second-order expansion is:

$$g(f)\approx af-\frac{c}{2}f^2,\quad a=pb-q,\quad c=pb^2+q$$

This **quadratic approximation** peaks at $a/c$ and crosses zero at $2a/c$. The zero is exactly twice the optimum of that quadratic model. The approximation can put the binary zero near twice exact Kelly for a small edge, but the identity does not hold for the general binary logarithmic curve.

### When to use it

Use the exact function when probabilities and binary payoffs are specified. Keep quadratic or continuous-model identities labelled with their assumptions. Negative $g$ implies wealth tends to zero almost surely over indefinitely repeated ideal bets; for $f<1$ it remains positive after every finite number of bets. Real minimum stakes, margin calls and spending needs can create practical stopping thresholds sooner.

## Fractional Kelly changes the trade-off under uncertain inputs

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="You size a bet from an estimated edge. Does staking half that estimated Kelly fraction guarantee positive growth under the true distribution?"
  options={[
    { text: 'No; the true edge may be different', correct: true },
    { text: 'Yes; halving eliminates estimation error' },
    { text: 'Yes; any fractional stake removes risk' },
  ]}
  explanation="Fractional Kelly reduces exposure relative to the estimated optimum. It does not make uncertain probabilities correct or turn a negative-edge bet into a positive-edge one."
/>

**Fractional Kelly** uses a chosen fraction of the estimated optimal stake, such as half or a quarter. It trades modelled growth against exposure and sensitivity to an overstated edge. It also changes finite-path losses. This growth-risk trade-off is discussed in [William Ziemba's Stanford educational seminar](https://or.stanford.edu/seminars/ziemba_111116.html).

### Worked growth and drawdown trade-off

For the known 60/40 even-money bet, half Kelly is 10%. Its expected log growth of 0.01504190 retains:

$$\frac{g(0.1)}{g(0.2)}=\frac{0.01504190}{0.02013551}\approx0.74703$$

That is **about 74.70%**, not exactly 75%, of maximum binary log growth. After five consecutive losses, €1,000 becomes €1,000 × 0.8⁵ = **€327.68** at full Kelly, versus €1,000 × 0.9⁵ = **€590.49** at half Kelly. Neither amount is zero; both can matter to a real cash requirement.

If a policy forces you to stop below €500, the full-Kelly path crosses that threshold after four consecutive losses: €1,000 × 0.8⁴ = **€409.60**. The ideal fractional model's “never exactly zero in finite time” property does not prevent that practical failure.

### Worked edge-estimation error

Suppose you estimate $p=0.60$ at even money and choose 20%, but the true probability is only **0.55**. Then:

$$g_{\text{true}}(0.2)=0.55\ln(1.2)+0.45\ln(0.8)\approx-0.00013774$$

The true optimum is 10%, where $g_{\text{true}}(0.1)\approx0.00500837>0$. Half of your estimated Kelly happens to equal true Kelly in this example; that coincidence is not a universal rule. If the true win probability were below 50% at even money, any positive stake would have negative expected log growth.

### When to use it

Use fractional sizing as an explicit exposure choice, then test probability error, changing payoffs, correlation and practical stopping rules. It does not guarantee a drawdown limit, a positive finite result or positive growth under a misspecified distribution. The next lesson develops fractional sizing further.
