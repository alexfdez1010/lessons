A strategy wins 60 of 100 recorded bets. At even money, inserting 0.60 into Kelly gives a 20% stake. Before using it, inspect what those records represent and what you are trying to optimise. One hundred independent trials, twenty outcomes copied five times and a changing trading regime do not provide the same evidence for the next bet.

This lesson adds an uncertainty model to the sizing decision. Every example is invented. An **edge** is a positive expected net payoff per unit staked under a stated model; the stake still determines whether expected log growth is positive. We use a binary bet that earns the stake on a win and loses the stake on a loss, with unused money earning zero. Wealth is positive, shorting and borrowing are excluded, and we state any fees or stake caps explicitly. These are decision models, not personal sizing recommendations.

## A win-rate estimate needs an information model

The **win probability** $p$ is an input to a model of future outcomes. The **sample win rate** $\hat p$ is a calculation on recorded outcomes. With 60 wins in 100 observations, $\hat p=60/100=0.60$. That arithmetic does not establish independence, a constant probability or relevance to the next market regime.

For independent Bernoulli trials, each outcome is 1 for a win and 0 for a loss, with the same fixed probability $p$. The count of wins has variance $Np(1-p)$, so the sample proportion has

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat p)=\frac{p(1-p)}{N},\qquad
\operatorname{SD}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}}.
$$

This is variability of the estimated proportion across repeated studies under the model. It is not the volatility of a trading account. [NIST's binomial reference](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda366i.htm) gives the fixed-probability count model and its standard deviation. Substituting $\hat p$ for an unknown $p$ produces a plug-in standard-error estimate, with additional limitations near boundaries or under dependence.

### The same hundred rows, different information

Under an invented model with $p=0.60$ and $N=100$ independent trials,

$$
\operatorname{SD}(\hat p)=\sqrt{0.60(0.40)/100}=0.04898979,
$$

or **4.898979 percentage points**. Using the observed 0.60 gives that same numerical plug-in estimate, not knowledge that the true $p$ equals 0.60.

Now suppose there are only 20 independent outcomes. Each one has been copied into five records, so every five-record block wins or loses together. The recorded win proportion is the mean of those **20 block outcomes**. Its standard deviation at the stipulated $p=0.60$ is

$$
\sqrt{0.60(0.40)/20}=0.10954451,
$$

or **10.954451 percentage points**. Sixty winning records can simply mean 12 winning blocks out of 20. Five copies of a result do not make five independent trials.

### When to use it

Inspect overlap, common market episodes, selection of successful strategies and changes in the payoff process before turning a sample into a probability model. The block example is exact for its stated construction; it is not a universal effective-sample-size formula. A standard error alone supplies neither a defensible probability interval nor a probability that a future position will be safe.

## A smaller fraction can still overbet the actual edge

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A 20% stake is optimal at p=0.60. If the actual p is 0.52, must halving that stake make expected log growth positive?" text="No. At a 10% stake and p=0.52, expected log growth is about −0.00101175. The optimum for that fee-free alternative is 4%; an arbitrary fractional rule is not a probability model." />

Let $f$ be the stake as a fraction of **current wealth**. With no costs, wealth changes by a factor $1+f$ on a win and $1-f$ on a loss. The model's expected natural-log increment per round is

$$
g(f,p)=p\ln(1+f)+(1-p)\ln(1-f).
$$

We write $\ln$ for the natural logarithm. The result is a log increment per round, not a cash amount, an arithmetic percentage return or an annual CAGR. A constant repeated-model interpretation needs its own assumptions, such as independent rounds with the same probabilities and a fixed rebalanced fraction.

At a known $p=0.60$, the fee-free even-money optimum is $2p-1=0.20$. If 0.60 is only an estimate, its optimum is conditional on that estimate. Imagine using a map whose hilltop moves when its probability input changes.

### The hill moves when p changes

The 20% stake gives, under the estimated probability,

$$
g(0.20,0.60)=0.60\ln(1.20)+0.40\ln(0.80)=0.02013551.
$$

Under an alternative $p=0.52$, the same stake gives

$$
g(0.20,0.52)=0.52\ln(1.20)+0.48\ln(0.80)=-0.01230170.
$$

Half of the estimated stake is 10%. That still gives $g(0.10,0.52)=-0.00101175$. The optimum for this alternative probability is 4%, with $g(0.04,0.52)=0.00080021$.

Starting with 1000 USD, the 20% stake at $p=0.52$ ends at 1200 on a win or 800 on a loss. Expected one-round wealth is $0.52(1200)+0.48(800)=1008$. Positive expected money growth coexists with negative expected log growth. The objective changes the answer.

### When to use it

Evaluate the actual proposed stake under several justified probability/payoff assumptions. “Half Kelly” states how a stake relates to an estimated optimum; it does not state which true probabilities make growth positive. It also cannot repair an omitted loss state, an invalid fill model or a missing trading cost.

## Worst-case sizing requires an explicit set and objective

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"You maximise the minimum expected log growth over p in [0.52,0.60], allowing cash and only nonnegative stakes. Which probability determines that minimum for a positive stake?"}
  options={[{"text": "The lower endpoint 0.52", "correct": true}, {"text": "The midpoint 0.56 automatically"}, {"text": "The upper endpoint 0.60"}, {"text": "A random endpoint selected after the outcome"}]}
  explanation={"For these wealth factors and a nonnegative stake, ∂g/∂p=ln((1+f)/(1−f)) is nonnegative."}
/>

A **robust**, or worst-case, decision specifies a set of distributions and chooses the feasible stake with the highest minimum objective across that set. Here the uncertain input is only $p$, with an assumed interval $[p_L,p_U]$. The interval is a modelling choice; it has no automatic confidence coverage.

With no fees and a chosen cap $F<1$, the decision is

$$
\max_{0\le f\le F}\ \min_{p\in[p_L,p_U]}g(f,p).
$$

Cash, $f=0$, is feasible and gives log growth zero. We do not force a positive stake merely because the point estimate is attractive. [Sun and Boyd's distributionally robust Kelly formulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.10371) uses this distinction between maximising a known-distribution objective and its worst value over a specified set. The binary arithmetic here is a separately derived teaching case.

### Why the lower probability is the worst case here

For a nonnegative stake,

$$
\frac{\partial g}{\partial p}=\ln\left(\frac{1+f}{1-f}\right)\ge0.
$$

Growth rises with $p$, so its minimum over the interval is at $p_L$. That conclusion uses the long-only even-money payoff; a different payoff or permission to reverse the bet requires a fresh analysis.

For the resulting objective,

$$
\frac{\partial g(f,p_L)}{\partial f}
=\frac{2p_L-1-f}{1-f^2}.
$$

The function is concave. Its interior optimum is $2p_L-1$, clipped to the feasible interval:

$$
f_{\mathrm{robust}}=\max\{0,\min(F,2p_L-1)\}.
$$

For the invented interval $[0.52,0.60]$ and cap $F=0.50$, choose $f=0.04$. Its worst expected log increment is 0.00080021, whereas the point-estimate stake of 20% has worst value −0.01230170 and half of it has −0.00101175.

Widen the assumed set to $[0.48,0.60]$. Any positive long stake has negative expected log growth at the lower endpoint, so the maximin choice is **cash, f=0**. The upper endpoint being favourable does not cancel the adverse endpoint in a minimum objective.

### When to use it

Use worst-case sizing when that is the stated objective and the uncertainty set has a defensible scope. Report what changing the set changes. A set that excludes the actual loss process cannot protect against it; nonnegative worst **expected** log growth is not a bound on realised losses, a drawdown probability or a capital guarantee.

## Probability-weighted uncertainty is a different decision

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"For one next fee-free even-money bet, p is 0.40 or 0.80 with equal model weights. You maximise their average expected log objective, with stake capped at 50%. Which result follows?"}
  options={[{"text": "The objective equals g(f,0.60), so its optimal stake is 20%", "correct": true}, {"text": "Uncertainty alone forces the stake to exactly half of 20%"}, {"text": "Worst-case p=0.40 must be used despite the average objective"}, {"text": "Average the two unconstrained optimal stakes and ignore the objective"}]}
  explanation={"Expected log growth is affine in p at fixed f. Mean p is 0.60. A worst-case criterion or another loss constraint would be a different decision."}
/>

Instead of guarding against every model in a set, you can assign probability weights to possible models and maximise their average expected log objective. Those weights need justification too. A **prior** states model weights before the data; a **likelihood** measures how compatible the observed data are with each model; a **posterior** updates their relative weights after the data.

Suppose the unknown, fixed win probability is 0.40 or 0.80, initially weighted one-half each. For the next single even-money bet with a common chosen stake, the objective is

$$
\frac12g(f,0.40)+\frac12g(f,0.80)=g(f,0.60).
$$

This is an exact identity because $g$ is affine in $p$ for fixed $f$. More generally,

$$
\mathbb E_p[g(f,p)]
=\bar p\ln(1+f)+(1-\bar p)\ln(1-f),\quad \bar p=\mathbb E[p].
$$

The one-step optimum at mean $\bar p=0.60$ remains 20%, including under the 50% cap used below. Uncertainty in $p$ has **not automatically inserted a fractional-Kelly penalty**. Worst-case log growth, average log growth and a drawdown constraint are different objectives. The [Columbia summary of Browne and Whitt's Bayesian Kelly research](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/portfolio-choice-and-bayesian-kelly-criterion) likewise identifies posterior means in its specified model; it is not a universal rule for every learning problem.

### Update the two model weights by hand

Observe three wins and two losses, independent conditional on the fixed $p$. For the specified ordered outcomes, likelihoods are

$$
L_{0.40}=0.40^3(0.60)^2=0.02304,\qquad
L_{0.80}=0.80^3(0.20)^2=0.02048.
$$

Using the count rather than its order adds the same binomial coefficient to both likelihoods, so it cancels in the weight ratio. Multiply each by its prior weight 0.50 and normalise:

$$
w_{0.40}=\frac{0.50(0.02304)}{0.50(0.02304)+0.50(0.02048)}=\frac9{17},
\qquad w_{0.80}=\frac8{17}.
$$

Thus the posterior mean is $(9/17)(0.40)+(8/17)(0.80)=10/17=0.58823529$. The one-step fee-free optimum is $2(10/17)-1=3/17=17.647059\%$ of current wealth. It fell because the data changed the mean, not because an unspecified uncertainty penalty was applied.

Try four wins and one loss instead. The likelihoods are 0.01536 and 0.08192, so posterior weights become $3/19$ and $16/19$. Their mean is $14/19$, giving a stake $9/19=47.368421\%$, still within the displayed cap. Bayesian updating can increase the chosen stake under these assumptions.

### The same next-bet probability need not mean the same path law

If the unknown $p$ is fixed across two future rounds, the mixture gives

$$
P(\text{two wins})=\tfrac12(0.40^2)+\tfrac12(0.80^2)=0.40.
$$

Two independent Bernoulli trials with a known $p=0.60$ instead give $0.60^2=0.36$. Conditional independence within a model is not unconditional independence across a mixture. The one-step objective identity does not make these repeated processes identical or settle every adaptive strategy.

### When to use it

State the prior, likelihood, observations available at the decision and objective before interpreting a posterior-based stake. Averaging **log objectives** is not taking the logarithm of expected money. Compare robust and weighted decisions as explicit choices, and keep model misspecification and future learning in view.

## Costs and feasible losses belong inside the payoff model

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A fee is 3% of the stake. At wealth 1000 USD and stake fraction 0.10, is the fee 30 USD?" text="No. The stake is 100 USD and its fee is 0.03×100=3 USD. A fee on stake has a different base from a fee on total wealth." />

A probability interval does not describe every source of uncertainty. Payoff asymmetry, fees, funding and feasible position limits can change the decision even if $p$ stays the same. We will keep the even-money payoff and add one explicit invented friction.

Let $c$ be a fee per unit **staked**, paid in either outcome. With current wealth $W$, a stake $fW$ pays $cfW$, so the wealth factors become

$$
1+(1-c)f\quad\text{on a win},\qquad
1-(1+c)f\quad\text{on a loss}.
$$

Both must remain positive for finite logarithms. The expected log objective is

$$
g_c(f,p)=p\ln[1+(1-c)f]+(1-p)\ln[1-(1+c)f].
$$

This is a stated teaching cost rule, not a universal commission, spread or impact formula. Under a nonnegative stake, the lower $p$ is still the worst interval endpoint. Differentiating the fee-adjusted objective gives its interior optimum

$$
\frac{\partial g_c}{\partial f}=\frac{2p-1-c-(1-c^2)f}{[1+(1-c)f][1-(1+c)f]},\qquad
f^*_c=\frac{2p-1-c}{1-c^2},
$$

again clipped to zero and the chosen cap. A lower-bound heuristic that ignores this fee solves a different problem.

### A 3% fee on stake changes the 4% robust result

Keep $[p_L,p_U]=[0.52,0.60]$ and set $c=0.03$. Then

$$
f_{\mathrm{robust}}=
\frac{2(0.52)-1-0.03}{1-0.03^2}
=\frac{0.01}{0.9991}=0.01000901,
$$

or **1.000901% of current wealth**, before any tighter cap. For wealth 1000 USD, the stake is 10.009008 USD and the fee 0.300270. A win leaves $1000+10.009008-0.300270=1009.708738$; a loss leaves $1000-10.009008-0.300270=989.690722$.

If the fee rises to 5% of stake, $2p_L-1-c=-0.01$. The maximin long-only decision becomes cash. Charging a fee only when a position is opened also means no fee at $f=0$ in this model.

### A one-round cap is not a lifetime drawdown limit

With no fee, suppose a constraint requires the next loss outcome to leave at least 85% of the wealth **just before that bet**. Then $1-f\ge0.85$, so $f\le0.15$. At known $p=0.60$, the unconstrained optimum is 20%; the constrained optimum is 15%. Its expected log increment is 0.01884959 instead of 0.02013551.

<RobustKellyChoice client:visible lower={60} upper={60} estimate={60} cap={15} />

The constraint does not promise to retain 85% of initial wealth forever. Five consecutive losses at a 15% rebalanced stake leave $100(0.85)^5=44.370531$ USD from 100. If the 3% fee applied too, the one-step inequality would instead be $1-1.03f\ge0.85$, or $f\le0.15/1.03=14.563107\%$.

A probability bound on breaching an initial wealth floor is a different, path-dependent constraint. [Busseti, Ryu and Boyd](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06183) study such constraints under an explicit repeated-gambling model; their conditional bounds should not be inferred from an arbitrary half-Kelly label or this one-step cap.

### When to use it

Write down net payoff states and constraints before optimising, including the option of no position. A defensible process can combine probability estimation, scenario stress, explicit costs and feasible funding; its output remains conditional. Document which decisions would change if the edge, payoff or trading environment changed, then evaluate the stated process on data that were available at each decision.
