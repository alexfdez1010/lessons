An allocation of (120%,−40%) has gross risky exposure 160%, yet its ideal cash account is **positive 20%**. An allocation of (80%,80%) has the same gross exposure and **60% cash debt**. The signs and funding rules matter as much as the sum of the absolute positions.

This lesson extends [the cash-benchmarked diffusion model](/kelly-and-cagr/continuous-and-fractional-kelly) to several assets. It also identifies where its inverse formula stops applying. All coefficients and scenarios are invented; an optimizer inside a model is not a recommendation to implement its exposures.

## Derive the risky vector and keep the cash account

Let f=(f₁,…,fₙ) contain each risky asset’s value divided by **current wealth**. A negative entry is a short position; 1 denotes the vector of ones. The signed cash fraction is $c=1-\mathbf1^\mathsf Tf$. In this ideal account, cash earns continuously compounded rate r and negative cash pays the same r; short-sale proceeds can be reused there. Actual collateral, short-borrow charges and rebates need their own cash rules.

For risky assets with constant total instantaneous drifts a, write **μ=a−r·1 for excess drifts**. Let Σ be their instantaneous return covariance matrix, with Σ_ij=σ_iσ_jρ_ij. All time units match, innovations are jointly Brownian, and trading continuously restores the fractions. Assume no costs, withdrawals, jumps or forced closeout. The wealth log drift is

$$
G(f)=r+g(f),\qquad g(f)=f^\mathsf T\mu-\frac12 f^\mathsf T\Sigma f
$$

The first term rewards modeled excess drift; the quadratic term accounts for the volatility of the **whole** allocation, including covariance. For fixed finite T, expected log wealth is ln(V₀)+G(f)T, so the same maximizer works for that objective at any fixed T>0. Its almost-sure long-run interpretation is another property, not a requirement to wait infinitely long. [Brockwell derives the multivariate self-financing diffusion and its cash/debt term in equations 28–39](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.10814).

If Σ is **positive definite**, every nonzero direction has positive variance. Then

$$
\nabla g(f)=\mu-\Sigma f=0,\qquad f^*=\Sigma^{-1}\mu,\qquad g(f^*)=\frac12\mu^\mathsf T\Sigma^{-1}\mu
$$

The gradient is the vector of first partial derivatives; the Hessian is the matrix of second derivatives. Here the Hessian is −Σ, so this stationary point is the unique unrestricted maximum. More precisely,

$$
g(f)=g(f^*)-\frac12(f-f^*)^\mathsf T\Sigma(f-f^*)
$$

### Work the asymmetric matrix rather than sizing two independent bets

Take excess drifts (8%,4%), volatilities (20%,10%) and correlation 0.3:

$$
\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.04&0.006\\0.006&0.01\end{pmatrix},\quad \det\Sigma=0.0004-0.000036=0.000364
$$

Multiplying the two-by-two inverse by μ gives

$$
f_A^*=\frac{0.01(0.08)-0.006(0.04)}{0.000364}=\frac{20}{13},\quad f_B^*=\frac{0.04(0.04)-0.006(0.08)}{0.000364}=\frac{40}{13}
$$

Thus f≈(1.538462,3.076923), signed total 60/13≈4.615385, and cash 1−60/13=−47/13≈−3.615385. The full vector’s variance is 16/65≈0.246154, and its excess log growth is 8/65≈**12.307692% per year**. At r=2%, absolute log growth is 14.307692%. None of these rates is a guaranteed realized annual simple return.

### When to use it

State the model, covariance, cash convention and permitted positions before using the inverse. The vector contains risky exposures; it need not sum to one. A sum-one tangency mix uses Σ⁻¹μ divided by its signed sum only when that denominator is nonzero and its sign/conventions support the comparison. Near-zero normalization can be fragile even when Σ is well conditioned. Optimizing finite expected log wealth also does not solve a different goal such as meeting a fixed withdrawal or minimizing a loss probability.

Two channels playing the same noise are not two independent channels. In the diffusion model, off-diagonal covariance changes the joint variance. It can also change the composition: it does not simply multiply every position by a common smaller number.

For two **symmetric** assets with common μ and σ and $-1<\rho<1$, solving the covariance system gives equal exposures

$$
f_A=f_B=\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2(1+\rho)}
$$

Here raising ρ reduces each exposure because means and variances are held equal. A general vector behaves differently. Keep volatilities (20%,10%), but set excess drifts to (8%,2%). At ρ=0, the solution is (2,2). At ρ=0.8,

$$
\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.04&0.016\\0.016&0.01\end{pmatrix},\quad f^*=(10/3,-10/3)
$$

A’s exposure **rises** while B becomes short. The mean difference relative to covariance favors a hedged direction. You can reproduce this by changing B’s excess drift and correlation in the first viewer. This is not evidence that the assumed spread is reliably tradable.

### When to use it

Use the symmetric shortcut only for its stated symmetric inputs. Zero instantaneous covariance in the jointly Brownian model means independent Gaussian innovations there; zero correlation in general return data need not imply independence. Also distinguish changing covariance from adding an asset. If the old portfolio remains feasible with a zero weight in the new asset, enlarging the opportunity set cannot lower the model’s **maximum objective**, since the old choice is still available. It says neither that every exposure rises nor that realized performance improves.

## A singular covariance can mean a family or an unbounded model

<MCQ

An ordinary inverse requires full rank. With a **positive semidefinite** covariance, some nonzero direction z can satisfy Σz=0. The model assigns that spread no diffusion variance. Examine its excess drift before reaching for a pseudoinverse.

Along f+t z, variance is unchanged and g changes by $t z^\mathsf T\mu$. If zᵀμ≠0 and t is unrestricted, the objective is unbounded in one sign of t. If μ is orthogonal to **every** null direction, stationary solutions can exist but are not unique: adding any null direction to a solution leaves g unchanged. A pseudoinverse can select one representative; it does not prove future risklessness.

For two exact clones with σ=20%,ρ=1 and μ=(8%,8%), the only effective risky quantity is f_A+f_B. Any pair summing to 2 is optimal: (2,0), (1,1) or (0,2), each with g=8%. The viewer displays the minimum Euclidean norm representative, (1,1), meaning the member with the smallest sum of squared fractions, and labels the family.

If their means are instead 8% and 4%, the model gives zero variance but positive excess drift to an A-minus-B spread. Its unrestricted optimum is unbounded. If ρ=−1 with equal positive means, an equal-long combination instead cancels the innovations while retaining positive drift, again giving an unbounded uncapped model.

### When to use it

Audit rank, redundant exposures and compatibility of means with weak/null risk directions. A hard feasible cap can make a bounded constrained problem even when the unrestricted model is unbounded. Estimation, omitted risks or frictions may explain implausible inputs; a numerical inverse or pseudoinverse does not establish an executable arbitrage. The sample-rank and sensitivity distinctions are developed further in [portfolio optimization](/portfolio-optimization/estimation-error-and-instability).

For marked risky holdings in this ideal cash account, **gross** exposure is |f_A|+|f_B|, **net** exposure is f_A+f_B, and cash is 1−net. From wealth 100, (1.2,−0.4) means long 120, short 40 and cash 20:120−40+20=100. Its gross 1.6 does not imply cash debt 0.6. By contrast, (0.8,0.8) means assets 80+80 and debt 60:80+80−60=100.

Reusing short proceeds is an assumption. A broker can segregate them, require extra collateral, charge stock borrow or pay a different rebate; then additional balances and constraints are needed. Derivative notional exposure is not automatically the same spot-asset value budget either.

### A cap requires reoptimization rather than automatic normalization

Return to the first asymmetric example and require f_A≥0,f_B≥0,f_A+f_B≤1. The original vector is infeasible. Dividing it by its sum gives (1/3,2/3), but that is not generally the constrained growth optimum.

On the fully invested edge, put f_A=x,f_B=1−x. The mean is 0.04+0.04 x and variance 0.01−0.008 x+0.038 x². Thus

$$
g(x)=0.035+0.044x-0.019x^2,\qquad g'(x)=0.044-0.038x>0\quad(0\le x\le1)
$$

The permitted maximum is therefore (1,0), with g=0.08−0.04/2=**6%**. The normalized raw direction gives approximately 4.755556%. For any nonnegative fractions summing at most one, the marginal excess growth from adding A is 0.08−0.04 f_A−0.006 f_B≥0.04. Leaving the cap unfilled is therefore suboptimal here. A different feasible set or different parameters can give another answer.

### A borrowing spread applies to debt rather than every position

Let the common cash rate be r but borrowing cost r+δ, δ≥0. With ideal reusable cash and no separate short fee, revise absolute log growth to

$$
G(f)=r+f^\mathsf T\mu-\frac12f^\mathsf T\Sigma f-\delta\max(\mathbf1^\mathsf Tf-1,0)
$$

Below net risky exposure one, no extra debt spread is charged. Above it, the derivative subtracts δ from each asset’s marginal excess drift; the kink at one must also be checked. Subtracting the whole borrowing rate from all drifts and keeping the original inverse can double-count the cash reference or charge debt where none exists.

For the symmetric 8%/20%,ρ=0.8 example, r=2%,δ=4% and a long-only sum cap 4, the debt-region equation gives each exposure 0.04/[0.04×1.8]=**5/9**. Their sum 10/9 lies above one, so that region is valid. Its net excess log growth is 14/225≈6.222222%, and absolute growth 8.222222%. At δ=6%, the optimum reaches the financing kink: each fraction 0.5, sum one, excess growth 6.2%. Changing funding changes the solution.

### When to use it

Specify cash access, short collateral, borrowing spreads, permitted leverage and costs in the objective and feasible set. A gross cap is a different restriction from a net long-only sum cap. Margin calls and execution can force earlier closing; their timing and prices depend on actual rules and paths. Neither ideal formula specifies a universal liquidation threshold, and the original unrestricted size is not a general upper bound on every defensible alternative objective.

Turning down every channel preserves the mix. A scalar can reduce total exposure to an estimated allocation while leaving the wrong composition in place. Parameter errors can affect means, covariance, funding or the feasible set; they need not all be optimistic.

Suppose the first example’s estimated excess drifts are (8%,4%) but, in an invented alternative truth, they are (2%,4%), with the same covariance. The estimated vector is $\hat f=(20/13,40/13)$. Under the alternative coefficients, with subscript v denoting the supplied true scenario,

$$
a=\hat f^\mathsf T\mu_{\rm v}=2/13,\qquad q=\hat f^\mathsf T\Sigma\hat f=16/65,\qquad g_{\rm v}(k\hat f)=ak-\frac12qk^2
$$

At k=1, actual excess growth is 2/65≈**3.076923%**; at k=0.5 it is 3/65≈**4.615385%**. The best nonnegative scalar along this one estimated ray is a/q=5/8, giving 5/104≈4.807692%. But reoptimizing under the specified true inputs gives f_v=(−10/91,370/91), with g_v*=73/910≈**8.021978%**. Scaling the old positive pair cannot create the negative A weight in that new unconstrained optimum.

If both actual excess drifts are zero and the covariance is positive definite, every nonzero k on the estimated ray has $g_{\rm v}=-k^2q/2<0$. A constraint or covariance target may improve a decision under some uncertainty model and add bias under another; neither is a blanket survival guarantee.

### When to use it

Perturb directions as well as total scale, examine feasible alternatives and distinguish supplied scenarios from estimates supported by data. A chosen half or quarter fraction is a decision rule to justify against objectives and uncertainty, not a theorem that a fitted vector becomes safe. A later lesson considers explicit uncertain-edge decision rules; the calculation here compares deterministic parameter scenarios only.

For a true unrestricted solution with q=μᵀΣ⁻¹μ>0, scaling it by k gives

$$
g(kf^*)=(k-\tfrac12k^2)q=k(2-k)g(f^*),\qquad G(kf^*)=r+g(kf^*)

Use excess drifts (2%,1%), the first covariance and r=2%. The optimum is (5/13,10/13), q=1/65 and g*=1/130≈0.769231%. At k=2.4, k(2−k)=−0.96, so g≈−0.738462%, but **G≈+1.261538%**. Under this exact constant model, wealth can grow absolutely almost surely as time tends to infinity while tending to zero relative to the cash account. At k=2, g=0 and G=r; zero excess log rate does not mean the cash-relative path is constant.

## A joint jump, a positive floor and a withdrawal are different failures

<MCQ
  client:visible

Hold fixed initial fractions (2,1) during an instantaneous jump, with wealth 100 and cash−200. A falls 50% and B falls 20% before rebalancing. Their asset values become 200×0.5=100 and 100×0.8=80. Net equity is 100+80−200=**−20**:

$$
\frac{V_1}{V_0}=1+f_A R_A+f_B R_B=1+2(-0.5)+1(-0.2)=-0.2
$$

This fixed-position jump is outside the continuous-path model used for the inverse formula. A nonpositive factor cannot be inserted into a real positive-wealth logarithm and compounded onward as though nothing happened. Actual closeout, collateral and recourse rules require their own contract assumptions.

For discrete joint states j with probabilities p_j and fixed cash growth factor B, a positive-wealth log objective instead evaluates

$$
\sum_j p_j\ln\left[f_A(1+R_{Aj})+f_B(1+R_{Bj})+(1-f_A-f_B)B\right]
$$

The factors must be positive in the modeled admissible states; the full joint distribution matters. A zero factor in a positive-probability state makes expected log wealth −∞; a negative factor lies outside that domain. [Busseti, Ryu and Boyd formulate general joint-return Kelly with explicit cash and separately define a probability constraint on falling below a specified fraction of initial wealth](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/papers/kelly.html). Their long-only cash budget differs from the leveraged example above. Their chosen initial-wealth drawdown definition also illustrates why the word needs a precise reference; it is not automatically a running-peak drawdown.

### A positive balance can still violate an operational need

For a true diffusion ray with q>0, the [fixed initial cash-relative floor calculation](/kelly-and-cagr/continuous-and-fractional-kelly) remains applicable: for b∈(0,1), k>0 and positive excess log drift, the infinite-horizon hitting probability is $b^{2/k-1}$. At true full Kelly it is b; at half it is b³. For b=0.5 these give 50% and 12.5%. Those exact scoped figures are not universal one-year loss probabilities. At k=0 the cash-relative account stays one; at positive k with nonpositive excess log drift, eventual hitting probability is one.

A fixed withdrawal is another cash budget. Start 100, lose 10%→90, withdraw 40→50, then gain 20%→60. Without the withdrawal, the endpoint would be 108. A requirement to withdraw 95 immediately after the first loss cannot be met from 90 without additional funding, despite positive wealth. The no-flow expected-log problem did not promise that payment.
