A €100 investment gains 50% and then loses 50%. You finish with €75. Its two annual returns average to zero, yet a quarter of the initial capital is gone. Both calculations can be correct: one averages percentages; the other follows the money through changing bases.

This lesson separates three jobs: describing individual returns, summarising compound investment growth, and measuring the experience of someone who adds money along the way. All examples are invented, use equally long one-year periods and total returns with distributions reinvested, and exclude fees and tax unless stated otherwise.

The **arithmetic mean** is the sum of the observed returns divided by their count. Like dividing a restaurant bill equally, it gives each observation the same weight. For $n$ equal-length periods with simple returns $r_i$ written as decimals:

$$\bar r=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n r_i$$

For the two annual observations, $(0.50-0.50)/2=0$, or **0% per year as an arithmetic mean**. This is a description of the two rates, not a claim that €100 ends at €100.

| Time | Calculation | Invested wealth |
|---|---|---:|
| Initially | Initial investment | €100 |
| After year one | €100 × 1.50 | €150 |
| After year two | €150 × 0.50 | €75 |

### A sample mean is not a known forecast

An **expected return** is a probability-weighted mean of possible future returns. If a model assigns equal probabilities to +50% and −50% next year, its expected one-year return is 0.5 × 50% + 0.5 × (−50%) = **0%**. A historical sample average can estimate such an expectation when the observations are representative of the relevant process. Two past years do not establish the future probabilities or guarantee their own average will repeat.

### When to use it

Use the arithmetic mean to summarise return observations or, with a justified model, estimate a one-period expected return. It is also useful in multi-period models: if independent annual returns have a **known** common expectation $\mu$, expected terminal wealth is $V_0(1+\mu)^n$. That is an average across possible paths, not the wealth achieved by each path. Replacing an unknown $\mu$ with a sample estimate introduces another estimation problem.

## Geometric means reproduce compound growth

A **growth factor** is $1+r$: a return of 20% multiplies wealth by 1.20; a loss of 10% multiplies it by 0.90. Compounding chains these factors, like successive gear ratios. For an investment with **no external contributions or withdrawals**:

$$V_n=V_0\prod_{i=1}^n(1+r_i)$$

The **geometric mean return** $g$ is the constant per-period return that reproduces that product:

$$1+g=\left(\prod_{i=1}^n(1+r_i)\right)^{1/n}$$

Thus the +50%/−50% pair gives $g=\sqrt{1.50(0.50)}-1=\sqrt{0.75}-1\approx-0.1339746$, or **−13.3975% per year**. The check is $100(1+g)^2=75$. Use the unrounded rate for an exact reconstruction.

### Work three years without premature rounding

| Year | Return | Growth factor | Wealth from €100 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Start | — | — | €100.00 |
| One | +20% | 1.20 | €120.00 |
| Two | −10% | 0.90 | €108.00 |
| Three | +15% | 1.15 | €124.20 |

The arithmetic mean is $(0.20-0.10+0.15)/3=0.0833333$, or **8.3333%**. The geometric mean is $1.242^{1/3}-1\approx0.0749143613$, or **7.4914%**. The exact product gives €124.20; compounding the arithmetic mean instead would give $100(1+1/12)^3\approx127.1412$, or **€127.14**. That latter number describes a different, constant-return path.

### CAGR specifies the annual unit

**Compound annual growth rate (CAGR)** is the constant annual rate between two positive values over $T$ years:

$$\operatorname{CAGR}=\left(\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right)^{1/T}-1$$

Without external cash flows, it equals the geometric mean of the annual total returns. For monthly observations, their geometric mean is a monthly rate: annualising it requires an annual time basis, not merely renaming it CAGR. For example, two monthly returns of 1% give total growth $1.01^2-1=2.01\%$ and a monthly geometric mean of 1%, not a 1% annual CAGR.

### When to use it

Use geometric growth to describe the compound return of a specified investment path. Match periods, currency, fees and distribution treatment when comparing records. A smoothed annual rate does not describe drawdowns or forecast future returns. A headline “average annual return” can already be compounded; inspect its methodology rather than assuming it is arithmetic. For example, the [SEC’s published fund-return formula uses a compound annual rate](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/2001/09/disclosure-mutual-fund-after-tax-returns).

Assume every $1+r_i>0$. The arithmetic–geometric mean inequality applied to those factors gives

$$\left(\prod_{i=1}^n(1+r_i)\right)^{1/n}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(1+r_i)=1+\bar r$$

Therefore $g\leq\bar r$, with equality only when all returns match. [OpenStax’s finance text distinguishes the two averages and their compounding roles](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance-2e/pages/15-1-risk-and-return-to-an-individual-asset).

### Hold the mean fixed in a two-year experiment

Let the two returns be $m+s$ and $m-s$. Their arithmetic mean is $m$, while

$$g=\sqrt{(1+m)^2-s^2}-1$$

At $m=0$, a 10% swing gives $g=\sqrt{0.99}-1\approx-0.5013\%$; a 30% swing gives $g=\sqrt{0.91}-1\approx-4.6061\%$. This **specific fixed-mean family** makes the wider swing lower compound growth. In the visual, set +10%/−10%, then +30%/−30%, keeping the contribution at zero.

### Variance alone does not determine every exact gap

Variance measures squared dispersion around the mean. The product also depends on the rest of the return pattern. Here are two original three-year series, both with arithmetic mean zero and descriptive variance 0.06 using divisor three:

| Series | Annual decimal returns | Product of growth factors | Geometric annual return |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| A | −0.30, 0, +0.30 | 0.9100000 | About −3.0948% |
| B | −a, −a, +2a, with a = √0.03 | 0.9203923 | About −2.7273% |

For A, variance is $(0.09+0+0.09)/3=0.06$. For B it is $(a^2+a^2+4a^2)/3=2a^2=0.06$, but the product is $(1-a)^2(1+2a)=1-3a^2+2a^3$. Equal means and variances therefore do **not** force equal geometric returns. The next lesson’s variance-drag formula is an approximation for simple returns, not an exact universal conversion.

### When to use it

The inequality is exact for the same positive factors. Use the actual product for an exact historical geometric mean. Keep any “more volatility, less growth” claim tied to a stated comparison or model. Fees, risk premia and changes in the mean can also change a strategy’s results; reducing variance alone is not a universal guarantee of higher realised growth.

## A loss changes the base required for recovery

A recovery gain is measured from what remains. After a loss fraction $L$, with $0<L<1$, returning to the initial value without adding money requires

$$(1-L)(1+G)=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad G=\frac{L}{1-L}$$

A 20% loss leaves €80 from €100. The missing €20 is 20/80 = **25% of the remaining balance**. A 50% loss leaves €50, requiring a 100% gain. The arithmetic is a base change, not a prediction about whether recovery will happen.

| Loss from €100 | Remaining wealth | Required gain | Check |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 10% | €90 | About 11.1111% | 90 × 1.111111… = 100 |
| 50% | €50 | 100% | 50 × 2 = 100 |
| 80% | €20 | 400% | 20 × 5 = 100 |
| 100% | €0 | No finite percentage gain | 0 × any finite factor = 0 |

A −100% return makes the geometric product zero, giving −100% as its geometric rate for the full series, but subsequent percentage returns from a zero balance are not defined normally. Returns below −100%, possible with some leveraged liabilities, fall outside the positive-factor model used here. The logarithmic formulas used later require strictly positive factors.

### When to use it

Use the recovery formula to translate a drawdown into the gain required on the remaining capital. Contributions can restore an account balance, but are new capital rather than investment recovery. Return order does not change the no-flow product: 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.5 × 1.5. Order still changes intermediate balances and drawdowns, and matters when flows or trading decisions depend on time.

An **external cash flow** is money entering or leaving the measured portfolio, such as an investor’s contribution or withdrawal. A dividend retained and reinvested inside that portfolio is part of its total return, not an investor contribution.

**Time-weighted return (TWR)** links investment returns over subperiods separated at external flows. In an exact calculation, value the portfolio immediately before and after each flow so that the flow itself is not treated as investment gain. **Money-weighted return (MWR)** reflects both investment results and the size and timing of the investor’s flows. A common MWR is an **internal rate of return (IRR)**: a discount rate equating the investor’s dated cash outflows and inflows. The [GIPS handbook explains this distinction and the valuation requirements](https://www.gipsstandards.org/standards/gips-standards-for-firms/gips-standards-handbook-for-firms/).

### Add €100 after the first annual return

Keep +50% then −50%, but contribute **€100 at time one, immediately after the first return and before the second**. Liquidate the whole account at time two.

| Time | Account calculation | Balance before final withdrawal | Investor cash flow |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Zero | Initial investment | €100 | −€100 |
| One, before contribution | €100 × 1.50 | €150 | €0 |
| One, after contribution | €150 + €100 | €250 | −€100 |
| Two, before liquidation | €250 × 0.50 | €125 | +€125 on liquidation |

The first subperiod return is 150/100 − 1 = **+50%**. The second is 125/250 − 1 = **−50%**. The total TWR remains $1.5(0.5)-1=-25\%$, annualising to **−13.3975%** over two years. Annualising linked performance uses a geometric calculation, as the [GIPS asset-owner handbook specifies](https://www.gipsstandards.org/standards/gips-standards-for-asset-owners/gips-standards-handbook-for-asset-owners/).

The investor instead supplies €100 for two years and another €100 for one year. With annual IRR $i$:

$$-100-\frac{100}{1+i}+\frac{125}{(1+i)^2}=0$$

Writing $x=1+i>0$ gives $100x^2+100x-125=0$. Its positive factor is $x=(-100+\sqrt{60000})/200\approx0.7247448714$, so **MWR = −27.5255% per year**. Check: $100x^2+100x=125$. This cash-flow pattern has one economically admissible root; more complicated sign-changing flows need not have a unique IRR.

By contrast, naively annualising the account balances gives $\sqrt{125/100}-1\approx+11.8034\%$. That **unadjusted balance-growth rate** treats contributed money as growth. It is not this investor’s performance. CAGR does not acquire a money-weighting adjustment simply because its inputs are account balances.

### Reverse the returns while keeping the contribution date

With −50% first, €100 becomes €50; add €100 to reach €150; then +50% produces **€225**. The time-weighted product is still 0.5 × 1.5 = 0.75, so its annual rate stays −13.3975%. The investor’s equation becomes $100x^2+100x=225$, giving **MWR ≈ +8.1139% annually**. More money was exposed to the positive second year. The two sequences share the no-flow compound return but deliver different cash-flow-weighted experiences.

### When to use it

Use linked TWR to compare the investment path while separating outside flows; use MWR to describe the investor’s dated experience. Neither is a universal replacement for the other. Reconstruct the valuations and flows before choosing a measure. With only an initial investment and final withdrawal, both annualised measures coincide with the no-flow CAGR. With intermediate contributions, raw endpoint balances are insufficient.
