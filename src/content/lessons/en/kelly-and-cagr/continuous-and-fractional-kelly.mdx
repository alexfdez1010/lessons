“Half Kelly keeps 75% of growth” is a precise result after specifying which growth, whose parameters and which financing model. Remove those conditions and the slogan can give the wrong number.

We use invented coefficients, measured with time in years. No calculation below recommends a position size. The aim is to distinguish an optimizer inside a model from a decision that must also handle estimation, funding, jumps, constraints and a stopping floor.

## Define the cash benchmark before deriving log growth

Let S be a risky asset price, a its instantaneous total return drift and σ>0 its diffusion volatility. Let B be an ideal cash account earning a constant continuously compounded rate r. Write **μ=a−r for excess drift**:

$$\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=a\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t,\qquad\frac{dB_t}{B_t}=r\,dt,\qquad\mu=a-r$$

W is standard Brownian motion: an increment over a time interval Δt is normal with mean zero and variance Δt, with independent increments over disjoint intervals. These are model assumptions, not a claim that actual market returns are fully described by two parameters. a is an instantaneous coefficient; it is not a historical arithmetic mean of annual simple returns.

Maintain fraction f of **current wealth** V in the risky asset by continuous rebalancing. The remaining fraction 1−f earns r when positive or pays r as debt when negative. At f=2, initial wealth 100 supports assets 200 and debt 100; borrowed funds are 100, not 200. Assume self-financing trading (trades funded entirely inside the portfolio), no outside flows, no costs, no jumps and the same available rate for cash and borrowing. Then

$$\frac{dV_t}{V_t}=(r+f\mu)\,dt+f\sigma\,dW_t$$

The lognormal solution implies the exact log-growth identity

$$\ln\frac{V_T}{V_0}=\left(r+f\mu-\frac 12 f^2\sigma^2\right)T+f\sigma W_T$$

Hence expected log growth per year is **absolute** $G(f)=r+g(f)$, where **excess log growth above cash** is

$$g(f)=f\mu-\frac 12 f^2\sigma^2$$

This quadratic is exact inside this diffusion and trading model. It is not obtained by declaring the annual simple-return shortcut from [the CAGR lesson](/kelly-and-cagr/cagr-and-volatility-drag) exact. [Brockwell’s primary derivation explicitly includes cash/debt at r and continuous reallocation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.10814).

### Reconcile three different rates

Take a=10%, r=2%, μ=8%, σ=20% and f=2. Then g=$2(0.08)-0.5(4)(0.04)=0.08$, while G=0.02+0.08=0.10. The simple-rate equivalent of absolute log growth is $e^{0.10}-1\approx 10.517092\%$. Relative to the cash account, it is $e^{0.08}-1\approx 8.328707\%$. Neither simple rate is numerically equal to its log rate.

A realized finite path includes $f\sigma W_T/T$ in its log growth. Under this constant-parameter model, maximizing expected log wealth also works for any fixed T>0 because T scales the same objective. It does not require waiting infinitely long, and it does not guarantee the realized maximizing outcome in a particular year. Almost-sure long-run growth is a separate asymptotic interpretation.

With σ>0, the common-rate quadratic has derivative $g'(f)=\mu-f\sigma^2$ and negative second derivative $g''(f)=-\sigma^2$. Its unconstrained optimum is

$$f^*=\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2},\qquad g(f^*)=\frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}=\frac 12\left(\frac\mu\sigma\right)^2$$

The ratio μ/σ here uses instantaneous diffusion coefficients with matched time units. It is not a promise that two strategies with the same estimated backtested Sharpe will deliver the same realized growth. Absolute optimal log growth also includes r. With unrestricted shorting, negative μ gives a negative optimum; under our **long-only** restriction it instead leads to zero risky exposure.

For an allowed interval $0\le f\le L$, where L is a leverage cap, the common-rate optimum is the clipped value $\min(L,\max(0,\mu/\sigma^2))$. With μ=8%, σ=20%, the unconstrained f*=2. If L=1.5, the feasible optimum is 1.5 and its excess log growth is $1.5(0.08)-0.5(1.5)^2(0.04)=0.075$.

Half that constrained size is f=0.75, giving $0.75(0.08)-0.5(0.75)^2(0.04)=0.04875$. That is 65% of 0.075, not 75%. Half of a constrained solution is not the half of the true unconstrained solution used in the familiar identity.

### Borrowing need not be free, or priced like a deposit

The initial model **charges r on debt**. Its strong assumption is access to the same rate in both directions. Let the borrowing rate instead be $r_b=r+\delta$, with δ≥0. For long-only f,

$$G(f)=r+f\mu-\frac 12 f^2\sigma^2-\delta\max(f-1,0)$$

For a=10%, r=2%, borrowing at 6% means δ=4%. Above f=1,

$$G(f)=0.06+f(0.10-0.06)-0.5 f^2(0.04)$$

That branch has its maximum at f=1; the lower-leverage branch is still increasing up to 1. The combined optimum is 1, with absolute log growth 8%. At f=2 the financing-adjusted G is 6%, rather than the 10% obtained under common-rate borrowing. With a 4% borrowing rate instead, the upper branch peaks at f=1.5 and G=8.5%. A funding change changes the objective, not merely a footnote below the same optimum.

### When to use it

Optimize the objective that actually includes permitted positions and funding. Do not call the raw formula an executable recommendation when a cap, shorting prohibition or different borrowing rate binds. σ=0 is outside the formula’s stated domain; a deterministic positive excess return without a leverage limit has no finite optimum in that idealized setup.

Return to positive μ, the common cash/debt rate and the unconstrained f*. Set f=kf*. Substituting μ=f*σ² gives

$$g(kf^*)=\frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2}\left(k-\frac 12 k^2\right),\qquad\frac{g(kf^*)}{g(f^*)}=k(2-k)$$

The wealth diffusion volatility is $|f|\sigma$; for k≥0 it is k times the full-Kelly volatility. At k=0.5, excess expected log growth is 75% of maximum and diffusion volatility is 50%. Those are exact model identities, not rounded empirical regularities.

For our μ=8%, σ=20%, r=2% case, full Kelly f=2 has g=8% and G=10%. Half Kelly f=1 has g=6% and G=8%. Thus half retains 75% of **excess log growth**, but 80% of **absolute log growth**. Its cash-relative simple equivalent is about 6.183655%, versus 8.328707% at full Kelly; that ratio is not exactly 75% either.

The parabola is symmetric about the peak: k=0.5 and k=1.5 have the same g, with diffusion volatilities 20% and 60% here. Under this particular growth-versus-diffusion-volatility comparison, the larger exposure has no growth advantage. This does not make one fraction a universal choice for every objective or investor. At k=2, excess log growth is zero; absolute log growth is still r, not necessarily zero.

At the estimated full stake f=2, true excess log growth is $2(0.02)-0.5(4)(0.04)=-0.04$. Half the estimated stake, f=1, gives $0.02-0.02=0$. Quarter of the estimated stake, f=0.5, gives $0.01-0.005=0.005$, which is the true optimum in this particular scenario.

The point is not that quarter Kelly is generally optimal. It is that “half” refers to a particular estimate, not automatically to the true optimum. An optimistic estimate can over-size exposure; a pessimistic estimate can under-size it. Uncertainty is not a mathematical proof of universal upward bias. Reducing size may reduce exposure to errors while sacrificing growth under the estimated model; it cannot manufacture a positive edge.

## Positive finite-time wealth can still hit a practical floor

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Does strictly positive wealth at finite times rule out reaching half of initial cash-relative wealth?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "No; a positive stopping floor can still be crossed",
    "correct": true
  },
  {
    "text": "Yes; every positive account stays above every floor",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "Yes; full Kelly prevents every finite loss",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"Exactly zero and a positive operational floor are different failure events."}
  pretest
/>


In the ideal constant-fraction diffusion, $V_T=V_0\exp(GT+f\sigma W_T)$ remains strictly positive at every finite time for finite f and positive initial wealth. That is a property of the assumed continuous paths and trading rule. It does not stop the account from becoming too small to meet a spending, margin or operational requirement.

For a precise floor calculation, measure wealth relative to cash:

$$X_t=\frac{V_t}{V_0 e^{rt}}=\exp(mt+sW_t),\qquad m=g(f),\quad s=f\sigma$$

Fix $0<b<1$ as a fraction of **initial cash-relative wealth**. For s>0 and m>0, the probability of ever reaching b over an infinite horizon is

$$\Pr\left(\inf_{t\ge 0}X_t\le b\right)=b^{2 m/s^2}$$

For m≤0 with s>0 it is one. With f=0, X stays one and the probability is zero. This follows by taking the upper barrier to infinity in the Brownian two-barrier hitting formula; it is an inference from that formula, not a universal drawdown rule. [UChicago’s primary lecture derives the drifted Brownian barrier probabilities](https://www.stat.uchicago.edu/~yibi/teaching/stat317/2021/Lectures/Lecture24.pdf).

For positive μ at true full Kelly in the common-rate model, $m=\mu^2/(2\sigma^2)$ and $s^2=\mu^2/\sigma^2$, so 2 m/s²=1. For b=0.5 the probability is exactly 50%. At half Kelly, the exponent is 3, so it is $0.5^3=12.5\%$. More generally, for $0<k<2$ that exponent is 2/k−1.

This 50% statement concerns an infinite-horizon initial floor measured against cash. It is not a 50% chance of losing half during the next year, nor a running-peak maximum drawdown probability, nor the result for every binary model. Changing the horizon, benchmark, stopping rule or model changes the question.

### When to use it

Define failure operationally: exactly zero, negative equity and crossing a positive stopping floor are distinct events. Finite positive model wealth does not guarantee practical survival. Negative asymptotic log growth can approach zero over time without producing exact zero after finitely many ordinary diffusion steps.

## A fixed leveraged position across a jump has a different budget

<MCQ
  client:visible

Suppose initial equity is 100. Buy 200 of a risky asset and borrow 100, keeping that position unchanged through one instantaneous price jump. Assume no interest accrues during the jump and no fees or collateral transfer. If the asset falls 60%, its value becomes $200(0.4)=80$. The debt is still 100, so final net equity is $80-100=-20$.

For initial risky fraction f and a single simple asset return R, this static budget is

$$V_1=fV_0(1+R)+(1-f)V_0=V_0(1+fR)$$

At f=2, R=−50% gives exactly zero equity; a larger drop produces a net liability. At f=1 and R=−60%, equity is 40 and remains positive. You cannot take a real logarithm of a zero or negative wealth factor and quietly continue the positive-wealth Kelly calculation.

The diffusion result and this jump budget use different assumptions. A continuously rebalanced diffusion position is not a fixed purchase held across an arbitrary terminal asset-price change. Real liquidation rules can act before the full loss, while a gap can skip a planned threshold and create execution slippage. A generic leverage number does not specify a broker’s margin or liability rules.

### When to use it

Reconcile actual assets, cash, debt and permitted trading when evaluating implementation risk. Add relevant costs, financing, discrete rebalancing, jumps and stopping constraints to the model used for a decision. The ideal Kelly optimizer answers its specified expected-log problem; it does not remove these constraints.
