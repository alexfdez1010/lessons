The collateral report says today's value is covered. The counterparty then misses a call, market prices move and the securities you hold become harder to sell. By the time replacement trades are in place, “covered today” describes an old photograph.

The previous lesson introduced margin's purpose. Here we follow the cash and the clock. All amounts below are **invented USD units**, in one enforceable netting set, from the perspective of the non-defaulting party. We assume our rights to use the specified received collateral are effective when stated. Actual documentation and insolvency law can change that assumption.

## Start the clock at the right observation

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A margin call uses day 0 market prices, the collateral arrives on day 1, and closeout plus rehedging finish on day 7. Under Basel CRE50.19's FAQ convention, which dates define MPoR?" options={[{text:'Day 0 to day 7: the last met call’s market observation is the origin',correct:true},{text:'Day 1 to day 7: receipt must always be the model origin'},{text:'Default notice to day 7, excluding the last covered observation'}]} explanation="The cited Basel FAQ interprets the last collateral exchange using the market observation for the last call the counterparty would meet. The settlement date does not shift that model origin." />

The **margin period of risk**, or **MPoR**, describes the interval over which exposure can change after the last covered margin observation until closeout and the resulting market risk are rehedged. **Closeout** determines and settles or pursues the contractual termination amount. **Rehedging** replaces or offsets the market risk left by terminating the trades. Determining an amount and actually obtaining replacement trades or sale proceeds need not happen together.

Terminology must be tied to a framework. In the [Basel bank-capital framework, CRE50.19 and its FAQ](https://www.bis.org/committees/bcbs/basel-framework/standard/cre/50/inforce/2019-12-15/published/2024-07-05), the relevant origin is the **market observation date corresponding to the last margin call that would be met**, rather than the later collateral settlement date. This is a model definition, not a universal contractual grace period.

In this example, MPoR under that Basel origin is $7-0=7$ indexed business days. The interval from actual receipt to completion is $7-1=6$ days. Both intervals can be useful operational information; they are not interchangeable. The amount is assumed fixed at 130 between days 5 and 7, without interest or other payments, so it is not a claim that derivative prices stop moving after termination.

[ISDA's closeout-framework notes](https://close-out.isda.org/docs/explanatory-notes.pdf) explain that enforcement assumptions, notices, stays, calculation time and the applicable collateral documents affect the sequence. They do not supply one guaranteed timetable for every relationship. A missed call also does not automatically give every party an immediate unrestricted right to seize every asset.

### When to use it

Date the valuation, call, settlement, default trigger, termination calculation, collateral access and replacement execution separately. State whether days are business or calendar days and the applicable calendar. The seven indexed days here are chosen teaching inputs, not a regulatory minimum or a promised legal deadline.

## Credited collateral is not the cash it will realise

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Securities worth 125 are credited at 100 after a 20% haircut. Is the 25 difference an immediate fee or an intentionally unsecured exposure of 25?" options={[{text:'Neither: the haircut is a conservative collateral-credit rule',correct:true},{text:'Yes: the receiver immediately loses 25 in cash'},{text:'Yes: a haircut always leaves that exact amount unsecured'}]} explanation="125 × (1−0.20) = 100 covers a mark of 100. The haircut requires more collateral market value; it is not the same as a threshold that deliberately allows an unsecured amount." />

A **haircut** discounts an asset's market value for collateral-credit purposes. It is like allowing a smaller borrowing amount against a house than its current appraisal: a buffer, not a prediction of its eventual sale price.

Let the last covered mark be $V_0=100$ and haircut $h=20\%$. To credit 100 requires securities market value:

$$
M_0=\frac{V_0}{1-h}=\frac{100}{0.8}=125,\qquad C_{\text{credited},0}=125(0.8)=100.
$$

Now impose a **20% market-price decline** in those same securities, then a distinct **5% liquidation discount** relative to their stressed market quote. The latter represents a chosen sale-price shortfall, not a universal transaction fee:

The two occurrences of 20% have different roles: an initial credit rule and a subsequently stipulated market shock. Do not deduct the initial haircut **again** from the actual sale proceeds. The calculation already tracks what the assets sell for.

<CloseoutGapExplorer client:visible mode="collateral" title="A haircut credits value; the later sale produces cash" chartLabel="Four bars show initially posted securities, credited collateral, stressed market value and net sale proceeds, in that order. The initial haircut is a credit rule and is not charged again against sale cash." />

At inception, the 100 credit covers the mark of 100. Later, sale proceeds are 95. That outcome does not mean haircuts create a hole by design. The realised price and liquidity shock exceeded the protection needed in this scenario. A contractual **threshold**, by contrast, explicitly permits some unsecured exposure; a **minimum transfer amount** can delay small transfers. Collateral eligibility, currency and ability to access it also matter.

### When to use it

Stress the collateral and the trade together. The [Basel Committee's 2024 CCR-management guidelines](https://www.bis.org/publications/202412-guidelines-final-guidelines-counterparty-credit-risk-management.pdf) ask banks to challenge collateral liquidity and mitigation under stress. A haircut cannot guarantee execution at a quoted price, instant access or a sale before the market changes further.

## Residual exposure, recovery and costs are separate amounts

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A positive claim is 130 and usable collateral is 105. Before applying any recovery rate, which amount remains unsecured?" options={[{text:'25, the claim minus usable collateral',correct:true},{text:'15, already treating recovery as part of exposure'},{text:'130, ignoring the usable collateral'}]} explanation="Exposure remaining after collateral and the eventual unrecovered loss are separate steps. First compute max(130−105,0)=25." />

Use the agreed positive closeout claim $V_c=130$. The securities realise 95. We also hold cash **initial margin of 10**, and assume it is legally available to satisfy this defaulted netting set. Merely seeing collateral in custody does not establish that right. If it is blocked or unavailable, exclude it from this ledger.

The **residual unsecured claim** is the positive amount still owed after applying usable collateral:

$$
C_{\text{usable}}=95+10=105,\qquad E_c=\max(V_c-C_{\text{usable}},0)=\max(130-105,0)=25.
$$

This $E_c$ is conditional exposure in the chosen closeout scenario, not default probability. Assume a recovery rate $R=40\%$ on **only the remaining unsecured claim**. Then recovery is $0.4(25)=10$ and conditional credit loss is $(1-R)E_c=0.6(25)=15$.

Separately suppose we pay replacement execution cost $K=5$, not included in the agreed claim and assumed not recoverable. Total economic shortfall in this limited ledger is $15+5=20$. The cash reconciliation is:

$$
\underbrace{130+5}_{\text{replacement value and separate cost}}-\underbrace{95}_{\text{securities cash}}-\underbrace{10}_{\text{usable IM}}-\underbrace{10}_{\text{estate recovery}}=20.
$$

The 5 lost in selling collateral below its stressed quote is already reflected in proceeds of 95. The separate 5 is a different assumed expense of replacing the trades. Counting either one twice would inflate the loss. If contractual closeout terms instead include a recoverable execution adjustment, redefine the claim and recovery calculation explicitly; do not silently add that cost again.

With the 10 of IM unavailable, usable collateral is only 95. The gap becomes $130-95=35$, credit loss $0.6(35)=21$, and total shortfall $21+5=26$. Conversely, extra collateral cannot create a negative exposure or profit: use a zero floor and return any surplus under the stipulated rights. A negative derivative value may mean **we owe** the counterparty; this one-sided received-collateral illustration does not model that obligation or claims for return of our own posted margin.

### When to use it

Use one currency, valuation basis and enforceable netting set. Define which received assets can actually be applied, which costs are part of the claim, the recovery base and the timing of recovery. Discounting, interest, unpaid contractual payments, posted-collateral return exposure and multiple legal estates need a richer model than this deliberately bounded ledger.

## A stress path is not a probability model

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Three deliberately chosen stress cases have losses 8, 20 and 11, with no probability weights. Does the largest establish a 99% quantile?" options={[{text:'No: chosen scenarios do not assign that probability level',correct:true},{text:'Yes: every largest scenario is a 99% quantile'},{text:'Yes: the maximum necessarily equals expected loss'}]} explanation="The cases provide conditional losses. A quantile or expectation requires a probability model beyond this unweighted list." />

A longer closeout window allows more time for markets and liquidity to change. It does not force the realised exposure to rise at every later date. Consider three invented completions measured from the same day 0 observation. Each uses usable collateral 105, recovery 40% and separate cost 5:

The later partial reversal reduces the realised gap from 25 to 10. This does not prove that a longer model horizon is safer. It distinguishes **one path** from a distribution of future paths.

<CloseoutScenarioComparison client:visible />

No probabilities were assigned, so $\max(8,20,11)=20$ is merely the largest loss among these three chosen scenarios. It is not a 99% quantile, expected loss, required IM, regulatory capital or CVA. Averaging them equally would add an unsupported probability assumption.

For a separate diffusion approximation, independent zero-mean daily changes with variance $\sigma^2$ give horizon variance $n\sigma^2$ and standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt n$. A doubling from 5 to 10 days would multiply that standard deviation by $\sqrt2$. Jumps, serial dependence, changing volatility, concentrated positions, liquidity costs and collateral co-movement can invalidate that shortcut. An IM quantile is conditional on its calibrated model; it does not guarantee that a given percentage of real-world defaults will be fully covered. IM can also be recalculated as risk changes, rather than remaining fixed forever.

### When to use it

Use scenario analysis to find vulnerabilities, compare collateral access assumptions and identify cash needed before recoveries arrive. Even an ultimately recoverable claim can require temporary funding for replacement trades. Collateral reduces risk subject to legal and operational effectiveness; it does not provide an instant, universal transfer of spendable cash at default.
