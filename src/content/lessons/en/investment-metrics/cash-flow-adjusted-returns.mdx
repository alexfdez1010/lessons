Ana's account starts with €1,000 and ends with €1,890 two years later. An 89% gain? She also deposited another €1,000 halfway through. Her investments actually lost €110. The account balance tells you where the money ended up; a return calculation also needs to know how it arrived.

You already know percentage return, compounding and CAGR. Here you will separate external cash flows from investment results, calculate two return measures and decide which question each answers. All examples are invented, use one currency and exclude fees and taxes unless stated otherwise.

## Separate contributions from investment gains

<MCQ client:visible pretest

    { text: 'The investments earned a profit' },

  ]}
  explanation="A transfer increases the balance without being earned by the investments. Contributions and performance need separate records."

An **external cash flow** moves money or assets across the boundary of the portfolio you are measuring. A contribution comes in; a withdrawal goes out. Moving cash between two holdings inside that same portfolio is internal. A dividend received from a holding is investment income, not a new contribution from the owner. If that income is later paid out of the portfolio, record the withdrawal as well as the income.

Think of a shop's till: putting change into it before opening increases the cash available, but it is not a sale. The portfolio boundary plays the same bookkeeping role. A transfer between your two accounts can be external for either account measured separately and internal for the combined portfolio.

For a portfolio with an initial value and later external flows:

$$\text{investment gain}=\text{ending value}+\text{withdrawals}-\text{starting value}-\text{contributions}$$

### Worked example

Ana's market returns are **+10% in year one**, then **−10% in year two**. Her contribution arrives exactly between the two years, after the first year's return and before the second year's return.

| Event | Calculation | Portfolio value |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Initial contribution | €1,000 |
| End of year one, before flow | €1,000 × 1.10 | €1,100 |
| Contribution at year-one boundary | €1,100 + €1,000 | €2,100 |
| End of year two | €2,100 × 0.90 | €1,890 |

Investment gain = €1,890 − €1,000 − €1,000 = **−€110**. Dividing that loss by total contributions gives −5.5%, but does not account for one contribution being invested for two years and the other for only one. It is not an annual return.

### When to use it

Reconcile money amounts first: beginning balance, contributions, withdrawals, ending balance and investment gain. This catches the tempting mistake of treating a rising balance as a profitable investment. To turn the gain into a rate that treats timing consistently, continue with TWR or MWR.

## Chain the investment returns with TWR

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Two investors hold the same strategy, but one adds money before a losing year. Which measure isolates the strategy's sequence of returns?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Time-weighted return', correct: true },
    { text: 'Money-weighted return' },
    { text: 'Change in account balance' },
  ]}
  explanation="TWR links returns for intervals separated by external flows. The amount the investor contributes does not itself count as performance."
/>

**Time-weighted return (TWR)** geometrically links the portfolio's returns between external cash flows. For an exact calculation, value the portfolio immediately before each flow and start the next interval immediately after it. If no other flows occur within an interval, its return is:

$$r_j=\frac{V_{j,\text{end before flow}}}{V_{j,\text{start after flow}}}-1$$

For several intervals:

$$R_{\text{TWR}}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1+r_j)-1$$

The product symbol means multiply the growth factors. The word “time” does not mean take an arithmetic average of the returns. Even when the intervals have unequal lengths, their growth factors are multiplied; only the later annualization needs the total elapsed time.

### Worked example

In Ana's first year, €1,100 ÷ €1,000 − 1 = **10%**. The contribution then resets the capital base to €2,100. In year two, €1,890 ÷ €2,100 − 1 = **−10%**.

$$R_{\text{TWR}}=1.10\times0.90-1=-0.01=-1\%$$

That is the **total return over two years**. The constant annual equivalent is:

$$r_{\text{TWR, annual}}=(1-0.01)^{1/2}-1\approx-0.005013=-0.5013\%$$

A euro invested at the start and left untouched would end at €0.99. This hypothetical euro traces the investment path without giving Ana's later contribution extra influence on the return measure.

<CashFlowReturnComparison client:visible />

Move the contribution to zero, then increase it. The market path stays +10% followed by −10%, so total TWR stays −1%. The money lost changes because a different amount is exposed to the losing year. The next section explains the MWR readout.

### When to use it

TWR helps compare a strategy or manager when external flows are controlled by the investor. Match the benchmark's period, currency and fee basis. TWR does not remove trading decisions, market risk or costs included in the portfolio values. Exact flow-boundary valuations matter; estimated or infrequent valuations can leave timing effects in an approximation. [GIPS Handbook for Firms, calculation methodology](https://www.gipsstandards.org/standards/gips-standards-for-firms/gips-standards-handbook-for-firms/)

## Let the cash-flow dates matter with MWR

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Ana puts more money to work just before the losing year. Compared with the strategy's annualized TWR, what would you expect for her annual MWR?"
  options={[
    { text: 'A lower rate for this cash-flow pattern', correct: true },
    { text: 'The same rate for every cash-flow pattern' },
    { text: 'A higher rate because she deposited more' },
  ]}
  explanation="More of Ana's money experiences the losing year. A money-weighted calculation captures that exposure; the contribution itself is still not a loss."
/>

A **money-weighted return (MWR)** reflects both the investment outcome and the size and timing of external flows. One common method is the **internal rate of return (IRR)**: the constant rate that makes the present value of cash received equal to the present value of cash paid. Discounting converts a later amount to its equivalent at the starting date.

Use the investor's viewpoint for signs. Money invested is negative; money received is positive. Treat the terminal portfolio value as a final receipt for the calculation, even if Ana has not actually sold everything.

| Time in years | Investor cash flow | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | −€1,000 | Initial investment |
| 1 | −€1,000 | Additional contribution |
| 2 | +€1,890 | Terminal value |

The annual IRR solves:

$$0=-1000-\frac{1000}{1+r}+\frac{1890}{(1+r)^2}$$

Multiply by $(1+r)^2$ and set $x=1+r$:

$$1000x^2+1000x-1890=0$$

The economically relevant root has $x>0$:

$$x=\frac{-1000+\sqrt{1000^2+4\times1000\times1890}}{2\times1000}\approx0.962874$$

Therefore $r\approx-0.037126$, or **−3.7126% per year**. Check it forwards: €1,000 × 0.962874² + €1,000 × 0.962874 ≈ **€1,890**. The first contribution compounds for two years and the second for one.

### A useful boundary check

With no second contribution, the terminal value is €990 and the equation becomes €1,000 × $(1+r)^2$ = €990. MWR is then **−0.5013% per year**, equal to annualized TWR. Do not compare that annual rate with total TWR of −1% as though they described the same horizon.

### When to use it

MWR describes the result associated with the investor's actual cash-flow schedule. A difference from TWR can arise from cash timing; it does not by itself prove skill, poor decisions or a calculation error. If the manager controls when capital is called or distributed, those timing choices can also be relevant to assessing the manager. [CFA Institute, TWR and IRR differences](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2017/04/using-brinson-attribution-to-explain-the-differences-between-time-weighted-twr-and-money-weigh)

## Compare compatible rates and check the inputs

For Ana's original contribution schedule, these figures are all consistent:

| Measure | Result | Question answered |
|---|---|---|
| Investment gain | −€110 | How many euros were earned or lost, excluding contributions? |
| Total TWR | −1% over two years | How did the linked investment path grow? |
| Annualized TWR | About −0.50% a year | What constant yearly rate matches that path? |
| Annual MWR by IRR | About −3.71% a year | What rate fits the investor's dated cash flows? |

### Worked comparison with a different contribution

Keep the same initial €1,000 and the same annual market returns, but contribute only **€500** at the boundary. The second-year starting value is €1,100 + €500 = **€1,600**. The ending value is €1,600 × 0.90 = **€1,440**, and the investment loss is €1,440 − €1,000 − €500 = **−€60**.

The IRR now solves $1000x^2+500x-1440=0$. Its positive root is $x\approx0.975765$, giving **−2.4235% a year**. Total TWR remains −1%. Lower contribution reduced the capital exposed to the losing year; it did not change either year's investment return.

Before interpreting a reported rate, check:

- **Dates and units** — irregular cash flows need their actual dates and a stated year-fraction convention. Equal annual periods are a simplification of this example.
- **Valuations** — a stale terminal value affects MWR as well as TWR. MWR needing fewer intermediate valuations does not make the ending value reliable.
- **Fees and distributions** — define what “net” includes. Income retained in the portfolio is already in its value; adding it again double-counts it. Do not reclassify a fee as an owner withdrawal merely to make performance look better.
- **IRR existence and uniqueness** — our contributions are followed by one positive terminal receipt, giving one positive growth-factor root. Cash flows that switch signs repeatedly can admit multiple IRRs or no usable IRR. A spreadsheet displaying a rate is not a uniqueness proof. [GIPS Handbook, IRR discussion](https://www.gipsstandards.org/standards/gips-standards-for-firms/gips-standards-handbook-for-firms/)

### When to use it

Use a consistently calculated TWR for comparing investment paths and a dated MWR for the investor's cash-flow experience. Where useful, report both alongside the gain amount. Neither measure by itself captures drawdowns, liquidity, investment risk or whether the investment meets a spending goal.
