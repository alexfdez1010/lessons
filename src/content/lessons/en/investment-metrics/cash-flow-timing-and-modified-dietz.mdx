Your account statement has an opening value, a closing value and dated deposits and withdrawals. It has no reliable valuation immediately before each cash flow. You can reconcile the gain in money, but an exact time-weighted return needs information the statement does not contain.

[The preceding cash-flow lesson](/investment-metrics/cash-flow-adjusted-returns) distinguished time-weighted return (TWR) from money-weighted return (MWR). This lesson adds **Modified Dietz**, a weighted-capital approximation that can be calculated with fewer valuations. We will calculate it, explain the timing assumptions and deliberately push it beyond the conditions where it works well.

All examples are invented, use one currency and total portfolio values, and exclude fees and taxes unless stated. Contributions are **positive from the portfolio's viewpoint** and withdrawals negative. Investment income generated inside the portfolio is not an owner contribution. Returns here cover the whole stated measurement period, not a year unless that period is one year.

## Divide the gain by time-weighted capital

A deposit halfway through the month was not available to invest during the first half. Modified Dietz starts with the opening capital, then adds each flow multiplied by the fraction of the measurement period remaining after that flow. A withdrawal subtracts weighted capital: the money was no longer available after it left.

Let $V_B$ and $V_E$ be the beginning and ending values, $C_i$ an external cash flow and $t_i$ its elapsed date within a period of length $T$. With the end-of-day elapsed-time convention used here:

$$
w_i=\frac{T-t_i}{T},\qquad
 G=V_E-V_B-\sum_iC_i,\qquad K=V_B +\sum_iw_iC_i.
$$

For **positive weighted capital** $K$:

$$
R_{\mathrm{MD}}=\frac{G}{K}=\frac{V_E-V_B-\sum_iC_i}{V_B +\sum_iw_iC_i}.
$$

The numerator $G$ is the investment gain in money after removing net external funding. The denominator $K$ is beginning value plus signed time-weighted flows. It is **not** an observed average of the portfolio's daily market values. Calling it an average capital base must not imply that we measured the market path.

### Work one contribution and one withdrawal

A portfolio begins at \$100 and ends at \$130 after 30 days. It receives \$40 at elapsed day 6 and pays out \$20 at elapsed day 24. The ending value includes both external flows.

Net external funding is $40-20=20$. Therefore:

$$
G=130-100-(40-20)=10,
$$

$$
K=100+32-4=128,\qquad R_{\mathrm{MD}}=\frac{10}{128}=0.078125=7.8125\%.
$$

The account balance rose 30%, but \$20 of its \$30 increase came from net funding. Dividing the \$10 gain by the opening \$100 would also ignore when that funding was present. Dietz's 7.8125% is a **30-day approximate return**, not an annual rate.

### When to use it

Use a clearly labelled approximation when beginning and ending valuations and complete dated flows are available, but flow-boundary valuations are not. Check the portfolio boundary first: moving money between two holdings inside the same measured account is not an external flow. Dividend income inside the account belongs in performance; distributing that income outside creates a separate withdrawal. Missing flows or stale valuations are data errors that this formula cannot repair.

### End-of-day and beginning-of-day are different policies

For a 30-day month, a cash flow booked at the **end of day 6** has 24 days remaining, so $w=(30-6)/30=0.8$. If it instead occurs at the **beginning of day 6**, it is exposed for an extra day under that discrete daily convention:

$$
w_{\mathrm{beginning\ of\ day}}=\frac{30-6+1}{30}=\frac{25}{30}\approx 0.833333.
$$

A \$40 contribution then adds $40(25/30)=33.333333\ldots$ to weighted capital instead of \$32. Neither weight is automatically correct for every reporting system: document when flows are recognized and apply the policy consistently. The visual uses elapsed end-of-day dates; it does not silently add a beginning-of-day adjustment.

Irregular dates need their actual elapsed intervals. A February period, a 31-day month and a quarter do not share an automatic 30-day denominator. For example, a flow after 10 elapsed days in a 31-day period has weight $21/31\approx 0.677419$, not $20/30$. Record currency, valuation time, cash-flow recognition and treatment of noncash transfers together.

### When to use the convention

Use it when reconciling two reports or implementing the formula from transaction records. An otherwise small weight difference can matter when a flow is large or weighted capital is near zero. A changed convention is not evidence of skill or a changed market return. [The GIPS Handbook explains daily weighting and the need for a consistent beginning/end-of-day policy](https://www.gipsstandards.org/standards/gips-standards-for-firms/gips-standards-handbook-for-firms/).

## An approximation is not an exact IRR or exact TWR

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="We know beginning value, ending value and dated cash flows, but no intermediate market valuations. What can Modified Dietz reconstruct exactly from those inputs?"
  options={[
    { text: "The money gain reconciliation, while its percentage return remains an approximation", correct: true },
    { text: "The complete sequence of returns immediately before every flow" },
    { text: "The strategy's exact cash-flow-neutral time-weighted return" },
    { text: "The same compounded root as IRR for every possible return and flow size" },
  ]}
  explanation="The bookends and complete flows reconcile gain. They do not reveal the internal price path. Dietz uses time weights rather than solving a compounded equation or observing each flow boundary."
/>

An exact **cash-flow IRR for the period** seeks a constant compounded rate $r$ that fits the dated funding and terminal value. In a future-value form with our portfolio flow signs:

$$
V_E=V_B(1+r)+\sum_iC_i(1+r)^{w_i}.
$$

For small rates near zero, the first-order expansion is $(1+r)^w\approx 1+wr$. Substituting it gives:

$$
V_E\approx V_B +\sum_iC_i +r\left(V_B +\sum_iw_iC_i\right).
$$

Rearranging produces the Dietz formula. This derivation explains its **linear weighted-capital approximation**. It does not establish that the approximation is accurate for every return, every flow or every denominator. Repeatedly changing signs may also complicate the existence or uniqueness of the exact IRR; finding one spreadsheet result is not a proof.

### A modest-rate case shows a small but real difference

Begin with \$100, add \$10 halfway through a measurement period and finish at \$120. Dietz gives:

$$
R_{\mathrm{MD}}=\frac{120-100-10}{100+0.5(10)}=\frac{10}{105}\approx 9.523810\%.
$$

<ModifiedDietzTimeline client:visible title="The simple weighted result need not equal the compounded root"
  beginningValue={100} endingValue={120} duration={30}
  flows={[{ label: 'Midperiod contribution', amount: 10, time: 15 }]} />

The exact period IRR solves $120=100(1+r)+10\sqrt{1+r}$. Put $x=\sqrt{1+r}>0$:

$$
100 x^2+10 x-120=0,\qquad
 x=\frac{-10+\sqrt{48{,}100}}{200},\qquad r=x^2-1\approx 9.534144\%.
$$

The difference is about 0.0103 percentage point here. We can check the chosen root by substituting it into the original terminal-value equation. Exact TWR still cannot be calculated from these bookends alone: it would additionally need the value immediately before the contribution. A close Dietz/IRR match does not supply that missing valuation.

### Large flows and a nonlinear path can make the difference large

Now disclose the path in an extreme invented 30-day example. The initial \$100 doubles to \$200 over the first 15 days. A \$1,000 contribution then makes the portfolio \$1,200. Over the next 15 days it loses half its value, ending at \$600.

The IRR calculation becomes $100 x^2+1{,}000 x-600=0$, with positive growth-factor root $x=(-10+\sqrt{124})/2$. Thus $r=x^2-1\approx-67.7644\%$ for the period. Dietz differs by about 15.57 percentage points from that compounded rate, while exact TWR answers the distinct path question and is zero. All three results have the same 30-day horizon; no annualization explains the gap.

The large contribution puts much more money into the losing interval. The linearized capital weighting also misses important compounding curvature. [The GIPS Handbook identifies large flows combined with nonlinear returns as a particular weakness of this approximation](https://www.gipsstandards.org/standards/gips-standards-for-firms/gips-standards-handbook-for-firms/).

### When to use a more detailed calculation

If reliable values at flow boundaries exist, use them to form exact subperiod returns and geometrically link them for TWR. If the question is the investor's dated cash-flow experience, solve the appropriately dated IRR and inspect its roots. A return process may link Dietz estimates between valuation breaks to approximate TWR; that does not turn a single bookend estimate into a true TWR. Flow size, path variation, valuation quality and measurement purpose determine what additional information is useful. There is no universal small-flow cutoff assumed in this lesson.

## Zero and negative weighted capital need an explicit stop

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The weighted capital denominator is zero while the gain is positive. What should a conventional Dietz report show?"
  options={[

    { text: "Zero return because division by zero cancels the gain" },
    { text: "A positive infinity return as proof of exceptional performance" },
    { text: "The gain amount with a percentage sign added" },
  ]}
  explanation="A zero denominator does not define a finite Dietz rate. Keep the money reconciliation and inspect the measurement period, dates and valuations."
/>

Weighted capital is an algebraic approximation, not a guarantee of a positive amount. A large withdrawal can make it zero or negative even when all recorded market values are nonnegative.

Consider a 30-day portfolio starting at \$100. By day 15 it has grown to \$200; the owner withdraws all \$200, leaving zero, and the period ends at zero. The bookends and withdrawal give:

$$
G=0-100-(-200)=100,\qquad K=100+0.5(-200)=0.
$$

The \$100 gain is real within this invented record. Division by zero is undefined; report no finite conventional Dietz rate and explain the chosen boundaries.

<ModifiedDietzTimeline client:visible title="Positive money gain with zero weighted capital"
  beginningValue={100} endingValue={0} duration={30}
  flows={[{ label: 'Full midperiod withdrawal', amount: -200, time: 15 }]} />

If the portfolio instead grows to \$300 before a full day-15 withdrawal and still ends at zero:

$$
G=0-100-(-300)=200,\qquad K=100+0.5(-300)=-50.
$$

Mechanically dividing gives $200/(-50)=-4$, or −400%. That sign is not a sensible conventional positive-capital return for this profitable exit. The visual deliberately withholds that percentage and shows the negative weighted capital instead.

<ModifiedDietzTimeline client:visible title="Negative weighted capital reverses the mechanical quotient"
  beginningValue={100} endingValue={0} duration={30}
  flows={[{ label: 'Larger full withdrawal', amount: -300, time: 15 }]} />

Near zero on the positive side, the ratio is unstable rather than suddenly informative. With a full midperiod withdrawal of \$198 and ending value zero, gain is \$98, weighted capital is \$1 and the quotient is 9,800%. A small timing or valuation correction can produce a huge percentage change. That calls for examining the record and reporting scope, not celebrating a precise-looking ratio.

### When to use the stop

Inspect the cash-flow signs, duplicates, actual dates and beginning/end recognition. A zero or negative denominator need not be a data error: the fully specified exit cases above are coherent. Reconsider the active investment period, obtain flow-boundary valuations and choose a measure suited to the question. If the available data cannot support a useful rate, retain the reconciled gain and disclose the missing information. Shortening or splitting a period requires actual values at the new boundaries; do not invent them merely to get a convenient denominator.
