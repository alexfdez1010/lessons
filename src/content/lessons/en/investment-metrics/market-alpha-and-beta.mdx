A fund earns 12% while its market index earns 10%. The manager has beaten the index. Has the fund also beaten a comparison that allows for its market exposure? You need one more piece of information before handing out the trophy.

**Beta** measures sensitivity to market movements. **Alpha** measures performance left unexplained by a chosen model. A positive alpha can prompt an investigation into skill; estimating one does not settle the investigation.

## Three comparisons that answer different questions

Comparing cycling times requires knowing the route. Investment comparisons also change meaning when you change the reference. Unlike a measured cycling route, a financial risk model may omit relevant influences.

For one period, $R_p$ is the portfolio's total return, $R_m$ the market index's total return, and $R_f$ the return on the chosen risk-free reference. **Total return** includes price changes and income such as dividends.

| Comparison | Calculation | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Excess return over cash | $R_p-R_f$ | How much did the fund earn above this cash reference? |
| Active return against the index | $R_p-R_m$ | How much did it beat or trail this index? |
| CAPM-adjusted performance | $R_p-[R_f+\beta(R_m-R_f)]$ | What remains after allowing for market exposure? |

### Work through the first two comparisons

All examples here are invented. Use the same calendar year, currency and dividend-inclusive returns. The fund's return after ongoing fees is 12%, the market returns 10%, and the cash reference returns 2%.

- Excess return: $12\%-2\%=10$ **percentage points**.
- Active return: $12\%-10\%=2$ percentage points.

A percentage point is the difference between percentage rates. Neither subtraction tells you beta, so neither calculates the CAPM-adjusted result yet.

### When to use each reference

Active return compares a fund with an index; excess return compares it with cash. Match dates, currency, income and fees before comparing funds. A net return and a gross return give different treatment to costs. Choose a cash reference in the relevant currency and period: a government bond is not risk-free over every horizon.

## Beta measures sensitivity rather than a promised outcome

Think of beta as a volume dial for the market-related component. Other sounds can still enter the room. **Beta** ($\beta$) is the slope relating portfolio returns to market returns. In a CAPM performance regression, both are measured in excess of the risk-free return for each observation.

### Calculate a change in the fitted component

Hold the intercept and other influences fixed. If market excess return rises by 4 percentage points and beta is 1.5, fitted portfolio excess return rises by:

$$1.5\times4=6.$$

A fall of 4 points changes that component by $1.5\times(-4)=-6$ points. These are changes in a component, not guaranteed changes in the whole fund.

| Beta | Interpretation within the linear model |
|---|---|
| Above one | Greater positive sensitivity to market changes |
| One | The same sensitivity as the selected market |
| Between zero and one | Lower positive sensitivity |
| Zero | No linear sensitivity to this market |
| Below zero | A negative linear relationship |

Zero beta can coexist with substantial risk. A portfolio driven by another source of risk can move sharply without following an equity index. Beta equal to one likewise does not mean every return matches the index.

Horizontal zero means **the market earned the cash return**, not that its total return was zero. With excess returns on both axes, the intercept represents alpha. Using raw returns would change that interpretation when cash earns a nonzero return.

### When to use beta

Use it to describe exposure to a specified market and construct an exposure-adjusted comparison. Record the index, sample dates and observation frequency. Beta can change when holdings or market relationships change; a steeper line is not evidence of better management.

## Calculate a beta-adjusted comparison

<MCQ client:visible pretest

  options={[

    { text: 'Positive active return guarantees positive adjusted performance' },
    { text: 'Greater exposure forces active return to become zero' },
    { text: 'The cash return removes the need to measure exposure' },
  ]}
  explanation="A high-beta fund can beat the index yet fall short of the higher return implied by its exposure. Both comparisons can be calculated from the same returns."
/>

The **capital asset pricing model (CAPM)** relates expected return to market exposure. For a retrospective comparison, insert the period's realised market return into the beta-adjusted reference:

$$R_{\mathrm{ref}}=R_f+\beta(R_m-R_f).$$

Subtracting this reference gives the familiar one-period alpha calculation:

$$\alpha_{\mathrm{obs}}=R_p-[R_f+\beta(R_m-R_f)].$$

This is a model comparison. It is not a guaranteed return, and using the realised market return does not turn the calculation into a forecast made before the year began. CFA Institute presents the [CAPM and Jensen's alpha as performance-comparison tools](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/portfolio-risk-return-part-2).

### Beat the index and still fall short of the reference

Return to the annual example, now with beta supplied. Treat beta as known for this calculation.

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Fund total return after ongoing fees | 12% |
| Market total return | 10% |
| Cash reference return | 2% |
| Fund beta | 1.5 |

1. Market excess return: $10\%-2\%=8$ percentage points.
2. Beta-adjusted component: $1.5\times8\%=12\%$.
3. Add cash: $2\%+12\%=14\%$ reference return.
4. Compare the fund: $12\%-14\%=-2$ percentage points.

The fund has a **positive active return of 2 points** and a **negative CAPM-adjusted result of 2 points**. Both statements are correct because their references differ.

### Matching the market does not settle alpha

Keep market return at 10% and cash at 2%. Another fund returns exactly 10% with beta 0.5:

$$R_{\mathrm{ref}}=2\%+0.5(10\%-2\%)=2\%+4\%=6\%.$$

Its active return is $10\%-10\%=0$; its one-period adjusted result is $10\%-6\%=4$ percentage points. With these inputs, matching the index gives zero adjusted performance only when beta is one.

### When to use the calculation

Check whether a headline comparison changes after allowing for market exposure. Major exposures to other risks can make a single-market model incomplete. The result remains relative to the model you chose.

## Estimated alpha needs evidence and a suitable model

<MCQ client:visible pretest

    { text: 'The manager has a persistent forecasting edge' },
    { text: 'The next year should repeat the same result' },
    { text: 'All relevant sources of risk were removed' },
  ]}
  explanation="The sign describes the observed comparison. Persistence, causation and model completeness require further evidence."
/>

A thermometer reading has measurement uncertainty. An estimated alpha also needs context: a finite sample, a particular index and an imperfect return model.

Analysts fit the relationship across many matching observations:

$$R_{p,t}-R_{f,t}=\alpha+\beta(R_{m,t}-R_{f,t})+\varepsilon_t.$$

The subscript $t$ identifies a period. Alpha is the fitted intercept, beta is the fitted slope, and the **residual** $\varepsilon_t$ is that period's deviation from the line. Ordinary regression with an intercept makes the average fitted residual zero, but an individual residual can have either sign. A one-period adjusted result combines the intercept and that period's residual.

### A month can differ from fitted alpha

Suppose a fitted monthly model has alpha of 0.2 percentage points. One month's residual is −0.7 points. That month's adjusted result is:

$$0.2+(-0.7)=-0.5.$$

A negative month can therefore occur with a positive fitted intercept. The reverse is possible too. Neither observation alone establishes what caused performance.

CFA Institute's *Manager Selection* explains [regression-based alpha and the statistical noise in historical performance](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/sites/default/files/-/media/documents/book/rf-publication/2013/rf-v2013-n4-1-pdf.pdf). A positive estimate can reflect luck, omitted exposures or genuine decisions. A different benchmark or an added risk factor can change it; past statistical significance does not guarantee future returns.

### When to investigate further

Check sample length, uncertainty, net versus gross fees, benchmark suitability and strategy changes. Examine observations outside the period used to fit the model. Sharpe and drawdown still answer useful questions: adjusted performance does not describe every risk an investor experienced.
