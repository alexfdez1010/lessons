The fund reports NAV 50. A buyer offers 40, future calls remain possible, and a credit line has postponed the LP's first payment. Each number answers a different question. Reconcile them before declaring a discount, profit or superior return.

These are independent invented cases in thousands of EUR. Contract rights, consent, collection dates, fee bases and omitted zero costs are stated locally.

## Bridge portfolio equity value to fund net assets

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"Portfolio-company equity already accounts for company debt. What belongs in the fund bridge?"}
options={[{"text": "Separate fund liabilities, without deducting company debt twice", "correct": true}, {"text": "The same company debt subtracted again from every equity mark"}, {"text": "Only portfolio enterprise value with every liability omitted"}]}
explanation={"Keep equity and enterprise value at their proper level."}
/>

A fund's NAV is a dated valuation of assets less its liabilities and relevant allocations. A portfolio company's **equity value** has already accounted for that company's debt. Subtracting the same company debt again at the fund level would double count it.

Stipulate portfolio equity values of 70, fund cash 5, **fund-level** debt 20, accrued fund expenses 3 and estimated accrued carry 2. Then

$$NAV=70+5-20-3-2=50.$$

If the portfolio equity mark falls 20% to $70(0.8)=56$ while the other entries are held fixed in this local stress, NAV becomes $56+5-20-3-2=36$. A 20% change in that asset entry produces $14/50=28\%$ NAV loss. The stress holds the carry deduction fixed solely to isolate the balance effect; a complete valuation must recalculate any contract-dependent carry allocation.

### Attach the mark to its evidence and date

NAV is an estimate, not an instruction that the LP can withdraw 50 cash today. Underlying cash-flow forecasts, valuation comparables and debt assumptions can change; a report arriving today may describe an earlier measurement date. The [December 2025 IPEV valuation guidance](https://www.privateequityvaluation.com/Portals/0/Documents/Guidelines/2025%20IPEV%20Valuation%20Guidelines.pdf) discusses fund-interest NAV adjustments and current information. It is not a universal redemption right or a single mandatory valuation shortcut.

## Reconcile a secondary price and the obligation being transferred

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"A secondary interest trades at 80% of an earlier NAV. What follows automatically?"}
options={[{"text": "Only that price-to-reference ratio, not a guaranteed profit", "correct": true}, {"text": "The buyer must earn 20% risk-free after every future call"}, {"text": "The seller can ignore all contractual consent requirements"}]}
explanation={"Reference date, changed values, fees and future obligations matter."}
/>

A **secondary sale** transfers an existing fund interest to another investor. It is not necessarily a fund redemption. Restrictions, required consent and whether the seller is released from outstanding obligations must be checked separately from the price agreement.

An LP interest has reported NAV 50 and remaining commitment 30. Stipulate that the fund consents and expressly releases the seller, with the buyer assuming the remaining calls. At 80% of the reference NAV, the buyer pays $0.8(50)=40$. A seller transaction fee of 1 leaves cash 39; the buyer has no transaction fee. The buyer's immediate outlay is 40 and potential further calls are up to 30 under the agreement. Their nominal sum 70 is neither an automatic loss nor a fair-value formula: future calls may fund assets and future distributions remain uncertain.

Now the agreement includes intervening cash flows between the report and transfer: a call of 2 invested at cost and a distribution of 5. Assume no valuation changes or other costs. The mechanically adjusted reference is $50+2-5=47$ and remaining commitment $30-2=28$. Price is $0.8(47)=37.6$, seller cash after the fee is 36.6, and adding the seller's interim net cash $5-2=3$ gives 39.6 from report date through sale.

### Match both the reference date and the transferred rights

The mechanical adjustment alone is not a complete fair-value estimate. Current portfolio information, carry terms, debt and evidence from an orderly transaction may also matter. A sale price below an earlier NAV does not guarantee a buyer profit, and agreed buyer assumption does not by itself release a seller unless the relevant contract permits it. [IPEV's fund-interest discussion](https://www.privateequityvaluation.com/Portals/0/Documents/Guidelines/2025%20IPEV%20Valuation%20Guidelines.pdf) treats secondary evidence with judgment, not a universal fixed discount.

## Separate subscription-line funding from the LP return clock

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"Borrowing postpones LP contribution but adds interest. Can a higher LP IRR coexist with lower fund-related profit?"}
options={[{"text": "Yes; its cash-flow clock and costs have both changed", "correct": true}, {"text": "No; a higher IRR always implies higher terminal wealth"}, {"text": "Only if the asset’s exit date must also move earlier"}]}
explanation={"The case has 33.333333% versus 18.321596%, but profit 10.5 versus 12."}
/>

A **subscription credit line** is fund borrowing supported by contractual capital-call rights or commitments under stated lending terms. It can bridge an investment before LP cash is drawn. It does not make the fund investment costless or postpone the asset's economic exposure until the LP contributes.

The fund buys an asset for 30 at year 0 and sells it for 42 at year 2, with no other expenses, carry or tax. Without a line, the LP pays 30 at year 0 and receives 42 at year 2:

$$IRR_{\mathrm{no\ line}}=\sqrt{42/30}-1\approx18.321596\%,\qquad \Pi=42-30=12.$$

With a line, the fund borrows 30 at year 0 at an invented 5% rate for one year. Interest is 1.5; the LP is called for 31.5 at year 1 to repay principal and interest. At year 2 the LP receives 42:

$$IRR_{\mathrm{line}}=42/31.5-1=33.333333\%,\qquad \Pi=42-31.5=10.5.$$

The LP's cash-flow IRR is higher while the fund-related cash profit is lower. The asset's dates and exit cash did not improve. At 20% line interest, the year-1 call is 36 and LP IRR is $42/36-1=16.666667\%$, below the unborrowed rate. Borrowing does not universally raise the reported IRR.

### Report both financing and the money it leaves outside

The example excludes returns on the LP's retained cash outside the fund. A full wealth comparison must include them and the commitment exposure from year 0. The LPA's preferred-return clock also need not begin on the same date used in an LP cash-flow return. Line interest, fees, collateral, call rights, tenor and repayment source belong in the analysis. [ILPA's principles](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/ILPA-Principles-3.0_2019.pdf) discuss transparent subscription-line reporting; their suggested terms are not legal limits applied here.
