A private-fund return arrives through a sequence of calls and distributions. An attractive final total cannot pay an earlier call unless cash is available at that earlier date.

Amounts are thousands of EUR. The principal reporting example has an initial fee of 5 included in the first call, no other fund expenses, no carry and no in-kind distributions. Its hypothetical path is not a forecast of when actual proceeds will arrive.

Cumulative contributions are $30+20+15=65$; cumulative distributions are $10+40+25=75$. Remaining commitment is $100-65=35$, not $100-65+75$. The final residual NAV estimate of 20 is separate from the 75 already distributed.

If the LP begins with bank cash 70, earns zero on it and makes no other withdrawals, its final bank balance is $70-65+75=80$. Bank 80 plus fund NAV 20 gives estimated total value 100, compared with initial cash 70: gain 30 under the stated mark. The portfolio initially received 25 after the fee and another 20+15 later: cost 60. Distributions 75 plus residual 20 make 95; that 35 portfolio gain less the 5 fund expense reconciles the LP's 30 marked gain.

### Keep gross and net views identifiable

Use actual settlement dates and signed amounts, not merely call-notice dates or annual net totals when timing matters. A return of unused capital, an in-kind asset or a tax withholding needs its own classification. The [ILPA performance guidance](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/ILPA-Performance-Template-Suggested-Guidance-Gross-Up-Methodology-v1.1.pdf) asks for clear cash-date and transaction conventions. A distribution may contain returned capital and profit; it is not automatically profit in full.

## Restore callable capacity only when the contract says so

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"The LP receives 10 after paying 50 of a 100 commitment. Is capacity automatically 60?"}
options={[{"text": "Only if an eligible unexpired clause restores those 10", "correct": true}, {"text": "Always; every distribution resets commitment"}, {"text": "Never; no contract can permit any recall"}]}
explanation={"The illustration compares two explicit alternative clauses."}
/>

**Recycling** lets specified proceeds support new investment or expenses under the agreement. A **recallable distribution** has been paid to the LP but may validly be called back under a defined clause. A permanent distribution need not have that feature. Neither label is determined by the mere fact that cash arrived.

Take a separate 100 commitment, 50 already contributed and a distribution of 10. If that distribution is permanent and does not replenish the commitment, remaining callable capacity stays $100-50=50$.

An alternative clause expressly allows these exact 10 of returned capital to be recalled before a stated unexpired deadline, with no other recycling. Its counter is $100-50+10=60$. Historical contributions remain 50 and distributions remain 10: the extra capacity is a future obligation, not a reversal of cash already received. If all 60 is later called, gross lifetime contributions reach 110; subtract the 10 previously returned and the net funding is 100. The contract, not the arithmetic alone, permits that gross amount above the original figure.

### Track cash and the commitment counter independently

Read caps, eligible uses, expiry and the treatment of expenses or unused calls. Recycling retained *inside* the fund differs from cash first paid to the LP and later recalled. Netting both silently can hide liquidity demands and alter performance conventions. [ILPA's principles](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/ILPA-Principles-3.0_2019.pdf) discuss explicit recycling terms; they are advisory guidance, not a clause automatically present in every LPA.

## Test the next call before counting future proceeds

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"A 30 call precedes a possible 40 distribution; current bank 15. What is the cash-only result?"}
options={[{"text": "Funding gap 15 before any assumed payment", "correct": true}, {"text": "Fully funded because 40 exceeds 30 eventually"}, {"text": "An automatic loan 15 already approved by the LPA"}]}
explanation={"Later receipts do not automatically pay an earlier deadline."}
/>

Return to the dated reporting case. Just after year 3, the LP has paid 65 and received only 10. From initial bank 70, cash available is $70-65+10=15$. Remaining callable commitment is 35.

Suppose a valid new call for 30 falls due before any year-4 distribution. Its amount fits the remaining commitment, but the cash-only funding plan has a gap of $30-15=15$. The possible later 40 distribution does not fund the earlier deadline. The panel diagnoses this gap; it does not assume that the LP can borrow or that a payment was executed despite insufficient cash.

<PrivateFundLab client:visible mode="liquidity" title="Check the next call against cash available before it" />

A call of 10 would be fully fundable from this bank account, leaving 5 after payment. A call of 35 would require 20 more than the account currently holds. Other liquid assets, new income or a credit facility could change the plan, but their dates, availability and costs must be modelled explicitly. A commitment to a fund cannot itself be spent as cash to meet the call.

### Stress calls and distributions jointly

Use a dated liquidity plan rather than a single assumed “net investment.” Calls can coincide across several funds while distributions are delayed; historical average pacing does not guarantee the next year's schedule. A sale of the fund interest can also require consent, take time and yield a price different from NAV. Avoid turning an illustrative cash buffer into a universal allocation rule.
