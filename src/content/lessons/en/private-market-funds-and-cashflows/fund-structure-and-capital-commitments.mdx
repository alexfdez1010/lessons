You promise a private fund 100, receive a call for 30, and later see a reported value of 25. Those figures can all be correct. They describe an obligation, a cash payment and an asset value—three different entries.

This course models **closed-end private-market funds**: money is committed under a contract, drawn over time, invested and eventually distributed. It does not treat every private investment vehicle as identical. All agreements, dates, prices and rates are invented. Currency amounts are **thousands of EUR**, unless a ratio or percentage is named. Omitted taxes, costs and cash interest are zero where stipulated; individual examples do not inherit another example's contract.

## Locate the fund between the investor and the assets

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"An LP has 10% of the capital rights in the stated fund snapshot. What does that establish?"}
options={[{"text": "A contractual fund interest, not on-demand control of particular assets", "correct": true}, {"text": "A direct withdrawal right over every portfolio company’s shares"}, {"text": "A mandatory carry rate equal to that capital percentage"}]}
explanation={"Fund ownership, investor rights and profit-sharing terms are different levels."}
/>

A **fund** pools investor capital and holds portfolio investments. In our limited-partnership model, **LPs**, limited partners, supply capital under agreed rights; the **GP**, general partner, has the contractual management role. A separate **management company** may perform investment management and receive fees. A GP or other designated carry recipient can receive a profit share, **carried interest** or **carry**. These functions need not belong to one legal entity.

The **limited partnership agreement**, or **LPA**, and related subscription documents define contributions, authority, fees, distributions, transfers and other rights. Side agreements may alter particular rights. Actual legal wrappers and liability rules depend on jurisdiction and documents; “LP” is not a universal guarantee that no other obligation can arise.

Consider a fully funded snapshot with total capital paid of 1,000: GP 20, our LP 100 and other LPs 880. Assume equal capital rights, no carry accrual and contributions in those same proportions. Our capital weight is $100/1000=10\%$. If current fund assets are 700 and fund liabilities 100, **net asset value**, NAV, is $700-100=600$. The attributed capital-account value is $0.10(600)=60$.

<PrivateFundLab client:visible mode="ownership" title="Separate investor capital rights from fund assets" />

The LP holds an interest in the fund; the fund holds its assets. A 10% capital weight does not let the LP withdraw a particular portfolio company's shares or 10% of the fund's bank account on demand. The GP's 20 capital contribution is 2% of total paid capital; that is separate from a possible carry percentage on profits. Later fee and distribution terms can make net cash allocations differ from simple capital weights.

### Use the legal and economic levels together

First identify whose asset, debt or fee is being measured. The [SEC description of private funds](https://www.sec.gov/about/starting-private-fund) explains capital calls and contractual terms in a US context; it does not establish one legal form worldwide. Our course isolates a specified closed-end structure rather than assuming the withdrawal rights of a daily-dealing public fund.

## Separate commitment from contributed cash

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"Immediately after signing a 100 commitment but before any contribution, how much LP cash has the fund received?"}
options={[{"text": "Zero under the stated no-upfront-payment case", "correct": true}, {"text": "100 because every commitment is bank cash"}, {"text": "The future NAV regardless of settlement dates"}]}
explanation={"The commitment is an obligation; no transfer has yet occurred."}
/>

A **capital commitment** is a contractual promise to provide eligible capital when validly called. A **capital call** requests funding under those terms. A funded **contribution** is cash actually paid. Signing a promise is not the same as transferring the promised amount to the fund today.

In a fresh case, the LP commits 100 and initially keeps 100 in its bank account. No call has been paid, so the fund has received zero from this LP. A valid call for 30 is then fully funded. LP bank cash falls to 70 and fund cash rises by 30. Before the fund invests or spends it, the two cash changes reconcile: $-30+30=0$.

Assume all 30 consumes commitment and there are no arrears, recallable distributions or outside-commitment charges. Remaining callable capacity is $100-30=70$. The 70 is an obligation that may be drawn later; it is not cash already held by the fund and not the investor's fund NAV. A call notice and its settlement date should be distinguished if payment is pending.

### Read what can be called and when

Use the LPA and notices to identify eligible uses, deadlines, limits and the investment period. The fund's investment period and its eventual realisation period need not end on the same day; extensions and follow-on investments are contract matters. Do not assume a five-year example guarantees liquidity after five years. A missed call can have contractual consequences, but there is no single universal default penalty to insert into every model.

## Explain why paid capital may exceed invested value

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"Paid capital 30 becomes asset cost 25 after expense 5. Must NAV initially equal 30?"}
options={[{"text": "No; the expense can leave initial net assets 25", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; a contribution is always all portfolio investment"}, {"text": "Only if the management fee consumes no commitment"}]}
explanation={"Use of cash and value of remaining assets must reconcile."}
/>

A capital call can fund investments **and expenses**. Follow the use of proceeds before labelling the whole call “capital invested in companies.”

The LP pays 30. The fund deploys 25 into portfolio assets initially worth their cost, and pays a 5 management or formation expense. Assume no other assets, debts or liabilities. Cash use reconciles: $30=25+5$. Initial net assets are 25, not 30. If the LP started with cash 100, its remaining bank 70 plus fund interest 25 gives value 95; the 5 expense has gone to another party.

Now compare two explicit clauses. In A, investments and this fee both sit **inside commitment**, so all 30 consumes the 100 and 70 remains callable. In B, the 100 commitment covers investments, while this 5 fee is **outside commitment**: only 25 consumes the investment commitment, leaving 75. Cash already paid is still 30 and initial NAV still 25. If all 75 remaining investment capacity were later drawn, lifetime cash paid would reach 105 including this fee, before any other separately agreed amounts.

“Outside commitment” is not the same as “outside the fund's legal entity.” A fee may pass through the fund but not reduce the commitment counter. The [ILPA capital-call reporting guidance](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/ILPA-Suggested-Guidance-2025-Final.pdf) explicitly separates those classifications.

### Reconcile obligation, payment and valuation separately

Keep the total LP cash payment for net performance, even if only part consumes commitment. Do not count an expense twice by subtracting it again from NAV already reported after that expense. Nor does early NAV below contributions guarantee a later recovery: an initial fee drag can occur without any subsequent investment success.
