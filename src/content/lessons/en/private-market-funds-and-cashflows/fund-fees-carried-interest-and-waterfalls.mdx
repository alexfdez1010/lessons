“Two percent and twenty” leaves out most of the calculation. Two percent of what, which expenses are already paid, and twenty percent of which profits after which priority? This lesson writes the brackets and recipients into each invented contract.

Amounts are thousands of EUR. Cases are independent; their fee and carry clauses do not combine automatically. A **waterfall** here allocates investment proceeds between capital providers and a carry recipient. It is different from a credit tranche's priority over losses.

## Specify the management-fee base and the offset recipient

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"A fee quote is 2% annually. What is needed to calculate its cash amount?"}
options={[{"text": "The contractual base, period and applicable offsets", "correct": true}, {"text": "Only the carry percentage on a different profit pool"}, {"text": "Only the current LP bank balance outside the fund"}]}
explanation={"A rate without its base is not a complete fee account."}
/>

A **management fee** pays for the defined management service. Its quoted rate needs a base, time period, accrual rule and recipient. For one year, a 2% rate on commitment 100 gives $0.02(100)=2$. The same rate on invested cost 60 gives 1.2. Those are two alternative clauses in this example, not a claim that every fund automatically changes basis after an identical date.

Now stipulate that a wholly owned portfolio company separately pays the management company a transaction fee of 1. The LPA offsets 80% of that receipt against the fund management fee. With commitment as the base, offset $0.8(1)=0.8$ reduces the fund fee from 2 to 1.2. Manager cash receipts are company 1 plus fund 1.2, or 2.2. Without the offset they would total 3. With the alternate 60 base they total $1+(1.2-0.8)=1.4$.

<PrivateFundLab client:visible mode="fees" title="Change the fee basis and offset before summing receipts" />

The company payment already reduces that company's cash and equity value by 1 in this case. If portfolio value or proceeds are already measured after it, subtracting that same 1 again from fund performance double counts the cost. Conversely, counting only the fund's 1.2 payment omits the company-level charge when asking the LP's combined economic burden under full ownership.

### Follow all entities before comparing a headline rate

State offsets, expense allocation and whether figures are gross or net. Actual agreements may use other bases, waivers, side letters and timing rules. The [Investor.gov private-equity discussion](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/private-investment-funds/private-equity) identifies fees and conflicts; it does not supply one standard rate or allocation formula.

## Allocate a whole-fund profit with explicit preferred and catch-up terms

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"Available proceeds are below contributed capital. Does an 8% preference create a minimum top-up?"}
options={[{"text": "No; this contract allocates only the available cash", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; the GP must always supply the preference from wealth"}, {"text": "Only if the carry rate is labelled twenty percent"}]}
explanation={"A priority is not an investment-return guarantee."}
/>

**Carried interest**, carry, is a contractual profit share, not the GP's invested capital. A **preferred return** gives LP distributions priority under a specified rule; it does not create investment cash that the assets failed to earn. A **catch-up** can then direct cash to the GP until a target sharing ratio is reached.

LPs contribute 100 at year 0. In this independent case there is no GP co-investment. At year 1 the fund has 130 cash **after all other fund expenses**. Contract A returns contributed capital first, then up to 8 to the LP as its one-year 8% preference, then a full GP catch-up, and finally splits remaining cash 80% LP/20% GP.

1. Return 100 capital to LP; 30 remains.
2. Pay 8 preference to LP; 22 remains.
3. Pay GP catch-up 2, since $2/(8+2)=20\%$; 20 remains.
4. Split the final 20: LP 16 and GP 4.

Total LP payment is $100+8+16=124$ and GP carry $2+4=6$, reconciling to 130. GP carry is 20% of the 30 profit after this full catch-up.

<PrivateFundLab client:visible mode="waterfall" title="Allocate available cash through the stated waterfall" />

Contract B has the same quoted 8% preference and 20% carry but **no catch-up**, a hard hurdle in this example. GP gets only $0.2(130-100-8)=4.4$; LP gets 125.6. With only 109 available, A pays capital 100, preference 8 and partial catch-up 1; it cannot pay the full 2. With only 80, all 80 goes toward capital and no preference or carry is paid. There is no promised top-up.

### Model the exact allocation stages

Use the LPA's return-of-capital base, dates, expense and tax treatment, hurdle, catch-up and residual split. The [ILPA principles](https://ilpa.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/ILPA-Principles-3.0_2019.pdf) emphasise understandable calculations; their preferred terms are not universal law. Avoid using geographic nicknames as a substitute for the formula.

## Distinguish early deal carry from the final clawback collection

<MCQ client: visible pretest
question={"Early deal carry exceeds final whole-fund entitlement. What must be separated?"}
options={[{"text": "The clawback obligation and the amount actually collected", "correct": true}, {"text": "Only the original carry rate and manager’s press release"}, {"text": "The fact that unpaid amounts disappear without recourse"}]}
explanation={"A contractual receivable need not have become cash."}
/>

A **deal-by-deal** rule can pay carry on an early profitable realisation before later investments finish. A **clawback** can require excess carry to be returned under the final fund-level test. Its contractual amount and its collected cash are different quantities.

Two investments each cost 50. Ignore fees, tax, preferred return and escrow in this simplified case. The first exits for 80 in year 1: return its 50 cost and split profit 30 at 80/20. LP receives $50+24=74$ and GP 6. The second exits for 20 in year 2 and pays 20 to LP, with no new carry. Across both deals the fund received $80+20=100$, exactly its initial cost, but LP cash is only 94 and GP holds 6.

The LPA stipulates final carry of 20% of aggregate fund profit, now zero, so GP owes a 6 clawback. Full collection gives LP $94+6=100$ and leaves GP zero carry cash. Collecting half gives LP 97 and leaves GP holding 3 cash while still owing the uncollected 3 under this contract.

<PrivateFundLab client:visible mode="clawback" title="Compare clawback owed with cash actually collected" />

### Separate the obligation from its credit value

The 97 is cash received at that date, not proof that the remaining claim has no value. If it is later paid, the permanent cash shortfall disappears before considering delay costs. If a separate stress assumes it irrecoverable, that is an additional credit loss. Escrow, guarantees, tax-netting, timing and recourse can change actual protection; no clawback clause is an automatic cash guarantee.
