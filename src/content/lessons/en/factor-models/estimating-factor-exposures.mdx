So far you've met the *cast* of factors — market, size, value, momentum, profitability, investment — and the idea that a factor is a portfolio whose return is a reward for bearing some shared risk. But naming factors is the easy part. The hard, money-deciding part is **measurement**: given an actual fund's monthly returns, *how much* of each factor does it carry? Is its leftover return real skill or just statistical noise? And do these factors even earn a premium, or did someone backtest themselves into a mirage?

That's all econometrics, and it splits cleanly into **two regressions that ask two different questions**. A **time-series regression** looks at *one asset over time* and asks "what are *this* asset's exposures, and is its alpha real?" A **cross-sectional regression** looks at *many assets at one moment* and asks "across the whole market, does loading on this factor actually pay?" Confuse them and nothing in this field makes sense; keep them straight and the famous **Fama–MacBeth** procedure falls out naturally. Let's measure.

## Time-series regression: one asset's exposures

**Analogy.** Picture a single sailboat over a season. Some days it's pushed by the wind (the market), some days by the tide (the value factor), some days by a current (size). A time-series regression watches *that one boat* across many days and asks: how strongly does it respond to each force, and is there any leftover motion the known forces can't explain?

**Definition.** For a single asset $i$, you regress its **excess return** (return minus the risk-free rate, $R_i - R_f$) on the contemporaneous factor returns:

$$R_i - R_f = \alpha_i + \sum_k \beta_{i,k} F_k + \varepsilon_i.$$

Each $\beta_{i,k}$ is a **loading** (also called a factor exposure): the slope on factor $k$, telling you how many units of that factor the asset carries. The intercept $\alpha_i$ is the **model alpha** — the average return left over *after* every factor has been paid its due. The residual $\varepsilon_i$ is the wiggle no factor explains.

**Reading the picture.** With a single factor this is just a scatter of the asset's excess return (vertical) against the factor's return (horizontal), with a fitted line through it. The **slope is the loading** and the **intercept is alpha** — exactly the geometry below. Toggle the presets to watch a defensive (low-loading) asset's line lie flat, a market-tracking asset sit at slope one, and an aggressive asset swing hard; flip the alpha control to shift the line vertically. This sets a model intercept; it does not estimate or establish skill.

**What R² measures here.** Alongside the loadings and alpha, the regression spits out an $R^2$ — the **fraction of the asset's return variance the factors explain**. A broad, diversified equity portfolio is almost nothing *but* factor exposure, so its $R^2$ on a good multi-factor model is routinely **0.90 or higher**. A single stock, dragged around by company-specific news, has far more idiosyncratic wiggle, so its $R^2$ is much lower — often 0.3 to 0.5. Diversification literally *raises* $R^2$ by averaging away the idiosyncratic part.

**How uncertain is alpha?** A **t-statistic** divides the estimated intercept by its standard error (SE), a model-based measure of sampling uncertainty. A rule such as $|t| \gtrsim 2$ is only a rough reference under the chosen assumptions, sample and testing procedure; significance does not establish investment skill. Return residuals can be **heteroskedastic** (their conditional variance changes) and **autocorrelated** (errors at different dates are related). Conventional OLS errors can then overstate or understate uncertainty.

**Newey–West** is a heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (**HAC**) covariance estimator. It changes estimated standard errors, not the OLS coefficients or R². Its SE can be **larger or smaller** than a conventional SE: there is no universal ordering. The calculation requires an appropriate time sequence, lag/weight choices and conditions for its large-sample justification. It cannot repair a misspecified conditional mean, omitted-factor bias or coefficients that changed during the window. See the [official statsmodels HAC API](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.stats.sandwich_covariance.cov_hac.html) and [Stata Newey–West manual](https://www.stata.com/manuals/tsnewey.pdf).
<Callout variant="info" title="Loadings and alpha, in one breath">
A time-series regression of one asset's excess return on the factors gives you, per asset: the **loadings** (slopes = how much of each factor it carries), the **alpha** (intercept = average return no factor explains), and **R²** (how much of its variance the factors account for). Loadings and alpha are *return* quantities; R² is a *variance* quantity. Keep that distinction — the next section is built on it.
</Callout>

## What R² does and doesn't tell you

With an intercept, ordinary least squares (**OLS**) makes residuals average zero. Alpha is the **intercept of the fitted conditional mean**; it is not the residual mean. For a sample with nonzero return variation,

$$R^2=1-\frac{\sum_t \hat\varepsilon_t^2}{\sum_t(y_t-\bar y)^2}.$$

This compares **variation** around a fitted line with variation around the sample mean. It does not put an upper bound on the intercept. As [NIST's model-validation guidance](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmd/section4/pmd44.htm) explains, R² alone is insufficient to validate a model.

**Keep the earlier examples, change the inference.** A fund that closely tracks the value factor can have R² near **0.99** and alpha zero. That describes one possible combination; high R² alone does not establish that it is a closet index fund or has no skill. A different strategy could have R² **0.40** and a positive, statistically significant alpha. Low R² does not establish skill either: omitted exposures, costs, data selection and an unsuitable error model still matter.

For A, the means are zero, the slope is $4/4=1$ and alpha is zero. Residuals are −0.1, −0.1, +0.1, +0.1 pp, so their squared sum is **0.04 pp²**. Total variation is $1.21+0.81+0.81+1.21=4.04$ pp². Thus $R^2=1-0.04/4.04=100/101\approx0.9901$. For B, alpha becomes **2 pp/month** and the slope remains one. Centered returns, residuals, SSE and R² are all unchanged. More generally, shifting every y by a constant shifts alpha by that constant while leaving R² fixed.

### When to use it

Use R² to describe sample fit and residual variation for the specified factor model. Evaluate alpha separately with suitable uncertainty estimates, costs and model checks. Neither a high nor a low R² proves the manager can repeat an excess return.

## Cross-sectional regression: are loadings priced?

The time-series regression answered "what does *this one* asset carry, and is *its* alpha real?" It cannot answer a different, equally vital question: **across the whole universe of assets, does loading more heavily on a factor actually earn you more return?** A factor only deserves the name if its premium is positive — if the assets that load on it are *paid* for it. That's a statement about the **cross-section**, so it needs a cross-sectional regression.

**Analogy.** Forget the single boat over a season. Line up *every* boat in the marina on one afternoon and plot, for each, its sail area against its average speed. If bigger sails systematically go faster, "sail area" is priced; the *slope* of that cloud tells you how many extra knots one extra unit of sail buys. That slope is a **premium**.

**Definition.** Using the loadings $\hat\beta_{i,k}$ already estimated from the first-pass time-series regressions, you regress assets' **average excess returns** on those loadings — *across assets*:

$$\bar R_i = \lambda_0 + \sum_k \lambda_k \hat\beta_{i,k} + u_i.$$

Now the unit of observation is an **asset** (or a test portfolio), not a date. The slopes $\lambda_k$ estimate the **factor risk premia** — the extra average return earned per unit of loading on factor $k$. The intercept $\lambda_0$ is the **pricing error**: the average return common to all assets that *isn't* explained by any loading (it should be near zero if the model prices the cross-section well).

This is a genuinely different regression from the time-series one, even though both involve "betas." Time-series: *one asset, many dates, asks for exposure and alpha.* Cross-section: *many assets, the loadings as the X variable, asks whether those loadings are rewarded.*

**Reading the picture.** Each dot below is one test portfolio: its factor loading runs across, its average realised excess return runs up. The best-fit line's **slope is the estimated premium** (how much average return one extra unit of loading earns) and its **intercept is the pricing error**. A priced factor tilts the line clearly upward — high-loading portfolios genuinely earn more.

## Fama–MacBeth in two passes

In 1973 Eugene Fama and James MacBeth turned the two regressions into a clean, two-pass recipe that's still the workhorse for estimating factor premia and their significance. The trick solves a thorny statistical problem — assets' returns are correlated *with each other* at any moment, which wrecks naive cross-sectional standard errors — and it does so almost elegantly.

**Pass 1 — time series (estimate the loadings).** For each asset, run the time-series regression from the first section to estimate its loadings $\hat\beta_{i,k}$. After this pass, every asset has a fixed set of factor exposures.

**Pass 2 — cross section, *one regression per period*.** Here's the clever bit. For **each month** $t$ separately, run a cross-sectional regression of *that month's* returns across assets on the loadings:

$$R_{i,t} = \lambda_{0,t} + \sum_k \lambda_{k,t}\,\hat\beta_{i,k} + u_{i,t}.$$

That gives you not one premium estimate but a whole **time series** of monthly premium estimates $\hat\lambda_{k,t}$ — one number per factor per month.

**Combine.** The estimated premium is simply the **time-series average** of those monthly slopes,

$$\bar\lambda_k = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \hat\lambda_{k,t},$$

and — this is the elegant part — its **t-statistic comes from the standard error of that time series of monthly estimates**. By treating each month's cross-sectional slope as one observation and averaging over months, cross-sectional correlation *within* a month is automatically absorbed: you never have to model it, because you only ever average *across* independent-ish months.

**Worked example — testing a value (HML) premium.** Suppose Pass 2 gives just four monthly HML slopes (a real study uses hundreds; four keeps the arithmetic visible):

| Month | Monthly HML slope $\hat\lambda_t$ | Deviation from mean | Squared deviation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +0.4% | +0.2% | 0.04 |
| 2 | −0.2% | −0.4% | 0.16 |
| 3 | +0.6% | +0.4% | 0.16 |
| 4 | 0.0% | −0.2% | 0.04 |
| **Avg** | **+0.2%** | — | **sum = 0.40** |

Walk the arithmetic (working in units of percent):

- **Mean premium:** $\bar\lambda = (0.4 - 0.2 + 0.6 + 0.0)/4 = 0.8/4 = 0.2\%$ per month.
- **Sample variance:** divide the sum of squared deviations by $T - 1$: $0.40 / (4 - 1) = 0.1333$.
- **Standard deviation:** $\sqrt{0.1333} = 0.365\%$ per month.
- **Standard error of the mean:** $\mathrm{SE} = 0.365 / \sqrt{4} = 0.365 / 2 = 0.183\%$.
- **t-statistic:** $t = \bar\lambda / \mathrm{SE} = 0.2 / 0.183 \approx 1.1$.

A t-stat of about **1.1 is well below the rough bar of 2**, so on this (deliberately tiny) sample the value premium is **not statistically significant** — the +0.2%/month average is swamped by month-to-month noise. The premium *might* be real, but four months can't prove it. Run the same procedure over 600 months and the $\sqrt T$ in the denominator shrinks the standard error dramatically, which is exactly why the canonical Fama–French studies use decades of data.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why divide by √T">
The standard error of an average is the data's standard deviation divided by $\sqrt{T}$. That $\sqrt{T}$ is why **more months are the cheapest way to buy significance**: a real premium with steady month-to-month noise will eventually clear the t = 2 bar simply because the denominator keeps shrinking. A premium that *can't* clear the bar even with hundreds of months is genuinely indistinguishable from noise.
</Callout>

## Reading the t-stats honestly

The whole machine exists to separate **signal from noise**, and the t-statistic is the verdict. A factor premium isn't "real" because its average is positive — it's credible only when that average is **large relative to its month-to-month wobble**. A premium of +0.2%/month sounds like a clean win until you notice the months range from −0.2% to +0.6%; that spread is the noise the t-stat measures against.

**Why short windows mislead.** With a small $T$, the $\sqrt{T}$ in the standard error is small, so the error bars are wide and even a genuine premium can fail the t = 2 bar (as our four-month example did). The reverse trap is just as dangerous: data-mine enough candidate factors over a short window and *some* will clear t = 2 by pure luck. This is why the field now demands **higher hurdles** (some argue t &gt; 3) for *newly discovered* factors — you're implicitly testing hundreds of them, so the bar for "not luck" must rise.

**The distinction to keep.** High R² does not determine alpha or investment quality. A significant model alpha is evidence to investigate under the stated model and test, rather than proof of skill. HAC changes the uncertainty calculation under its assumptions; it can increase or reduce the SE and cannot make an unstable coefficient relationship constant.

## Putting it together

Estimating factor exposures is two regressions doing two jobs. The **time-series regression** ($R_i - R_f = \alpha_i + \sum_k \beta_{i,k} F_k + \varepsilon_i$) takes one asset over many dates and returns its **loadings** (slopes), its **alpha** (intercept — return no factor explains), and its **$R^2$** (variance explained, *not* average return — diversified portfolios sit at 0.90+, single stocks far lower). R² does not determine alpha: adding a constant to all returns shifts the intercept and leaves R² unchanged. The **cross-sectional regression** ($\bar R_i = \lambda_0 + \sum_k \lambda_k \hat\beta_{i,k} + u_i$) takes many assets at a moment, uses the loadings as inputs, and returns the **premia** (slopes) and the **pricing error** (intercept) — answering whether loading on a factor is actually *rewarded*. **Fama–MacBeth** chains them: estimate loadings (Pass 1), run a cross-sectional regression every month to get a time series of premium estimates (Pass 2), then take their average and judge it by the t-stat from that series' standard error. Our four-month value example gave a +0.2%/month premium but only $t \approx 1.1$ — not significant, because the $\sqrt{T}$ in the standard error punishes short samples. Assess the intercept and its uncertainty separately from R². When appropriate, HAC changes the estimated covariance under its conditions; it can raise or lower SE and cannot fix a changing relationship.
