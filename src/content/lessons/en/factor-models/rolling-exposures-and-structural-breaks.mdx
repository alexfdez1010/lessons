A fund's estimated market beta moves from 0.6 to 1.2. Has the manager doubled the exposure, or has a short sample changed the estimate? A moving line cannot answer that question on its own. We need to know which observations entered it, how much information they contain, and what evidence supports a change in the underlying relationship.

The course has already introduced regression loadings and model-dependent alpha. Here we will let the **sample move through time**. The laboratory uses invented monthly observations with an explicitly known generating beta. That lets us check the calculation against a teaching truth that a live fund does not hand us. Neither the disturbance patterns nor the change dates are empirical market estimates.

## Put every exposure estimate on a dated sample

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An estimate is fitted using fund and factor returns through month 6, both released after month 6 ends. Can it be treated as information available before month 6's return occurred?" options={[
 {text:'No; the fit uses month 6 outcomes and becomes available only when all required inputs are known',correct:true},

]} explanation="An observation date, publication date and decision date can differ. A fitted loading uses the returns inside its sample; a trading or evaluation rule must respect when those data became available." />

A **factor exposure**, or loading, is a slope in a specified return model. Write one asset's monthly excess return y, its return minus the matched risk-free return, as

$$y_t=\alpha+\beta f_t+\varepsilon_t.$$

Here f is the factor's return, β is dimensionless exposure, α is the intercept per month, and ε is the unexplained return. For this lesson, y, f, α and ε are measured in **percentage points per month**: a factor return of −1 means −1%, not a fractional return of −100%. With the same units on both return axes, beta's slope is unchanged.

A **rolling window** of W observations ending at t uses dates $t-W+1,\ldots,t$. When t advances, the oldest observation leaves and the new one enters. An **expanding sample** here uses all dates $1,\ldots,t$. Both are backward-looking fits at their stated endpoint; expanding does not mean future observations have become available. The [official rolling-regression documentation](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/examples/notebooks/generated/rolling_ls.html) aligns a window's coefficients with its last observation, not its first.

For an explicit-intercept ordinary least-squares fit, define the sample means $\bar f,\bar y$ and calculate

$$
S_{ff}=\sum(f_i-\bar f)^2,\qquad
S_{fy}=\sum(f_i-\bar f)(y_i-\bar y),\qquad
\hat\beta=\frac{S_{fy}}{S_{ff}},\qquad
\hat\alpha=\bar y-\hat\beta\bar f.
$$

At the close of month 6, the four-observation rolling fit uses months 3–6. Both means are zero, $S_{ff}=1+1+1+1=4$ and $S_{fy}=1+1+2+2=6$, so $\hat\beta=6/4=1.5$ and $\hat\alpha=0$. Expanding through all six uses $S_{ff}=6$, $S_{fy}=8$, giving $\hat\beta=8/6=4/3$. The current constructed beta is 2; neither fit equals it yet.

**Pitfall:** calling a coefficient “June beta” without saying whether it uses June's already realized return or was available before June. If the fund's May return is released on 8 June and a required factor on 3 June, a May-end fit requiring both is unavailable until at least 8 June. Restated data can create another timing mismatch.

### When to record the sample explicitly

Whenever comparing exposures, setting a hedge or evaluating a risk model. State endpoint, window length, frequency, currency, excess-return and fee treatment, publication lags and missing-data policy. A window of 12 calendar months is not necessarily 12 valid paired observations. [RollingOLS's API](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.regression.rolling.RollingOLS.html) explicitly distinguishes dropping, skipping and rejecting missing data; our laboratory uses complete paired observations and requires the full rolling window.

## Watch adaptation and the cost of carrying old observations

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="In the compact zero-noise example, current true beta is 2 after month 5. Why is the rolling estimate only 1.5 at month 6?" options={[

A window is a moving bucket of evidence. Shortening it ejects old observations sooner; lengthening it keeps more observations that may help estimate a stable relationship but may also describe an obsolete one. The relevant trade-off is **sampling variability versus sensitivity to change**, rather than a rule that the largest sample always wins.

The month 5 rolling sample has factors +1,−1,+1,−1 and returns +1,−1,+1,−2. Its factor mean is zero and return mean −0.25. Thus $S_{fy}=1+1+1+2=5$, $S_{ff}=4$, $\hat\beta=1.25$ and $\hat\alpha=-0.25$ pp. **Mixing two zero-alpha relationships produced a nonzero estimated intercept.** It has not created evidence of manager skill.

At month 8, the rolling sample contains only the new relationship, so its beta reaches 2 in this noiseless construction. Expanding still contains four old and four new observations: $\hat\beta=(4\times1+4\times2)/8=1.5$. It retains information and retains obsolete exposure at the same time.

The next laboratory uses **24 months with the change starting at 13**, allowing a longer adaptation path. At endpoint 16, the latest four observations all have beta 2, whereas expanding mixes 12 old and four new observations, giving $(12\times1+4\times2)/16=1.25$.

**Pitfall:** concluding that the fast-adapting window is always more accurate. With disturbances, it can jump because a few observations changed. A long window can be useful under stability and misleading after a change; a short window can adapt and also be noisy. No window length establishes its own suitability.

### When the trade-off matters

After a documented mandate, leverage or holdings change, compare recent and historical estimates and inspect the relevant holdings-based evidence. [CFA Institute describes factor models as tools for measuring and managing exposures](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/using-multifactor-models); choosing a sample is part of that model specification.

## Let estimates move while the underlying beta stays fixed

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A four-month estimated beta changes from 0.575 to 1.2. Must the underlying exposure have changed?" options={[
 {text:'No; different disturbance realizations can move estimates even when the generating beta is constant',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; a regression estimate is the exact true parameter'},
 {text:'Yes, if both fits include an intercept, because that removes sampling error'},
]} explanation="A changing estimate combines possible parameter change with sampling effects and specification/data issues. The constructed example below holds true beta fixed and still produces those two estimates." />

Set true beta to **1 for every month**, zero true intercept, factor −1,+1 repeated, and add this fixed eight-month disturbance sequence in percentage points:

$$\varepsilon=(0.8,-0.4,0.6,0.1,-0.7,0.5,-0.2,-0.6).$$

The sequence repeats in the laboratory. It is a deliberately chosen teaching path, not a representative random sample or an empirical distribution. To interpret the stated beta as a conditional-mean coefficient, one possible additional toy model makes disturbances iid and independent of the factors, uniform over the symmetric values ±0.1, ±0.2, ±0.4, ±0.5, ±0.6, ±0.7 and ±0.8 pp. That model has zero conditional mean and permits the displayed path. We do not use its probabilities to claim that this selected pattern is typical or to calibrate a confidence interval.

In the first four months, asset returns are −0.2,+0.6,−0.4,+1.1. Their mean is 0.275 pp; factor mean is zero. The centered factor sum of squares is 4. Since the factors sum to zero, subtracting the return mean cancels from their cross-product, leaving

$$S_{fy}=(-1)(-0.2)+(1)(0.6)+(-1)(-0.4)+(1)(1.1)=2.3.$$

So $\hat\beta=2.3/4=0.575$ and $\hat\alpha=0.275$ pp. Months 5–8 instead have returns −1.7,+1.5,−1.2,+0.4: their mean is −0.25 pp and cross-product 4.8, giving $\hat\beta=4.8/4=1.2$. The actual generating beta remained 1.

<RollingExposureLab client:visible view="noise" scenario="stable" noiseAmplitude={1} cutoff={8} title="Move the sample without changing the constructed exposure" caption="Initial case: true beta is 1 throughout, with the declared fixed disturbance sequence and amplitude 1 pp. The four-observation estimate at month 4 is 0.575; at month 8 it is 1.2. Moving estimates therefore need not establish a structural change. Overlapping windows share most of their observations; the points on their fitted paths are not independent experiments." />

There is a further trap in the chart's smoothness. Consecutive W-observation windows share W−1 observations. Twenty adjacent beta estimates are not twenty independent confirmations of a change. A smooth-looking drift can be generated partly by the mechanical entry and exit of the same few observations.

**Pitfall:** labeling every change “style drift.” Estimated movement can reflect disturbance variation, outliers, stale or nonsynchronous fund marks, factor-definition revisions, a changed omitted exposure, or a real portfolio change. A rolling regression alone does not identify which mechanism occurred.

When a monitoring chart triggers an investigation. Check the observations that entered and left, input definitions and available holdings evidence; compare residual behavior across plausible specifications. Treat the rolling path as a diagnostic that asks a question.

## Check factor variation before trusting slope precision

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Every factor observation in a window is zero, while the asset return moves. What factor beta does that sample identify?" options={[
 {text:'It does not identify a slope: there is no factor variation against which to measure a response',correct:true},
 {text:'Exactly zero, because zero factor returns force zero exposure'},
 {text:'Exactly one, because the intercept absorbs everything else'},
]} explanation="With an intercept, slope identification requires centered factor variation. A constant factor leaves the slope nonidentified; a software fallback of zero would create information that the sample does not contain." />

A loading measures response to factor variation. If the factor hardly varies, a disturbance can dominate that comparison. The denominator $S_{ff}$ in the slope formula records this information: zero makes the slope nonidentified, while a small positive value can make the estimate sensitive to relatively small return changes.

Use the first four noisy observations above. The fitted values are −0.3,+0.85,−0.3,+0.85 pp. Residuals, actual minus fitted, are +0.1,−0.25,−0.1,+0.25 pp, so their **residual sum of squares**, SSE, is

$$\text{SSE}=0.1^2+(-0.25)^2+(-0.1)^2+0.25^2=0.145\text{ pp}^2.$$

Under an **additional hypothesized constant-coefficient model with iid, zero-conditional-mean, homoskedastic errors**, the conventional slope standard error is

$$s^2=\frac{\text{SSE}}{n-2},\qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta)=\sqrt{\frac{s^2}{S_{ff}}}.$$

“Iid” means independent and identically distributed; “homoskedastic” means equal error variance. There are two fitted coefficients, so $n-2$ is the residual degrees of freedom. With n = 4 and $S_{ff}=4\text{ pp}^2$, $s^2=0.145/2=0.0725\text{ pp}^2$ and $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta)=\sqrt{0.0725/4}\approx0.134629$. The ratio cancels the squared return units, leaving beta's dimensionless units.

This is a reproducible **standard-error calculation under stated assumptions**, not a validated confidence interval for our handpicked disturbance path. It does not prove those assumptions or establish a break-test significance level.

Now reduce factor amplitude from 1 pp to **0.1 pp**, keeping the same true beta and disturbances. Centered factor variation falls from 4 to 0.04 pp². The intercept remains 0.275, the estimated slope becomes **−3.25**, and the residual SSE remains 0.145 in this construction. The conventional slope SE becomes $\sqrt{0.0725/0.04}\approx1.346291$, ten times larger. If amplitude is zero, there is no identified slope at all; the mean return still identifies the intercept when every factor value is zero. A nonzero constant factor instead leaves only the combined intercept-plus-factor level identified.

<RollingExposureLab client:visible view="rank" scenario="stable" noiseAmplitude={1} cutoff={4} title="Separate weak factor variation from evidence of low exposure" caption="Initial sample: four observations, factor amplitude 1 pp and the fixed disturbance sequence. Reduce factor amplitude to 0.1 to see beta −3.25 with the same residual SSE and ten times the conventional slope standard error. At zero amplitude, the slope is nonidentified and no sloping fit or slope SE is reported. The conventional SE interpretation requires the adjacent explicit assumptions." />

Two distinct factor observations can identify an intercept and slope, but leave no residual degrees of freedom for this standard-error calculation. Coefficients being available does not mean their uncertainty has been estimated.

With several factors, enough observations are necessary but not sufficient: the predictor columns and intercept must have adequate **rank**, meaning they are not exact linear combinations of one another. Strong collinearity can make individual loadings unstable even when fitted returns change little. Adding more factors can therefore change both the question and its estimation uncertainty.

**Pitfall:** believing that a heteroskedasticity/autocorrelation-consistent standard error, often called HAC, repairs stale data or a changing coefficient model. It changes an uncertainty estimate under its assumptions, rather than automatically changing the OLS point estimate or proving a causal exposure. It need not always be larger than the conventional standard error. [NIST recommends residual diagnostics beyond a single fit statistic](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmd/section4/pmd44.htm), and high R² alone does not validate a model.

### When identification comes first

Before interpreting an unexpectedly large, small or negative loading. Inspect factor variation, paired sample count, rank, influential observations and the proposed error model. Report a nonidentified quantity as unavailable.

## Ask what would establish a structural change

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Fitting separate intercepts and slopes before and after a chosen date lowers in-sample SSE. Is that result alone evidence of a real structural break?" options={[
 {text:'No; adding parameters can reduce in-sample residuals even without a true change',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; any lower SSE proves a new economic regime'},
 {text:'Yes, provided the date was selected after examining the entire sample'},
]} explanation="Separate lines contain the pooled line as a special case, so their best in-sample fit cannot be worse. Inference requires assumptions, an appropriate comparison and treatment of any search over candidate dates." />

A **structural break** is a change in the modeled relationship's parameters, such as beta or intercept, rather than simply a change in a fitted estimate. In practice it is a hypothesis about data generation. A manager's mandate change may supply external evidence; a visual kink supplies a candidate to investigate.

Return to the **noiseless 24-month construction**, beta 1 for months 1–12 and 2 for months 13–24, zero intercept and alternating ±1 pp factor. Pooling all observations fits beta 1.5 and alpha zero. Each observation misses by 0.5 pp in magnitude, giving

$$\text{SSE}_{\text{pooled}}=24\times0.5^2=6\text{ pp}^2.$$

A predeclared split with month 13 first in the second segment fits betas 1 and 2 and zero SSE. That matches the truth because we constructed both the data and the change. A **wrong split at month 9** also improves fit: the first eight have beta 1; the last 16 mix four old and 12 new observations, fitting beta $(4\times1+12\times2)/16=1.75$. Its SSE is

$$4(1-1.75)^2+12(2-1.75)^2=2.25+0.75=3\text{ pp}^2.$$

The wrong split halves pooled SSE. A better fit alone did not identify the true boundary. Two separate intercept/slope pairs use four parameters instead of two. With constant true beta 1, disturbance amplitude 1 pp and candidate split 13 in this same laboratory, pooled SSE is 6.6225 pp² and split SSE is 6.178333 pp². The approximately 0.444167 pp² improvement occurs without a generating-parameter change.

<RollingExposureLab client:visible view="split" cutoff={24} title="Compare pooled fit with an explicitly chosen split" caption="Initial construction has a known change at 13 and no disturbances: pooled SSE is 6 pp squared; splitting at 13 gives zero. Moving the candidate split to 9 gives SSE 3, still below pooled despite the wrong date. The scenario control can remove the true change; add the declared disturbances to inspect fitting flexibility under stability. This is an in-sample comparison, not a calibrated break test." />

For a real analysis, distinguish a split specified from external information from one selected after scanning the data. A formal break test needs its null model, sample-size and error assumptions, and a method that accounts for any unknown-date search or repeated testing. Our noiseless toy has no estimated real-world significance level; it cannot supply one by drawing a larger gap.

Also investigate changes in factor definitions, currency, leverage, fees or fund pricing. A return-model break need not reveal its economic cause, and a real holdings change need not produce a statistically detectable beta jump in a short sample.

**Pitfall:** calling the best-looking break date “out of sample” after using the whole sample to select it. Its selection consumed the information. Extra parameters and a date search both affect the evidence needed to make a reliable inference.

### When to act on the diagnosis

When changes in holdings, mandate and residual behavior make the stable specification questionable. Compare plausible models and specify how evidence will be checked on later observations. A monitoring alert can justify investigation without proving the cause.

## Evaluate a frozen rule one observation later

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="To assess a beta estimate available through month 12 against month 13, which procedure preserves the time boundary?" options={[
 {text:'Fit only through 12, freeze the rule, then reveal month 13’s realized factor/asset pair for evaluation',correct:true},
 {text:'Include month 13 in the fit and label its fitted residual a forecast error'},
 {text:'Choose the window that wins all future observations before starting the test'},
]} explanation="A future outcome can score an earlier estimate after it occurs. It cannot also enter that earlier fit or the supposedly untouched selection of the rule." />

A **walk-forward evaluation** repeats a dated process: fit on available observations, freeze the rule for the next observation, record its error when that observation arrives, then advance. Fix candidate rules and evaluation dates before judging them. Input revisions, publication lags and costs matter if you later turn a risk model into an actual investment procedure.

Here is the one-step boundary in the noiseless change-at-13 example. At cutoff 12, the last four observations fit beta 1 and alpha zero. Month 13 later realizes factor −1 and asset excess return −2. The earlier fit's **conditional fitted return**, evaluated using that later realized factor, is

$$\hat y_{13\mid12}=\hat\alpha_{12}+\hat\beta_{12}f_{13}=0+1(-1)=-1\text{ pp}.$$

Its error, actual minus fitted, is $-2-(-1)=-1\text{ pp}$ and squared error is 1 pp². The realized factor is used **after the fact to diagnose the exposure model**. We have not predicted that factor or claimed that it was known before trading.

If you improperly refit using the target itself, the four-observation sample ends at 13, giving alpha −0.25 and beta 1.25. The same factor −1 then produces $-0.25+1.25(-1)=-1.5\text{ pp}$, with apparent error −0.5 and squared error 0.25. The “improvement” used the answer in the fit.

In the first path, the four-month rule has errors −1,+1,−0.5,+0.5 pp for the first four target dates and zero afterward. Thus $\text{MSE}=(1+1+0.25+0.25)/12=5/24\approx0.208333\text{ pp}^2$. The longer window wins neither path universally: in the stable disturbed example the eight-month rule has the smallest error among these three rules. These are two chosen constructions, not evidence that a particular window will win in markets.

**Pitfall:** selecting the four-month rule because it won the first test and still calling that test untouched evidence. It has now become selection data; assessing the selected rule needs later held-out information or a properly designed nested time-based procedure. Do not search all window lengths and report only the winner as though it had been fixed in advance.

### What the score does and does not establish

The score compares a specified conditional exposure model on shared dated observations. It is not a forecast of factor returns, a financed hedge's P&L, a fee-adjusted investment return or a causal proof of skill. A decision to trade needs its own implementable cash/risk accounting and a defensible objective.
