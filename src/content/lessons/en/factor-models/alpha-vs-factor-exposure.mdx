You add a factor to a fund's regression and its alpha **rises**. The fund's returns have not changed and the spreadsheet is not necessarily broken. The market beta may have changed too, along with the attribution assigned to every factor. An intercept is a model-dependent remainder, not a trophy that can only get smaller as the model grows.

This lesson builds on [estimating factor exposures](/factor-models/estimating-factor-exposures). We will distinguish three returns that are often given the same name: return above cash, return above a chosen benchmark and the intercept left after a factor regression. All worked data are invented. The four-period visual is an arithmetic demonstration, not a sample large enough to assess a manager.

For a time-series factor model, write the fund's return above the risk-free return as

$$y_t=R_{P,t}-R_{f,t}=\alpha+\sum_k\beta_k f_{k,t}+\varepsilon_t$$

Here $f_{k,t}$ is factor $k$'s return in period $t$; $\beta_k$ is its **loading**, or sensitivity; $\alpha$ is the **intercept**; and $\varepsilon_t$ is the period's **residual**, the difference between the observation and fitted return. An intercept and an individual residual are not the same quantity.

For ordinary least squares, or **OLS**, fitted with an intercept on the same sample, the residuals average to zero. Therefore the sample means satisfy

$$\bar y=\hat\alpha+\sum_k\hat\beta_k\bar f_k$$

A factor's mean contribution is its fitted loading times its sample mean return. A long-run expected factor premium is a different input: using it for a forecast requires assumptions about the future, rather than merely reading this sample identity.

### A one-period remainder is not automatically alpha

Suppose a year's fund return is **14%**, cash return **3%**, and fitted factor contribution **10%**. Excess return is 14% − 3% = **11%**. The period remainder is 11% − 10% = **1 percentage point**. In the equation above, that 1 point is **alpha plus that year's residual**. One observation does not separate them.

A factor model is like an itemised bill: changing what is classified as an ingredient changes the remainder. It does not certify that the remainder is the chef's talent. Model error, omitted exposures, implementation costs, timing and luck can also enter an estimated alpha. The [CFA Institute overview explains factor models and their performance uses](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/using-multifactor-models).

### When to use it

State the model, sample window, return frequency, currency and treatment of fees before comparing intercepts. A positive estimate is not proof of skill; a negative estimate is not proof of incompetence. Examine uncertainty, stability and whether the chosen factors answer the investor's question.

Adding a regressor cannot increase OLS residual sum of squares on the same sample: the old fitted model remains an available restricted choice. But that does **not** make the intercept monotonic. A better in-sample fit to variation is not the same as a larger mean factor contribution.

### See the simultaneous refit

The visual constructs four periods with zero risk-free returns. The market factor $M$ averages **1%**, and the style factor $S$ has an adjustable mean. For the initial case its mean is **1%**. The invented fund is generated by

$$y_t=0.5\%+1\cdot M_t+bS_t+e_t$$

Start with $b=1$. The four observations are:

| Period | Market factor | Style factor | Fund excess return | Full-model residual |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | −1% | −1% | −1.3% | +0.2% |
| 2 | 0% | +1.5% | +1.6% | −0.4% |
| 3 | +2% | +2.5% | +5.4% | +0.4% |
| 4 | +3% | +1% | +4.3% | −0.2% |
| Mean | +1% | +1% | +2.5% | 0% |

The full-model residual is orthogonal to the intercept and both factors, so refitting recovers the generating coefficients in this demonstration. The two model fits to the **same fund observations** are:

| Fit | Market loading | Style loading | Mean market contribution | Mean style contribution | Intercept | Total mean |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Market only | 1.5 | Omitted | 1.5 × 1% = 1.5% | — | 1% | 2.5% |
| Market and style | 1 | 1 | 1 × 1% = 1% | 1 × 1% = 1% | 0.5% | 2.5% |

Alpha falls by **0.5 points**, not by the entire **1-point** new style contribution. The market contribution also falls by 0.5 points. Holding the old beta fixed while subtracting the new factor would not be the same refitted regression.

### Reverse the direction without a negative factor premium

Keep the style exposure at one, but use the separate invented case in which the style factor averages **0.25%**. The fund mean is now 0.5% + 1% + 0.25% = **1.75%**. Within that case:

- Market-only loading = **1.5**, contribution = **1.5%**, intercept = 1.75% − 1.5% = **0.25%**
- Full-model loadings = **1 and 1**, contributions = **1% and 0.25%**, intercept = 1.75% − 1% − 0.25% = **0.5%**

Adding a factor with a **positive** loading and **positive** sample mean has raised alpha by **0.25 points**. It reduced the total attributed mean contribution after all slopes were refitted. The 2.5% and 1.75% fund means belong to different generated cases; each within-case model comparison uses identical fund observations.

### When to use it

Use this check whenever someone subtracts a new loading-times-premium from an old alpha without refitting the retained factors. Compare the same sample and reporting conventions; a changed sample can change the intercept for additional reasons. Improved in-sample fit does not establish a better out-of-sample model.

## The omitted factor has a projection intercept too

    "text": "Yes, every positive mean must increase the intercept",

To make the direction precise, project the omitted factor $S$ onto the retained market factor on the same sample:

$$S_t=a+\gamma M_t+v_t$$

Substituting into the full model and using the OLS projection relationships gives

$$\alpha_R=\alpha_U+ba,\qquad\beta_{M,R}=\beta_{M,U}+b\gamma$$

$R$ labels the restricted market-only model and $U$ the unrestricted two-factor model. The relevant omitted-factor contribution to the restricted intercept is **loading times projection intercept**, not unconditionally loading times the raw factor mean.

### Work the projection in the examples

Our data have $\gamma=0.5$ and a market mean of 1%. Consequently $a=\bar S-0.5\bar M$:

| Style-factor mean | Projection intercept | With style loading one, restricted alpha | Full alpha | Change on adding style |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1% | 1% − 0.5 × 1% = +0.5% | 0.5% + 0.5% = 1% | 0.5% | −0.5 points |
| 0.5% | 0.5% − 0.5 × 1% = 0% | 0.5% | 0.5% | 0 points |
| 0.25% | 0.25% − 0.5 × 1% = −0.25% | 0.5% − 0.25% = 0.25% | 0.5% | +0.25 points |

The model can move the intercept in either direction or leave it unchanged. “Hidden beta” is a useful possibility to investigate, not a theorem that every extra factor destroys alpha.

### When to use it

Inspect the factor's relationship to those already included, the estimated loading and the sample means. Strongly correlated regressors can make coefficient estimates unstable even when fitted returns change little. Economic justification and validation matter alongside the algebra; adding every available factor can fit noise or change the question being asked.

## Appraisal and information ratios use different returns

<MCQ

We use these explicit conventions:

$$\operatorname{AR}=\frac{\hat\alpha}{\hat\sigma_\varepsilon}$$

$$a_t=R_{P,t}-R_{B,t},\qquad\operatorname{IR}=\frac{\bar a}{\operatorname{SD}(a_t)}$$

The appraisal ratio divides a specified model's intercept by **residual volatility**. The information ratio divides mean **active return**, portfolio minus the chosen benchmark, by **tracking error**, the standard deviation of that active-return series. The benchmark, return period and fee basis belong to the definitions.

The [Nasdaq glossary defines appraisal using alpha and residual standard deviation](https://www.nasdaq.com/glossary/a/appraisal-ratio). The [CFA Institute paper explains that information-ratio naming has multiple conventions](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/-/media/documents/code/gips/sharpe-ratio-and-the-information-ratio.pdf): some authors also use that name for a residual-return ratio. Read the numerator and denominator rather than relying on the name alone. Here we retain the two separate names above.

### Calculate both on the four-period data

With style exposure and mean both one, full-model alpha is **0.5%**. The full residuals are +0.2%, −0.4%, +0.4%, −0.2%. For this descriptive demonstration, treat the four observations as equally weighted and divide variance by **4**:

$$\hat\sigma_\varepsilon=\sqrt{\frac{0.2^2+(-0.4)^2+0.4^2+(-0.2)^2}{4}}=\sqrt{0.1}\approx 0.316228\%$$

Appraisal ratio = 0.5 ÷ 0.316228 ≈ **1.5811**.

Choose the **market return** as the benchmark, with the risk-free rate zero in this example. The active returns are −0.3%, +1.6%, +3.4%, +1.3%, averaging **1.5%**. Their deviations from that mean are −1.8, +0.1, +1.9 and −0.2 percentage points:

$$\operatorname{TE}=\sqrt{\frac{(-1.8)^2+0.1^2+1.9^2+(-0.2)^2}{4}}=\sqrt{1.725}\approx 1.313393\%$$

Information ratio = 1.5 ÷ 1.313393 ≈ **1.1421**. Different numerators, different risk series, different answers. The visual shows the information ratio once because changing the fitted model within a state does not change the fund–benchmark active returns.

### When to use it

Specify the volatility estimator. A sample standard deviation with divisor $n-1$ or a regression residual standard error with divisor $n-k-1$ will give different small-sample values from this descriptive divisor-four example. Do not silently mix them. Match time units; annualising a short-period ratio needs assumptions about the return process and serial dependence.

Zero residual volatility or zero tracking error makes the corresponding ordinary ratio undefined; a zero denominator is not an infinite-quality manager. A high historical ratio, particularly from four constructed observations, does not prove repeatable skill. If the benchmark's return equals the fitted factor return plus the same cash component, active returns may equal alpha plus residuals and the ratios can coincide; that is a special alignment, not a definition.

## Mean attribution is a comparison under a model

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"A fund’s average annual excess return is 11%, while its fitted mean factor contributions sum to 11.65% on the same OLS sample. What is the fitted annual intercept?"}

  pretest
/>

Keep the original attribution numbers, now with the statistical scope visible. Suppose the **same sample's arithmetic annual means** are fund return 14% and risk-free return 3%, so mean excess return is **11%**. The fitted constant loadings and observed mean factor returns give:

| Factor | Fitted loading | Mean annual factor return | Mean contribution |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Market excess return | 1.10 | 6% | 6.60% |
| Size | 0.50 | 2.5% | 1.25% |
| Value | 0.40 | 3.5% | 1.40% |
| Momentum | 0.60 | 4% | 2.40% |
| Sum | — | — | **11.65%** |

Fitted alpha = 11% − (6.60% + 1.25% + 1.40% + 2.40%) = **−0.65% per year**. This is a negative intercept relative to the specified factor model on that sample. It does not show what a particular ETF clone actually earned after costs, nor does it establish that the manager lacked skill.

Suppose annual residual volatility is measured as **4%** on the same basis. Appraisal ratio = −0.65 ÷ 4 = **−0.1625**. Separately, the market benchmark's average total return is cash 3% + market excess 6% = **9%**. The fund's mean active return is 14% − 9% = **5%**. If annual tracking error versus that benchmark is **10%**, information ratio = 5 ÷ 10 = **0.5**. Positive index-relative performance can coexist with negative multi-factor alpha.

### When to use it

Choose a benchmark appropriate to the mandate and an economically defensible factor model. Check available instruments, investability, leverage, turnover and fees before claiming a theoretical factor return could have been replicated cheaply. Examine confidence intervals, stability, out-of-sample performance and the costs faced by the investor. A richer model can help answer a question; it can also add noise or remove a return source the mandate intentionally rewards.
