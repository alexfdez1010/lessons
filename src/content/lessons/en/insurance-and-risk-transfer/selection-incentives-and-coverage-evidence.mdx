An insurer can estimate each risk type correctly and still misprice the pool it actually attracts. A buyer can understand the price and still misread what a claim must prove. Information and incentives belong beside the payment formula.

## Separate hidden risk type from the composition you insure

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"The same risk types remain, but higher-risk buyers become a larger share. What can change?"}
options={[{"text": "The enrolled pool’s expected claims per policy", "correct": true}, {"text": "Every lower-risk individual’s probability must increase"}, {"text": "Only claim severity; membership can never affect means"}]}
explanation={"Changing composition changes the weighted average."}
/>

**Adverse selection** arises when differences in information and willingness to purchase change the risk mix entering a contract. It concerns selection of participants, not a verdict about the character of an individual claimant.

Take two types, each facing a 1,000 EUR loss. Lower-risk buyers have annual event probability 1%, so expected claim cost under full cover is $0.01(1000)=10$. Higher-risk buyers have probability 10%, giving 100. With half of each type, expected claim cost per policy is $0.5(10)+0.5(100)=55$.

Stipulate a premium of 80 and zero other costs. That composition gives expected premium-minus-claims of $80-55=25$. If lower-risk buyers leave and all remaining buyers are higher-risk, expected claims become 100 and the margin becomes $80-100=-20$. No loss severity or type-specific probability changed; membership did.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="selection" title="Change the enrolled mix without changing each risk type" />

This construction illustrates a mechanism, not a claim that real markets inevitably unravel. Underwriting—assessing risks and deciding terms—information, participation rules, competition and applicable law can change the result. One observed claim does not identify an individual's underlying probability. The [NAIC glossary](https://content.naic.org/glossary-insurance-terms) distinguishes adverse selection from the claim and underwriting processes.

### Compare the expected pool with the enrolled pool

Use composition scenarios when interpreting a quoted expected cost. Avoid multiplying the size of a newly selected pool by an average estimated for a different population. Raising a premium alone need not leave participation unchanged.

## Examine incentives without assuming fraud

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"Prevention reduces total expected damage by 30. Must the insured privately save 30?"}
options={[{"text": "No; its retained part and other assumptions determine private savings", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; the entire reduction always remains with the insured"}, {"text": "Only when the insurer has no claims at all in the year"}]}
explanation={"Under retained loss 100, the private reduction is 3 before effort cost 10."}
/>

**Moral hazard** describes how protection can change incentives or actions after cover is available. It is different from selection of pre-existing risk types. The term does not imply that all insured people behave carelessly, deliberately cause losses or commit fraud.

Suppose prevention costs 10 EUR and reduces an event probability from 5% to 2%, with loss 1,000. Without cover, the reduction in expected loss is $(0.05-0.02)(1000)=30$; after the prevention cost, the buyer's monetary benefit is 20.

Now assume cover leaves exactly 100 of every event loss with the buyer. Holding the premium fixed, prevention reduces their expected retained loss by $0.03(100)=3$. Paying 10 produces a private monetary difference of $3-10=-7$. The total expected loss still falls by 30; the insurance arrangement changes who receives that reduction.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="incentives" title="Compare private prevention benefit with effort cost" />

With 500 retained, the private reduction is 15 and net benefit 5. At 1,000 retained it is 30 and 20 again. These comparisons ignore any nonmonetary harm, premium feedback, legal duties and prevention benefits to others. They therefore do not predict actual behaviour or prescribe an “optimal deductible.”

### Read incentive clauses with their coverage trade-off

Deductibles, inspections, risk-based terms and prevention support can affect incentives, but more retention also leaves more financial risk with the buyer. Separate that trade-off from exclusion of an event and from a requirement to provide truthful evidence. An incentive explanation does not determine what an actual policy legally owes.

## Validate the event and evidence before applying the formula

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"A damage photo is available but cause is disputed. What follows?"}
options={[{"text": "Evidence of damage alone may not establish coverage", "correct": true}, {"text": "The policy limit must automatically be paid in full"}, {"text": "A dispute proves the loss definitely falls outside the policy"}]}
explanation={"Eligibility needs the actual wording, cause, period and evidence."}
/>

A calculator answers “how much?” only after the contract answers “for which event, interest and period?” A photograph of damage proves something happened, but may not establish its cause, amount or coverage.

Use an invented annual property policy with premium 200. It covers fire during the term and excludes water damage. Eligible claims have a 500 deductible, no percentage share and no binding limit. For verified fire damage of 4,000, payment is $4000-500=3500$, retained loss 500 and buyer cost including premium 700. For water damage of exactly the same amount, this example's exclusion gives payment zero and buyer cost $4000+200=4200$.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="exclusions" title="Change the stipulated cause before applying the deductible" />

The arithmetic does not say real policies all exclude water, or that a disputed cause is already resolved. If cause or timing is unknown, “not yet determined” is different from “definitely excluded.” A complete record should connect the policy version, insured interest, event date, cause and loss evidence to the claim decision.

### Use a claim checklist with the full wording

Check notice requirements, supporting documents, treatment of damaged property and dispute channels under the applicable contract and jurisdiction. Do not invent a universal deadline from one product. The [EU non-life information summary](https://www.eiopa.europa.eu/browse/regulation-and-policy/insurance-distribution-directive-idd_en) identifies main cover, exclusions and obligations, while complete terms appear in the other documents. Coverage uncertainty, insurer default and delayed payment are three different problems.
