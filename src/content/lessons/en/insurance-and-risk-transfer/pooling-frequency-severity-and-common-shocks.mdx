One household cannot average away its own fire. A pool can share many households' losses—but only the risks that do not all arrive together. This lesson separates average loss, uncertainty and the dependence that remains after a pool grows.

The probabilities are stipulated models, not claims about actual insurance frequencies. Losses and claim sizes in this lesson are the insurer’s payment obligations under invented full cover, without additional deductibles, percentage participation or caps. They do not inherit the earlier contracts. We introduce the needed statistics directly.

## Pool independent losses without promising a fixed total

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"Quadruple independent identical policies. What happens to the average’s standard deviation?"}
options={[{"text": "It halves while the total’s standard deviation doubles", "correct": true}, {"text": "Both total and average standard deviations fall to zero"}, {"text": "The expected loss of each policy becomes one quarter"}]}
explanation={"Square-root scaling concerns independent dispersion, not each mean."}
/>

A **pool** combines exposures whose losses are shared through an insurance arrangement. Model each annual loss $X$ as 5,000 EUR with probability $p=0.02$ and zero otherwise. This is a **Bernoulli loss**: a binary event multiplied by its amount. Its mean is $E[X]=0.02(5000)=100$.

**Variance** is the expected squared distance from the mean. Here $E[X^2]=0.02(5000^2)=500000$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X)=500000-100^2=490000$ EUR squared. The **standard deviation**, the square root of variance, is 700 EUR. It measures dispersion, not a maximum loss or a promised confidence interval.

Assume $n$ identical losses are mutually independent. The total has mean $100n$ and variance $490000n$. The average per policy has variance $490000/n$ and standard deviation $700/\sqrt n$.

Four times as many independent exposures halves the average's standard deviation while doubling the total's. Pooling makes the per-policy average more stable; it does not shrink the amount of business the insurer has promised to fund. A finite pool can still have an unusually bad year.

### Use the independence assumption visibly

Use this benchmark before deciding whether geographic, peril or customer concentration undermines it. Premium also funds administration, claims handling, distribution, risk-bearing capital and other contract-specific costs. Expected claims of 100 do not prove that any premium above 100 is pure profit.

## Construct a common shock instead of assuming diversification

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"A common shock affects many policies. What independence term was missing?"}
options={[{"text": "Positive pairwise covariance in total variance", "correct": true}, {"text": "An automatic fall in each policy’s expected loss"}, {"text": "A legal promise that every pool must be equally correlated"}]}
explanation={"The same marginal means can coexist with very different dependence."}
/>

A storm hitting every house is different from unrelated kitchen accidents. **Covariance** records how losses move together; positive covariance adds uncertainty that independence would omit. **Correlation** divides covariance by the two standard deviations.

Construct a precise mixture for the same 100 policies. With probability $\rho$, give every policy the same Bernoulli event $Z$ with event probability 0.02. With probability $1-\rho$, draw their Bernoulli events independently, still with probability 0.02. In either branch each policy has the same mean 100 and variance 490,000. For two different policies, covariance is $\rho(490000)$, giving pairwise correlation $\rho$.

To check that covariance, the common branch has $E[X_iX_j]=E[X^2]=500000$; the independent branch has $E[X_iX_j]=100^2=10000$. Their mixture has cross-product mean $\rho(500000)+(1-\rho)(10000)$. Subtracting $E[X_i]E[X_j]=10000$ leaves $\rho(490000)$.

There are $n$ individual variance terms. There are $n(n-1)$ ordered pairs of different policies, each contributing that covariance. Divide total variance by $n^2$ to obtain the variance of the average:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{490000}{n}\left[1+(n-1)\rho\right].$$

At $n=100$ and $\rho=0.25$, variance of the average is $4900(1+99\times0.25)=126175$, with standard deviation approximately 355.211205 EUR. At independence it was 70. At $\rho=1$, every policy always shares the same event; the average is as uncertain as one policy, with standard deviation 700.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="correlation" title="Add a precisely constructed common-loss branch" />

As $n$ grows with fixed positive $\rho$, the average's variance approaches $490000\rho$, not zero. The component's control is the probability of the common branch **in this construction**. Correlation is not a universal parameter of insurance, nor does it identify a real catastrophe probability without a model.

### Stress the joint loss process

Use shared-peril scenarios and concentration information alongside marginal claim rates. Spreading exposures across addresses does not automatically spread their weather, legal, cyber or economic drivers. A correlation formula also cannot describe every tail-dependence pattern from one number.

## Weight frequency and severity by the same population

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"Both frequency and severity rise in one environment. Can we multiply two equally weighted regime means?"}
options={[{"text": "Not generally; the joint weighting of claims matters", "correct": true}, {"text": "Always; expectation of a product is every product of means"}, {"text": "Only after deleting the higher-frequency regime entirely"}]}
explanation={"Average conditional loss first, or use claim-weighted severity."}
/>

**Frequency** is claim count per exposure and period; **severity** is claim size. Conditional on an environment, suppose sizes are independent of count. Then expected total loss in that environment is expected count times expected size. Mixing environments requires preserving their joint weighting.

There are two equally likely regimes. In the low regime, expected count is 0.2 and mean claim size 500 EUR, so expected total is $0.2(500)=100$. In the high regime, count is 0.8 and mean size 2,000, giving $0.8(2000)=1600$. Counts are nonnegative integers in individual outcomes; 0.2 and 0.8 are expectations.

Average the conditional totals: $0.5(100)+0.5(1600)=850$ EUR. Mean count is $0.5(0.2)+0.5(0.8)=0.5$. Averaging the two severity means by environment gives 1,250, but $0.5(1250)=625$ is the wrong expected total: high-count environments generate more claims.

The correct mean severity for a claim selected from the combined claim population weights the regimes by expected claims:

$$\frac{0.5(0.2)(500)+0.5(0.8)(2000)}{0.5(0.2)+0.5(0.8)}=1700.$$

Now expected count times claim-weighted severity is $0.5(1700)=850$.

### Preserve conditional assumptions when mixing risks

Use this decomposition to explain what a price or reserve estimate averages. Do not multiply two conveniently available means drawn from differently weighted populations. The identity relies on the specified count/size assumptions or correctly defined claim-weighted severity; it is not a licence to ignore dependence.
