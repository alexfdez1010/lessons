A repair bill arrives for 5,000 EUR. The insurance policy does not erase the bill: it allocates it between people who have different contractual obligations. To understand the protection, follow both the promise and the cash.

All contracts, prices, probabilities and dates in this course are **invented teaching examples**. Terms vary by product and jurisdiction. The course concerns risk transfer, not life-annuity design or a recommendation to buy a particular policy.

## Identify the promise and the parties

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"A policy names a limit. What else is needed before treating it as a payable claim?"}
options={[{"text": "A matching covered event and the actual payment terms", "correct": true}, {"text": "Only a premium receipt with any requested amount"}, {"text": "Only the limit multiplied by the number of insured people"}]}
explanation={"A limit constrains a defined benefit; it does not establish eligibility."}
/>

**Insurance** transfers a specified financial consequence of an uncertain event in exchange for a **premium**, the price of the cover. The **policy** is the contract. The **insurer** promises the stated benefit; the **policyholder** contracts with it and is responsible for the premium under its terms. The **insured** is the person or interest protected, while a **beneficiary** is entitled to receive a benefit under the wording. One person can fill several roles, but need not do so. A company might buy cover for equipment, with a lender named to receive a defined part of a payment.

A **claim** is a request for a contractual benefit, not proof that the whole requested amount is payable. The event, insured interest, coverage period, exclusions and payment rule must match. A policy limit is not a balance you can withdraw whenever you want.

For a covered repair loss of 5,000, stipulate a 500 EUR **deductible**: the buyer retains the first 500 of each loss. No percentage participation or binding limit applies here. The insurer pays $\max(5000-500,0)=4500$; the buyer retains $5000-4500=500$. A smaller loss of 300 gives payment zero and retention 300. Retention never becomes negative.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="contract" title="Allocate a covered loss before adding the premium" />

The deductible is part of the loss allocation. It is not automatically a second fee paid to the insurer. Who pays a repairer directly, and whether the buyer must advance the bill, are separate cash arrangements. The [NAIC consumer explanation](https://content.naic.org/consumer/how-does-insurance-work) introduces the contract, premium, covered loss and claim process; our arithmetic states more specific invented terms.

## Add the premium without confusing it with a loss

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"Can cover raise expected buyer cost while reducing a large loss?"}
options={[{"text": "Yes; a premium can buy transfer of an unaffordable state", "correct": true}, {"text": "No; every policy must improve its buyer’s expected wealth"}, {"text": "Only if the premium is refunded in every claim-free year"}]}
explanation={"Expected cost and dispersion across states are different quantities."}
/>

Think of a premium as paying for a standby promise. You can receive the agreed service—cover during the period—without a claim occurring. A claim-free year does not by itself prove that the contract failed.

Let the annual premium be 200 EUR, paid once. At most one covered event occurs during the year: a 5,000 loss with probability 2%, otherwise no loss. Probability is the model's frequency assumption across hypothetical repetitions, not a forecast guaranteed for an individual year. Keep every amount at the same date in this first simplified comparison; ignore interest, tax and other costs.

The **expected value** is the probability-weighted average. Without insurance it is $0.98(0)+0.02(5000)=100$. With insurance it is $200+0.02(500)=210$. The expected insurer payment is $0.02(4500)=90$. Thus the premium exceeds expected payment by 110 in this toy contract, before specifying insurer expenses or capital costs.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="retention" title="Compare state costs and expected costs" />

Insurance has raised the buyer's expected monetary cost while reducing the large-loss state from 5,000 to 700. Those are compatible facts. A person with a limited cash buffer may value that transfer; their circumstances and preferences determine the trade-off. Neither a positive nor negative expected value alone identifies the appropriate cover.

## Place the promise and payment on their dates

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"A valid payment arrives one year after the repair. What can still matter?"}
options={[{"text": "The cash needed before the receivable is collected", "correct": true}, {"text": "Only the nominal limit; funding dates cannot matter"}, {"text": "An automatic rule that the deductible becomes a refund"}]}
explanation={"A receivable can reduce ultimate loss without financing today’s bill."}
/>

An eventual indemnity is a receivable, not cash already in your account. A policy can reduce the ultimate loss while leaving a funding problem during repair and claim assessment.

Stipulate that a valid covered event occurs during the policy term. At the repair date the buyer pays 5,000, using 500 of their own funds and borrowing the promised 4,500. They separately pay the 200 premium. The insurer pays the nominal 4,500 exactly one year later; assume no default. The bridge loan charges an explicitly chosen 5% annual effective rate, with no fees. Interest is $4500(1.05-1)=225$. Repaying the loan needs $4500+225=4725$, so the buyer's total premium, retained loss and financing cost is $200+500+225=925$.

At the repair date, the present value of the future insurer payment at that same stipulated 5% rate is $4500/1.05=4285.714286$ EUR. This valuation calculation and the 225 financing bill are different objects. Do not subtract a discounted receipt from a later nominal bill without first choosing a common date.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="timing" title="Move the collection date while keeping the promise fixed" />

A two-year delay in this model leaves the nominal claim unchanged but produces interest $4500(1.05^2-1)=461.25$ and total cost 1,161.25. Actual claim timing, interest rights and funding terms depend on the contract and law; our numbers prescribe none of them.

### Check both eligibility and liquidity

First identify when the event must occur, how the contract treats notification and what evidence is required; then map payment dates. The EU non-life [insurance product information document described by EIOPA](https://www.eiopa.europa.eu/browse/regulation-and-policy/insurance-distribution-directive-idd_en) summarises key cover, exclusions and obligations. It accompanies fuller documents; the summary does not replace the complete wording. A known valid claim can still arrive too late to pay today's bill.
