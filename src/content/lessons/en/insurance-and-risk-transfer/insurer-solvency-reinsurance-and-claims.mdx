A policy promises payment. The insurer must estimate that obligation, hold resources against it and turn those resources into cash when claims fall due. Reinsurance can change its risk exposure without replacing the original promise.

Amounts in this lesson's balance and reinsurance examples are **thousands of EUR**. They are simplified current-value models with stated zero omitted costs, not a full accounting standard or a regulatory capital calculation.

## Distinguish an estimated liability from available capital

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"A claim estimate rises before any cash is paid. What can change immediately?"}
options={[{"text": "Liabilities rise and equity falls under the same asset values", "correct": true}, {"text": "Cash must rise by the same amount as the estimate"}, {"text": "A reserve creates a new asset equal to the obligation"}]}
explanation={"An estimate of an obligation is a liability, not a funding asset."}
/>

An **asset** is a resource the insurer owns or a receivable it recognises; a **liability** is an obligation. A claim **reserve** or provision estimates an obligation from incurred claims, including amounts not yet paid and potentially claims incurred but not yet reported. It is not an extra asset or necessarily cash in a separate box.

Start with assets of 120 and estimated liabilities of 100. **Equity**, the residual under these valuations, is $120-100=20$. Suppose new information raises estimated claim obligations by 15 without any payment yet. Liabilities become 115 and equity becomes $120-115=5$. If asset values also fall by 10, assets become 110 and equity $110-115=-5$.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="solvency" title="Change asset value after revising claim estimates" />

Recognising the additional claim estimate changes the balance before cash leaves. Paying an already recognised 10 claim then reduces both assets and liabilities by 10; it does not create a second 10 expense in this simplified unchanged-estimate model. Estimates can subsequently change, and an apparent surplus can disappear.

Regulatory solvency capital uses a defined valuation and risk framework; it is not always the same as this book residual. Positive equity alone is not proof of compliance or certain payment. Protection and resolution arrangements depend on jurisdiction, product and legal status; no universal repayment guarantee follows.

### Reconcile valuations and obligations

Use one date, currency and valuation basis when reading the balance. Ask whether reserves include the relevant claims and how assets may behave when those claims increase. The [NAIC definitions of loss reserves](https://content.naic.org/glossary-insurance-terms) distinguish an estimated liability from payment already made.

## Keep the original policy and reinsurance ledgers separate

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"The reinsurer fails to pay its ordinary recovery. Does that erase the valid original claim?"}
options={[{"text": "No; original insurer liability remains under this contract", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; the policyholder automatically bears all recovery default"}, {"text": "Only if a premium has ever been paid to the reinsurer"}]}
explanation={"Ordinary indemnity reinsurance is not a novation of the original policy."}
/>

**Reinsurance** is cover purchased by an insurer from another insurer, the **reinsurer**. The original insurer is the **cedent**. A proportional contract shares specified premiums and losses; an **excess-of-loss layer** pays a stated part above an attachment point, up to a layer limit. Neither name alone supplies all its terms.

The original insurer owes a valid claim of 100. Stipulate reinsurance paying up to 50 of this claim's loss above 30:

$$J(L)=\min\bigl(\max(L-30,0),50\bigr).$$

At $L=100$, recovery promised is $\min(70,50)=50$. If collected in full, net claim cash outflow is $100-50=50$. The separate reinsurance premium is 8, so claim-minus-recovery-plus-premium is 58. At loss 60 the promised recovery would be 30, not 50.

If only half of the valid 50 recovery is actually collected in the stated stress, cash received is 25, net claim funding is 75 and including the premium gives 83. The original valid policyholder claim is still 100. A recoverable is not collected cash; a payment delay can create a temporary gap even without an ultimate credit loss.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="reinsurance" title="Keep original claim and collected reinsurance separate" />

The [IAIS reinsurance principles](https://www.iais.org/icp-online-tool/) distinguish this indemnity from a legal transfer of the original policy: ordinary reinsurance does not discharge the cedent's underlying policyholder liability. Our example has no novation, direct-payment exception or other restructuring. Counterparty credit, concentration, collateral and payment terms still matter.

### Name which parties share the risk

Do not confuse reinsurance with several insurers directly sharing the original cover, or with the buyer retaining a percentage. In Spain, **coaseguro** can refer to the agreed distribution of shares among insurers; [article 33 of the Insurance Contract Act](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1980-22501) provides a jurisdiction-specific framework. In our numerical direct-sharing illustration, two insurers owe 60 and 40 of a valid 100 claim under explicit 60%/40% terms; the insured's deductible is separate. Other jurisdictions and contracts require their own analysis.

## Test cash due today as well as eventual asset value

<MCQ client:visible pretest
question={"Book assets exceed liabilities, but the due claim exceeds today’s cash. What needs testing?"}
options={[{"text": "Whether assets or receivables become cash before the deadline", "correct": true}, {"text": "Only the residual equity; payment timing cannot cause a gap"}, {"text": "Whether the unpaid amount can simply be deleted from liabilities"}]}
explanation={"Liquidity is dated cash capacity, distinct from a book residual."}
/>

**Solvency** concerns the capacity to meet obligations under a stated valuation and legal framework. **Liquidity** concerns obtaining cash on time. An asset can have positive value but be impossible to sell or collect before a payment deadline.

Consider a fresh, independent balance: cash 20 and other assets 100, liabilities 100, hence book equity 20. Of those liabilities, a claim 80 is due today and 20 later. Stipulate that only 40% of the other assets can be converted to cash today at book value, with no fees or haircut. Available cash is $20+0.4(100)=60$; today's claim has a funding gap $80-60=20$.

Paying the available 60 leaves assets $120-60=60$ and liabilities $100-60=40$: the unpaid 20 claim plus the 20 later obligation. Equity remains 20. The unpaid amount does not disappear just because cash ran out. If 60% of assets can be converted instead, cash reaches 80 and the due claim can be paid in full; remaining assets 40, liabilities 20 and equity 20.

<InsuranceRiskLab client:visible mode="liquidity" title="Convert assets in time to meet a due claim" />

This construction isolates timing. It assumes the unconverted assets keep book value; it does not guarantee that forced-sale prices would equal it. Selling at a discount would introduce an additional loss and could turn a liquidity problem into a capital problem. A positive book balance is not permission to miss a valid claim deadline.

### Date both sides of the funding plan

Match claim payment dates to cash, realisable assets and reinsurance collection dates. Stress late recoveries separately from permanent losses. The [NAIC reinsurance overview](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/reinsurance) explains its role in risk transfer; the primary contract, counterparty and liquidity still need their own ledgers.
