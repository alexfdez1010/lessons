Two portfolios can experience exactly the same annual returns and finish with different assets after the same requested spending. The missing detail is **when cash leaves relative to each return**. A retirement withdrawal policy is a dated rule, not just a percentage printed beside an average return.

Each example is a finite, invented, unlevered portfolio. Returns are assigned paths, not forecasts; there is no fee, tax or borrowing unless stated. The [SEC's retirement toolkit](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/retirement-toolkit/managing-lifetime-income) identifies investment and longevity risk. The short paths here isolate mechanisms; they cannot estimate a universal safe withdrawal rate.

## Let withdrawals reveal return-sequence risk

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"Start at 100,000. With no external cash flows, do +20% then −20% and the reversed order finish differently?"} options={[{"text": "No; both finish at 96,000", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; the positive year must come first"}, {"text": "Both finish at 100,000 because the mean is zero"}]} explanation={"100,000 × 1.2 × 0.8 = 96,000 in either order. A zero arithmetic average does not imply zero compound return."} />

**Sequence-of-returns risk** is sensitivity to the ordering of investment outcomes when the account also receives or pays cash. Without flows, the same multiplicative return factors commute. With fixed withdrawals, the cash is removed from different investment bases.

For an affordable fixed year-end withdrawal W, the recurrence is A_t = A_(t−1)(1 + r_t) − W. With W = 10,000, the two paths are:

- Order A: year 1 gives 100,000 × 1.20 − 10,000 = **110,000**; year 2 gives 110,000 × 0.80 − 10,000 = **78,000**.
- Reversed B: year 1 gives 100,000 × 0.80 − 10,000 = **70,000**; year 2 gives 70,000 × 1.20 − 10,000 = **74,000**.

Both paid the same **20,000 nominal cash**, but B has 4,000 fewer final assets. Algebra for two fully affordable withdrawals gives A₂ − B₂ = W(r₁ − r₂) = 10,000[0.20 − (−0.20)] = **4,000**. The first-year return affects the capital from which later cash is removed.

**Pitfall:** comparing only the arithmetic mean return, here zero, or asserting that an early loss is harmless because the next percentage gain is equal. A 20% gain on 70,000 is 14,000; it is not 20,000.

### When to use it

To test a specified spending rule against several plausible dated paths. Different contributions, withdrawals or asset exposures change the result. This two-year comparison does not prove that any fixed spending rate works for every longer path.

## Compare payment dates together with ending assets

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"At a fixed 5% return, a portfolio with end-of-year withdrawals leaves more final assets than one paying at the start. Has it necessarily provided more value?"} hint={"The household receives the starting payments earlier."} text={"No. Compare both the paid cash and final assets at one chosen valuation date."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

A **beginning-of-year** payment leaves before that year's return; an **end-of-year** payment leaves after it. Dates alter both investment exposure and when the household can use the cash.

Separate example: start 100,000, earn an assigned 5% each year and pay 10,000 twice. Beginning payments occur at t = 0 and 1. First year: (100,000 − 10,000)1.05 = **94,500**. Second: (94,500 − 10,000)1.05 = **88,725**. End payments at t = 1 and 2 instead give 100,000(1.05) − 10,000 = **95,000**, then 95,000(1.05) − 10,000 = **89,750**.

The 1,025 final-asset difference comes with different cash dates. At a **chosen matching 5% valuation factor**, the initial-value identities are:

$$
10{,}000+\frac{10{,}000}{1.05}+\frac{88{,}725}{1.05^2}=100{,}000,
$$

$$
\frac{10{,}000}{1.05}+\frac{10{,}000+89{,}750}{1.05^2}=100{,}000.
$$

Equal value under this factor is an accounting comparison for this deterministic example, not proof of household indifference, universal reinvestment access or a market price for uncertain assets. An earlier bill may make the earlier cash essential.

### When to use it

When a benefit date, withdrawal schedule or payment frequency changes. Keep dates and actual cash in the comparison; do not rank options by a final balance while ignoring what was already paid out. The chart's vertical drops occur at the actual cash dates.

## Keep an unmet request outside the portfolio balance

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"Assets of 10,000 lose 50% before a year-end request of 10,000. With no credit line, what can be paid?"} options={[{"text": "5,000, leaving 5,000 of the request unmet", "correct": true}, {"text": "10,000 and a negative 5,000 portfolio"}, {"text": "Nothing because the request cannot be met in full"}]} explanation={"The model allows partial cash payment limited to available assets; it does not create a loan."} />

A forecast that subtracts every requested withdrawal can quietly become a borrowing model. For the stated unlevered account, define available assets V_t = A_(t−1)(1 + r_t), request W_t, paid cash P_t and unfulfilled request U_t:

$$
P_t=\min(V_t,W_t),\qquad U_t=W_t-P_t,\qquad A_t=V_t-P_t\geq0.
$$

Assume nonnegative requests and r_t ≥ −100%, with no contributions, tax, costs or credit. Initial 10,000 loses 50%, so V₁ = **5,000**. The request is 10,000: P₁ = **5,000**, U₁ = **5,000**, A₁ = **0**. A subsequent +20% on zero leaves zero. The next 10,000 request is entirely unpaid, giving total cash actually paid **5,000** and total requests unmet **15,000**.

The unpaid request measures a spending or withdrawal shortfall in this model. It does not automatically create an enforceable loan: a desired holiday never purchased and an overdue contractual bill are different obligations. Any actual borrowing, unpaid debt interest or external rescue cash must be modeled explicitly.

**Pitfall:** plotting negative portfolio assets and continuing to multiply them by investment returns, while describing the account as unlevered. That silently assigns a fictitious financing arrangement and can even make losses on negative wealth look beneficial.

### When to use it

For stress paths, large costs and account exhaustion. Report actual consumption and unmet needs alongside the asset balance. A zero balance and a zero requested withdrawal have very different implications for a household.

## Distinguish an affordable policy from a funded living standard

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"If a rule withdraws 10% of available assets, can it avoid an unaffordable request and still miss a 10,000 spending target?"} hint={"The request itself shrinks when the account shrinks."} text={"Yes. Affordability of the policy-defined request is different from financing the original consumption target."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

A **current-asset percentage policy** sets its own request as W_t = wV_t, where V_t is the post-return balance and 0 ≤ w ≤ 1. Under our no-cost model it can always pay that request. It does not guarantee a fixed real or nominal living standard; after a total loss its affordable request is zero.

Start with 100,000 and use returns −20%, then +20%, requesting 10% of current post-return assets. Year 1: available 80,000, request/paid **8,000**, close **72,000**. Year 2: available 72,000 × 1.2 = **86,400**, request/paid **8,640**, close **77,760**. Every policy request was paid, so **unfulfilled withdrawal requests = 0**.

If the separate target is 10,000 each year, however, the target gaps are max(0, 10,000 − 8,000) = **2,000** and max(0, 10,000 − 8,640) = **1,360**, total **3,360**. Ending assets exceed the fixed-request path's 74,000 partly because less was consumed. A higher ending balance is not evidence that the original target was met.

A **safe withdrawal rate** claim would need a definition of success, horizon, returns, spending flexibility, costs, inflation, tax and uncertainty. Neither two invented paths nor a percentage that mechanically stays affordable proves such a universal number.

### When to use it

When comparing flexibility rules. Track three separate quantities: policy request, cash paid and the household target. If a policy pays above the target, record that surplus separately instead of treating it as required consumption.
