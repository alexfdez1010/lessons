A ten-payment financial annuity has a known last payment date. A life-contingent income promise pays according to survival and contract terms. Replacing one with the other because an average is ten loses the very uncertainty the contract may be designed to address.

The [ONS explanation of period and cohort life expectancy](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/lifeexpectancies/methodologies/periodandcohortlifeexpectancyexplained) distinguishes a population mean, mortality assumptions and projections. We use **no real mortality table** below. Our deliberately fictional law isolates payment dates and contract accounting; it is not a personal longevity prediction.

## A mean payment count is not a last payment date

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"A fictional person survives either 5 or 15 annual payment dates, each with probability 50%. If the mean is 10, is planning only ten dates certain to cover the path?"} options={[{"text": "No; half the paths still need a payment after date 10", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; the average is the right endpoint"}, {"text": "Yes; nobody can exceed the mean"}]} explanation={"E[N] = 0.5 × 5 + 0.5 × 15 = 10, but P(N > 10) = 50%."} />

Let **N count the annual end-of-year payment dates survived**. N = 5 means alive for payments at dates 1–5 and not alive for date 6. We do not specify the exact death time between those dates. Consequently E[N] is an expected count of payment dates, **not an exact remaining life expectancy measured continuously**.

In the fictional 50/50 law, E[N] = 10. Define s_t = P(N ≥ t), survival at the annual payment date. Then s_t = 1 for dates 1–5, 0.5 for dates 6–15 and 0 after 15. Those zeros are a property of the toy law, not a real maximum age.

At a planning horizon H, distinguish **survival at H**, P(N ≥ H), from **another survived payment date after H**, P(N > H). At H = 5, these are **100%** and **50%** respectively: the short branch receives the fifth payment but not the sixth. At H = 10 both are 50%, yet they remain different events.

Expected counts split as E[N] = E[min(N,H)] + E[max(N − H,0)]. For H = 10, that is **10 = 7.5 + 2.5**. An average shorter than a possible individual path cannot serve as its guaranteed funding endpoint.

### When to use it

When deciding what a planning window omits. Real mortality assumptions require age/cohort/selection and projection scope; population averages do not guarantee one person's duration. Keep the payment-frequency convention visible before using a survival curve.

## Discount expected life cash without inventing an insurance price

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"Do ten certain 10,000 payments and an average of ten life-only payments have the same expected PV at 5%?"} hint={"The dates carrying probability weight need not be the same."} text={"No. Under the stated toy law the life-only expected PV is 73,545.67, while ten certain payments have PV 77,217.35."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

A **certain finite financial annuity** pays at specified dates irrespective of the original person's survival, under its own recipient terms. A **life-only payment** requires survival at the payment date. Assuming performance and a chosen constant annual discount r, its expected discounted cash is:

$$
E[PV]=\sum_{t\geq1}\frac{B_t s_t}{(1+r)^t}.
$$

For constant B = 10,000, r = 5% and the full toy law, use a_n = Σ from t = 1 to n of 1.05^(−t). Values are 10,000a₅ = **43,294.77** and 10,000a₁₅ = **103,796.58**. Weight the complete branches: 0.5 × 43,294.766706… + 0.5 × 103,796.580382… = **73,545.67**. Ten certain payments instead give 10,000a₁₀ = **77,217.35**. Equal expected count does not put cash at equal dates.

If we truncate the life-only sum at date 10, it is **60,256.06**. The full-law omitted expected PV is **13,289.62**. The truncation is not a whole-life value. More generally a survival curve ending with positive survival can leave an unknown tail; our toy law explicitly supplies that tail through date 15.

**Pitfall:** treating expected PV as a sufficient reserve for one person. The long branch needs PV 103,796.58 under this discount, above the average 73,545.67. Nor is the average an insurer's market quotation: pricing can include different mortality, discounting, risk, expenses, capital and contractual features.

### When to use it

To understand which conditional cash flows a valuation includes. Label the chosen law and discount, the observation window and any omitted tail. Compare an actual insurance quote only after identifying its own coverage and terms.

## A deferred life payment still requires survival on its date

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"Keep the toy law but let life-only payments begin at date 6. Does the N = 5 branch receive ten later payments?"} options={[{"text": "No; it has not survived the first eligible payment date", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; deferral just shifts ten certain payments"}, {"text": "Yes; the average payment count is ten"}]} explanation={"The timing condition and survival condition both apply."} />

**Deferral** moves the first possible payment date. For certain payments this shifts a specified count. For life-contingent payments it can also remove branches that do not survive to that date.

Set B = 10,000, first possible payment at t = 6 and the same N = 5 or 15 law. The short branch receives **zero**. The long branch receives ten payments at dates **6–15**. Therefore:

$$
E[PV_{deferred}]=0.5\sum_{t=6}^{15}\frac{10{,}000}{1.05^t}
=\frac{0.5(77{,}217.349291\ldots)}{1.05^5}
=30{,}250.91.
$$

A separate claim of ten **certain** payments at dates 6–15 has PV **60,501.81**, twice that amount. The 0.5 is not an extra discount rate; it is the probability of meeting the survival condition at those dates. Multiplying by it is appropriate here because the long branch alone supplies every eligible payment.

Do not simply shift the entire original life-only expected cash schedule five years while holding its survival weights in payment-number time. A payment at calendar date 6 requires s₆, not s₁. A contract with refund, a guarantee or another beneficiary after death would have additional cash flows and a different value.

### When to use it

When combining a finite account bridge with income starting later. Date the bridge and the contingent promise separately. A deferred payment can help a specific late-life exposure without paying an earlier bill or solving provider, inflation and liquidity risk.

## Specify beneficiary guarantees and the risk they change

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"If the person receives five life-only payments, must an insurer then pay the other five to a beneficiary?"} hint={"A minimum payment count must be a stated contract feature."} text={"No. Our life-only claim stops; a separate ten-date guarantee adds beneficiary cash at dates 6–10."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

Compare three fictional fully performing contracts with annual cash B = 10,000: **life-only** while alive; **life plus G guaranteed dates**, paying a named beneficiary after death through date G; and **ten certain payments**, paid to the original person or named beneficiary. These recipient rules are stated assumptions, not features of every real product.

For realized N = 5 and G = 10: life-only pays **50,000**; life-plus-guarantee pays 50,000 to the person and **50,000** to the beneficiary; the ten-certain-payment claim does the same on this branch. For N = 15, life-only and life-plus-guarantee each pay **150,000** to the person; the finite claim pays only **100,000**, leaving five living-person payment dates uncovered. Those nominal totals do not substitute for a dated PV comparison.

Under the fixed 50/50 law and 5% discount, the ten-date guarantee adds expected PV 0.5Σ from t = 6 to 10 of 10,000/1.05^t = **16,961.29** to the life-only benchmark. Added coverage is an added claim; a common-price comparison cannot assume it is free.

The [SEC's annuity overview](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/annuities) describes varying contract features and provider claims-paying ability. A promise's longevity protection does not automatically give inflation protection, immediate access or immunity from provider failure. Survivor, guarantee, refund and surrender terms must be read directly.

### When to use it

When comparing household income needs and beneficiary goals. Specify who receives each payment under each event. A certain ten-payment contract and a life promise cover different tails even when one short realized path looks identical.
