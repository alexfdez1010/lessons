“I have a pension” can describe a promised payment, a formula still subject to conditions or a balance whose investment outcome changes. Before asking whether an amount is enough, identify **what the contract actually defines**.

The [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/retirement/typesofplans) distinguishes defined-benefit payment promises from defined-contribution accounts in its U.S./ERISA explanation. Those terms guide the comparison here; the formulas, access rules and prices below are fictional, not a statement of Spanish or U.S. legal entitlement. Provider performance, eligibility, taxation and survivor rights require the applicable terms.

## Read a defined payment as a formula with conditions

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"A fictional plan uses 1.5% × 20 service years × 40,000 annual salary. What does the result measure?"} options={[{"text": "An annual formula payment of 12,000", "correct": true}, {"text": "A current account containing 12,000"}, {"text": "A market price of 800,000"}]} explanation={"0.015 × 20 × 40,000 = 12,000 per year. The units are annual cash, not a stock."} />

A **defined-benefit right** specifies a benefit amount or formula under stated conditions. In our invented plan, the annual nominal payment is:

$$
B=a\,n\,S,
$$
where a is the annual accrual fraction per credited service year, n the credited years and S the annual salary specified by the formula. With a = 0.015, n = 20 and S = 40,000, B = **12,000 per year**, a salary replacement fraction of 12,000/40,000 = **30%**. “Credited” matters: the contract determines which service and salary count. We assume those historical inputs are already established.

The formula alone does not give a tradable asset price or cash available now. It also does not answer the first payment date, whether survival is required, how nominal payments change, who receives money after death or what happens if a provider cannot perform. A contingent promise should not become a certain cash line merely because its formula has a multiplication sign.

The [SEC's lifetime-income explanation](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/retirement-toolkit/managing-lifetime-income) distinguishes who bears investment risk in benefit and contribution arrangements. That distinction is useful without implying every promised benefit is risk-free: funding, issuer, legal and contract-specific conditions still matter.

### When to use it

When converting a benefit statement into a dated cash-flow model. Keep the annual amount separate from any chosen PV estimate and from actual eligibility. More service raises this fictional formula linearly; that is not a rule for every real plan.

## Track an invested account without calling contributions profit

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"An account rises from 100,000 to 108,950 after a 5,000 contribution. Was its investment gain 8,950?"} hint={"First separate the external transfer into the account."} text={"No. Under the stated return and fee order, investment gain after fees is 3,950; the other 5,000 is a contribution."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

A **defined-contribution account** records contributions and investment outcomes rather than defining the same fixed future payment for every market path. A known account balance does not by itself identify a lifetime spending amount.

For our separate account, apply the annual return r first, charge fee f on the assets after that return, then add contribution K at year-end:

$$
A_t=A_{t-1}(1+r_t)(1-f)+K_t.
$$
Starting at 100,000, with r = 5%, f = 1% and K = 5,000: gross gain is 5,000; post-return assets are 105,000; the fee is 105,000 × 0.01 = **1,050**; closing assets are **100,000 × 1.05 × 0.99 + 5,000 = 108,950**. Net investment gain is 5,000 − 1,050 = **3,950**, not 8,950.

With an assigned −20% return instead, the same fee rule charges 80,000 × 1% = 800 and closes at **80,000 − 800 + 5,000 = 84,200**. The contribution does not insure the old balance against loss. Future contributions are also assumptions, not investment returns.

The fee base and timing are contractual choices in this example. Some real products use daily charges, fixed amounts or other bases. The [SEC's fees bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/updated) explains how fees reduce resources available for investment; it does not impose our annual fee order on every product.

### When to use it

To reconcile an account statement or compare contribution, return and cost scenarios. Record each contribution at its actual date; a contribution at the start of a year has more invested time than the end-of-year contribution used here.

## Distinguish indexation from a nominal promise

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"A nominal payment stays at 12,000 while prices rise 2% annually. Does its real amount stay at 12,000?"} options={[{"text": "No; divide each payment by its date-specific price index", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; the promised euro amount is unchanged"}, {"text": "Yes; every benefit is fully indexed"}]} explanation={"A fixed nominal amount loses purchasing power when the assumed price index rises."} />

**Indexation** changes a contractual nominal payment according to an explicit rule. It may use a particular index, a cap, a lag or no increase. A real-spending model must not quietly substitute full inflation protection for those terms.

Let B₀ = 12,000 be the reference annual amount at date zero, contractual annual indexation i, and inflation π. In this unit example the **first payment is at year 1 after one indexation adjustment**:

$$
B_t^{nom}=B_0(1+i)^t,\qquad B_t^{real}=B_0\left(\frac{1+i}{1+\pi}\right)^t.
$$
For a fixed nominal payment i = 0 and π = 2%, the year-3 real amount is 12,000/1.02³ = **11,307.87**. With full matching i = 2%, nominal year-3 cash is **12,734.496**, and real cash is exactly **12,000** under these assumptions. With a fictional 1% annual cap while inflation stays 2%, the year-3 real amount is 12,000(1.01/1.02)³ = **11,650.51**.

A payment described as “indexed” therefore needs its actual rule. Even matching a general index need not match one household's basket. The example holds eligibility, survival and provider performance fixed; those are separate dimensions, not solved by deflation.

### When to use it

When comparing fixed, capped and uncapped payment schedules in common purchasing power. State whether the first adjustment occurs before the first payment. Applying one extra year's indexation changes every comparison.

## Convert account cash into a claim only once

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"A 100,000 account buys an income claim at a given price of 40,000. Can the balance sheet retain the full 100,000 and add the new 40,000 claim?"} hint={"The purchase must have a funding entry."} text={"No. With no fee, 60,000 account cash plus the claim at its 40,000 transaction price totals 100,000."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

Buying a payment claim changes the **form** of resources. It can trade accessible account cash for a stream with different timing, survival or beneficiary conditions. The transaction cannot create both the spent cash and the purchased right.

Our fictional purchase is all-or-nothing. Opening account A = 100,000; given claim price P = 40,000; fee F = 0. Remaining account is A − P − F = **60,000**. The new claim is entered at the given transaction price **40,000**. Total at those prices is **100,000**, before subsequent changes. With fee 1,000, the account would be 59,000 and total assets **99,000**. The fee consumes resources; it is not another purchased claim.

If A is only 30,000 while P + F = 40,000, our stated transaction does **not execute**. The account remains 30,000, no claim or fee is created and the unfunded request is recorded. A real partial purchase, cancellation or loan would need different explicit terms.

An **annuity insurance contract** can provide payments under its specified terms; the [SEC's overview](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/annuities) emphasizes contract features and the insurer's claims-paying ability. Here P is a given price, not one inferred from a simple annuity formula. Later we distinguish a financial annuity with a fixed payment count from a life-contingent promise.

### When to use it

When a household changes the mix of invested balances and payment rights. Compare liquidity, indexation, provider risk and beneficiary conditions as well as the annual amount. A high first payment alone does not establish a better contract.
