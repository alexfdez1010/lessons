A retirement plan needs a calendar before it needs a large number. A household may own valuable assets, expect future wages and still lack the cash for a bill due today. Start by asking **what amount, in which purchasing power, at what date, and available under which conditions?**

This intermediate course builds on [money and value](/money-and-value), [time value of money](/money-time-value) and [investing basics](/investing-basics). All numerical households, contracts and tax rules here are invented. Euros are the common calculation unit. Four-year windows teach the accounting; they do not cover a whole life.

## Turn a real spending target into dated cash

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"A household wants the purchasing power of 30,000 euros today at the end of next year. With assumed 2% inflation, what nominal cash does it need?"} options={[{"text": "30,600", "correct": true}, {"text": "30,000"}, {"text": "29,400"}]} explanation={"30,000 × 1.02 = 30,600. The target is constant in real units, not nominal euros."} />

**Nominal** money is the euro amount paid on its actual date. **Real** money expresses purchasing power in prices of a stated reference date. Set the price index today to 1. With constant assumed annual inflation π, its value at year t is (1 + π)^t. A real annual target C in today's euros therefore needs:

$$
C_t^{nom}=C(1+\pi)^t,\qquad C_t^{real}=\frac{C_t^{nom}}{(1+\pi)^t}.
$$
For C = 30,000 and π = 2%, end-of-year cash is **30,600; 31,212; 31,836.24; 32,472.9648** at years 1–4. The second value is 30,600 × 1.02, not 30,000 + 600 again. Displays round to cents; calculations keep the unrounded value.

This is a scenario price index for a household basket. Actual personal spending categories can change, and their prices can differ from a headline inflation index. A mortgage payment fixed in nominal money and a food budget targeted in real money need separate rows.

**Pitfall:** treating deflation as compensation for investment risk. Dividing by the price index changes the unit of purchasing power. It does not make a risky future payment certain or determine its present market price.

### When to use it

When comparing a future payment with a present living-cost target. Specify the reference date and the first payment date before applying a power. Here the first payment is at year 1, after one year's inflation.

## Separate wages, benefits and withdrawals on the calendar

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"Does a 12,000 annual benefit starting in year 3 fill a year-1 cash gap?"} hint={"A promise and a current payment have different dates."} text={"It adds no year-1 cash under these terms. A bridge needs existing liquid assets, a changed cash schedule or an explicitly available financing source."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

An **income cash flow** is money entering at a date; a **balance** is the amount held at a date. A quoted salary is not automatically take-home cash. We use assigned **net** labor income of 20,000 at the ends of years 1 and 2 and a net nominal benefit of 12,000 at the ends of years 3 and 4. “Net” means the example has already accounted for any deductions; we do not deduct them again.

Against the previous spending schedule, annual net flows are **−10,600; −11,212; −19,836.24; −20,472.9648**. For year 3: 0 labor + 12,000 benefit − 31,836.24 spending = −19,836.24. A negative flow means the listed income does not fund that year's entire spending. It does not by itself identify a loan or tell us the available portfolio balance.

An account withdrawal can provide cash for spending, but is also a reduction in the account. Counting the account's full value as resources and then adding all its future withdrawals as an independent asset counts the same resources twice. Investment returns, external contributions and actual spending must each have their own entry.

**Pitfall:** a yearly surplus can hide a February bill paid before a December benefit arrives. Annual snapshots omit intra-year liquidity. Refine the calendar to months or actual dates when timing controls feasibility.

### When to use it

To identify a transition between work and retirement, a benefit waiting period or a one-off cost. A calendar reveals which period needs funding before a valuation compresses several years into one number.

## Value resources and planned spending at one date

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"Can 20,000 due today be added directly to 20,000 arriving in four years to establish a date-zero value?"} options={[{"text": "Only after stating how the future amount is valued", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; the euro labels make dates irrelevant"}, {"text": "No future cash may ever be considered"}]} explanation={"The date-zero comparison needs a discount assumption; future cash may also be conditional or nontradeable."} />

A **lifetime balance sheet** broadens current financial assets and debts to include explicitly modeled future resources and spending. It is a planning comparison, not necessarily a set of saleable assets. Future earning capacity, sometimes called **human capital**, depends on continued work, health and wages. A discount rate alone does not settle those contingencies.

For this four-year scenario, current financial assets A = 100,000 and debt due now D = 20,000 give 80,000 net financial assets. Choose a nominal annual discount r = 5% and the cash schedule above:

$$
M=A-D+\sum_{t=1}^{4}\frac{L_t+B_t-C_t^{nom}}{(1+r)^t}.
$$
Here M is the **scenario funding margin** at zero, not an investment return. Labor PV is 20,000/1.05 + 20,000/1.05² = **37,188.21**. Benefit PV is 12,000/1.05³ + 12,000/1.05⁴ = **20,238.48**. Spending PV is Σ[30,000(1.02)^t/1.05^t] = **111,669.99**. Thus M = 80,000 + 37,188.21 + 20,238.48 − 111,669.99 = **25,756.70**, rounding only the displayed result.

That positive margin belongs to the assumed four-year window. It says nothing about year 5 onward, provider failure or a changed wage path. Carrying the unrounded margin at the chosen 5% to year 4 gives a value-equivalent 31,307.43; it does not automatically establish a funded investment account earning that rate.

### When to use it

To compare consistent scenarios, question an input or locate a resource/spending mismatch. Keep conditional income, nontradeability and horizon limits visible. Do not read a chosen discount calculation as a proof that a household can borrow against every future euro.

## Test liquidity separately from net resources

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"There is 10,000 cash, a restricted account worth 90,000 and a bill of 20,000 due now. Does future-income PV of 50,000 pay the missing 10,000?"} hint={"The fictional account cannot be accessed today and no lender is offered."} text={"No. Pay 10,000 cash and record the remaining 10,000 bill as unpaid. Future-income PV is not spendable cash."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

**Liquidity** is the ability to meet a payment when due using available cash or resources that can actually be converted on acceptable terms. **Solvency/net resources** asks a broader question about assets and obligations. A large positive resource valuation can coexist with a payment problem today.

In the separate example, opening financial assets are 10,000 cash + 90,000 restricted assets. The current bill is a 20,000 liability. Net financial assets are 10,000 + 90,000 − 20,000 = **80,000**. The fictional contract forbids accessing the restricted account today; we assume no asset sale or credit line.

Actual payment = min(10,000, 20,000) = **10,000**. After paying, cash is zero, restricted assets remain 90,000 and the unpaid liability is 10,000. Net financial assets remain **90,000 − 10,000 = 80,000**. Paying an already recognized liability reduces cash and liability together; it does not erase the missing payment or create a new investment loss.

Adding given scenario future-income PV of 50,000 gives 130,000 broader scenario resources, before any other future spending. That is a different measure, not cash available for today's bill. Real access restrictions, settlement delays and borrowing terms need their own contractual evidence; the restriction here is purely invented.

### When to use it

Alongside the resource balance, especially around retirement transitions or large one-off bills. Record available cash, required dates and any explicit financing separately. A forecast cannot settle a bill by being optimistic.
