A scenario comparison should show what people actually receive and what assets remain. “More money at the end” can simply mean less consumption was funded. Build the cash ledger first, then compare outcomes at a common date and in common purchasing-power units.

This lesson combines the preceding mechanisms in **independent three-year fictional scenarios**. The flat withholding is invented, not tax guidance for any jurisdiction. Returns, inflation and costs are assigned assumptions, not estimates or recommendations. A three-year stress check cannot establish whole-lifetime adequacy.

## Gross up a net target using the rule actually stated

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"A fictional rule withholds 20% of every gross account withdrawal. What gross withdrawal supplies a net 10,000 if assets are sufficient?"} options={[{"text": "12,500", "correct": true}, {"text": "12,000"}, {"text": "10,000"}]} explanation={"10,000 / (1 − 0.20) = 12,500. Adding 20% to the net target is insufficient."} />

**Gross** is the amount removed from the account before the stated withholding; **net** is cash reaching the household afterward. Let the fictional tax fraction on gross withdrawals be τ, with 0 ≤ τ < 1. For net request C:

$$
G_{required}=\frac{C}{1-\tau},\quad G_{paid}=\min(A_{available},G_{required}),
\quad C_{paid}=G_{paid}(1-\tau).
$$

For C = 10,000 and τ = 20%, required gross is **12,500**, withholding **2,500** and net paid **10,000**. A 12,000 withdrawal pays only 12,000 × 0.8 = **9,600**. The percentage applies to a different base from the net target.

With only 10,000 assets available, actual gross is 10,000, withholding 2,000, actual net **8,000**, unpaid net request **2,000** and remaining account **0**. Do not subtract a fictitious 12,500 from an account that holds 10,000.

This model taxes **gross account withdrawals only**. It does not separately tax the entire balance, every investment gain or a benefit already supplied net. Real tax systems can use other bases, allowances, brackets and dates; importing those details would require a jurisdiction, product and date. Here the explicit fictional rule is enough to teach gross versus net.

### When to use it

When matching a net spending gap with a source quoted before deductions. Identify the taxable base before doing arithmetic, then check available funding. A net benefit must not be taxed a second time merely because it enters the same ledger.

## Combine returns, costs and purchasing power multiplicatively

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"Is a 4% gross return minus a 1% post-return fee minus 2% inflation exactly a 1% real return?"} hint={"Apply each factor to its specified base before deflating."} text={"No. 1.04 × 0.99 / 1.02 − 1 = approximately 0.941176%, not exactly 1%."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

In the no-flow comparison, one annual return is followed by a fee fraction f of the post-return assets. The nominal net factor is (1 + r)(1 − f). Deflating by inflation π gives:

$$
1+r_{real,net}=\frac{(1+r)(1-f)}{1+\pi}.
$$

From 100,000, a 4% gross return creates 104,000; the 1% post-return fee is **1,040**; nominal ending assets are **102,960**. With 2% inflation, real ending assets are 102,960/1.02 = **100,941.17647…**, giving real return **0.94117647…%**. The simple subtraction 4% − 1% − 2% = 1% is only an approximation.

Over three identical years with no flows, nominal assets are 100,000(1.04 × 0.99)³. Real assets are 100,000[(1.04 × 0.99)/1.02]³. The two curves use different purchasing-power units even though they describe the same resources. Never add nominal and real balances as if they were separate accounts.

The [SEC's fee bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/updated) explains why costs reduce amounts left to earn returns. Our fee base is explicitly post-return assets; another schedule requires a different calculation. Taxes on withdrawals belong to the cash ledger, not an automatic second charge on this no-withdrawal comparison.

### When to use it

For a clean unit and cost check before introducing spending. Keep the compounding interval consistent. An annual percentage cannot simply be inserted as a monthly charge without a stated conversion and convention.

## Close the annual resource and spending ledger

<MCQ client:visible pretest question={"A 4,000 benefit is already net. The year-1 nominal spending target is 10,200. What net gap must the account finance?"} options={[{"text": "6,200", "correct": true}, {"text": "10,200"}, {"text": "7,000"}]} explanation={"10,200 − 4,000 = 6,200. Do not tax the already-net benefit again."} />

Both baseline and alternative begin with 100,000 invested assets, real annual spending target 10,000 and **fixed nominal already-net benefit 4,000 at each year-end**. The baseline assigns gross returns 4%/4%/4%, inflation 2%, post-return fee 1%, fictional gross-withdrawal withholding 20% and no contingency.

Each year's order is **return → fee on post-return assets → receive net benefit → fund year-end spending**. Year 1: 100,000 × 1.04 = 104,000; fee = **1,040**; assets available = **102,960**. Spending target = 10,000 × 1.02 = **10,200**. Net gap = 10,200 − 4,000 = **6,200**. Gross withdrawal = 6,200/0.8 = **7,750**, withholding **1,550**, closing assets **95,210**.

Year 2: 95,210 × 1.04 = 99,018.40; fee 990.184; spending 10,404; net gap 6,404; gross withdrawal 8,005; closing **90,023.216**. Year 3: post-return assets 93,624.14464, fee 936.2414464, spending 10,612.08, gross withdrawal 8,265.10; close **84,422.8031936 nominal**, or **79,553.49 real** after dividing by 1.02³.

The simulator preserves unrounded intermediate values and rounds displays. It caps gross withdrawals at available assets and reports any unpaid spending. If a net benefit ever exceeds the desired spending in another scenario with these rules, its surplus enters the account at year-end; it is not discarded or charged an earlier fee retroactively.

### When to use it

To find where a scenario fails and verify each cash source has one use. Compare the actual spending ledger with asset changes. A lower final account may reflect paid consumption, costs or investment losses; those mechanisms need separate entries.

## Compare contingencies and outcomes on a common basis

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt={"A scenario leaves more assets but fails to pay a large medical-cost target. Is the higher balance enough to call it better?"} hint={"Compare what was paid, what remains unpaid and the final resources."} text={"No. Show actual spending and unmet needs separately at a common date; a rank based only on assets can reward skipped consumption."} buttonLabel={"Show the reasoning"} />

A **contingency scenario** assigns an additional event and its date; it is not automatically an expected event with known probability. Add a fictional 5,000 **real** cost in year 2. At 2% inflation it becomes 5,000 × 1.02² = **5,202 nominal**, on top of regular 10,404 spending. With the fixed net benefit 4,000, the net gap is **11,606**. At 20% fictional withholding the required gross withdrawal is **14,507.50**. The additional gross resource use from that cost is 5,202/0.8 = **6,502.50**, if affordable.

A common **real annual discount** ρ values flows in date-zero purchasing power. Match each scenario's assumed inflation with nominal discount r_n by 1 + r_n = (1 + ρ)(1 + π). For ρ = 1% and π = 2%, r_n = **3.02%**. For any dated nominal cash C_t:

$$
\frac{C_t}{(1+r_n)^t}=\frac{C_t/(1+\pi)^t}{(1+\rho)^t}.
$$

This equality checks units; it does not price uncertain outcomes. The table reports PV of **actual net spending**, PV of **unmet spending** and PV of **ending assets** separately. Unmet spending is not secretly paid or added to assets. Fees and taxes are resource uses, not extra household consumption. If desired, an explicit objective can weigh these outcomes; the simulator does not impose one universal preference.

### When to use it

To compare inflation, costs, a changed year-2 return and dated contingencies fairly. Change one input to isolate an effect, then combine adverse inputs to test interaction. A short set of assumed paths provides a structured question list, not probabilities, a complete lifetime forecast or a personalized recommendation.
