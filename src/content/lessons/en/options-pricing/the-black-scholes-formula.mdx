A call becomes more valuable while its modeled chance of finishing in the money falls. That is possible because a probability counts how often a payoff is positive; a price also depends on how large those positive payoffs are and how they are valued today. Black–Scholes gives us a model in which we can calculate all three quantities separately.

The binomial lesson priced a payoff by trading the underlying asset and cash. The **Black–Scholes–Merton (BSM) model** develops that replication idea in continuous time. Its European formula is exact within its assumptions; numerical evaluation has finite precision, and an actual market premium can differ. A **premium** is the price paid for the option today, before any later exercise payment.

## Name the contract and match the input conventions

A **European call** pays $\max(S_T-K,0)$ at expiry; a **European put** pays $\max(K-S_T,0)$. Here Sₜ is the underlying's price at the stated date, K the contractual exercise price (**strike**) and T the remaining time. European exercise means the holder can exercise only at expiry. The formulas below price **one option share**, with no contract multiplier hidden in the result.

Use these six inputs consistently:

For example, a **5% continuous annual rate** grows 100 to $100e^{0.05}=105.127110$ after a year. Its effective annual rate is $e^{0.05}-1=0.05127110$, or 5.127110%. If the input is instead **5% effective**, its equivalent continuous rate is $\ln(1.05)=0.048790164$. Our worked option example uses r = 0.05 **continuous**, q = 0 and T = 1 year. If choosing a 365-day year convention, 90 days means $T=90/365$, while σ and r remain annualized.

**Which inputs are known?** Strike and exercise terms are contractual; spot is observed subject to execution and timing. A financing curve needs currency, maturity and compounding choices. Future dividends can be uncertain; the constant q representation is itself an assumption. Future volatility is unknown, but the σ entered into BSM can be an estimate, a scenario or an **implied volatility**, the model value fitted to a chosen market option quote. Fitting a quote does not establish a physical volatility forecast.

The model further assumes continuous frictionless trading and financing, the ability to implement the required hedge, no jumps, and a geometric Brownian-motion price process with constant σ. Its finite-horizon **log returns** are normal; its terminal prices are lognormal. A finite **simple return**, $S_T/S_0-1$, is a shifted lognormal variable, rather than an exactly normal variable. [CFA Institute's valuation curriculum](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/valuation-contingent-claims) connects these assumptions to replication. [MathWorks' official European pricing documentation](https://www.mathworks.com/help/finance/blsprice.html) states the continuous-rate and matched-time-unit conventions.

**Pitfall:** reading a government bond's quoted yield, an annual effective deposit rate or a regulatory APR and inserting the number as r without checking its convention. Likewise, announced cash dividends are not automatically a known constant proportional yield q.

### When to use this setup

Before comparing a model price with a quote or with another calculator. Match exercise style, settlement/payoff convention, spot, curve, dividends, time convention and volatility input. [OIC distinguishes theoretical model values from market premiums](https://prd-web.optionseducation.org/advancedconcepts/black-scholes-formula); the formula does not promise a price at which a trade will execute. American exercise, discrete dividends, jumps and trading frictions need additional treatment.

## Rebuild the price from exercise-weighted stock and cash

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A call pays the share price minus the strike only when the terminal share price exceeds the strike. Which information is needed to value that payoff?" options={[
 {text:'The values of the exercise-weighted stock and strike-payment pieces under a consistent pricing model',correct:true},
 {text:'Only the probability that exercise pays something positive'},
 {text:'Only today’s stock price, with no strike or discounting'},
]} explanation="Two payoffs can be positive equally often and have very different sizes. The stock received and the strike paid must each be valued with their timing and state dependence." />

Write $N(z)$ for the **standard normal cumulative distribution function**: the probability that a standard normal variable is at or below z. For example, $N(0)=0.5$. For positive σ and T, the continuous-dividend BSM formula is

$$
\begin{aligned}
C&=S_0e^{-qT}N(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}N(d_2),\\
P&=Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2)-S_0e^{-qT}N(-d_1),\\
d_1&=\frac{\ln(S_0/K)+(r-q+\tfrac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T},\qquad
d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt T.
\end{aligned}
$$

The model uses **pricing probabilities**, denoted Q, rather than automatically using real-world frequencies. An **indicator** $I_{\{S_T>K\}}$ equals 1 if the event happens and 0 otherwise. The call payoff decomposes as

$$
(S_T-K)^+=S_TI_{\{S_T>K\}}-KI_{\{S_T>K\}}.
$$

Under the model,

$$
\begin{aligned}
e^{-rT}E^Q[S_TI_{\{S_T>K\}}]&=S_0e^{-qT}N(d_1),\\
e^{-rT}E^Q[KI_{\{S_T>K\}}]&=Ke^{-rT}N(d_2).
\end{aligned}
$$

These are **unconditional, indicator-weighted expectations**, including zero payments outside the exercise event. The stock term is not a conditional average stock price with the exercise probability omitted. High-price outcomes contribute more to the stock receipt than merely counting them would. The [authors' digital-option derivation](https://book.derivative-securities.org/Chapter_BlackScholes.html) develops the same payoff decomposition.

Work through S₀ = K = 100, r = 0.05, q = 0, σ = 0.20 and T = 1:

1. Log-return width: $\sigma\sqrt T=0.20$.
2. $\ln(S_0/K)=0$ and $d_1=(0.05+0.5\times0.20^2)/0.20=0.35$.
3. $d_2=0.35-0.20=0.15$.
4. $N(0.35)=0.6368306512$ and $N(0.15)=0.5596176924$.
5. $e^{-rT}=e^{-0.05}=0.9512294245$.
6. Stock piece: $100(0.6368306512)=63.68306512$.
7. Cash piece: $100(0.9512294245)(0.5596176924)=53.23248155$.
8. Call: $C=63.68306512-53.23248155=10.45058357$.

All figures are currency units per option share, apart from the dimensionless d and N terms. Use full precision internally; rounding the CDF to four decimal places before multiplying can change the final cents.

<BlackScholesLab client:visible view="components" unitsLabel="Currency values per underlying share. Both payoff pieces use the same Q pricing model, exercise indicator and discount conventions. **Pitfall:** calling N(d₁) and N(d₂) the same probability. N(d₂) is the Q exercise probability in this positive-variance European model. N(d₁) is a different normal-CDF term; the stock receipt weights the values of the underlying across the exercise region. Its relationship to delta also needs the dividend factor, which we derive below.

## Keep physical price drift separate from pricing drift

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An investor changes the assumed physical price drift from 5% to 12% per year while holding the quoted spot, strike, rate, dividends, volatility and time fixed. Must the conditional BSM price change?" options={[

 {text:'Yes; the call price must rise by exactly seven currency units'},
 {text:'Yes; physical drift must replace the financing rate everywhere'},
]} explanation="The model price is conditional on its given traded/model inputs. A physical belief changes an outcome forecast. New information can also move real market quotes, so holding those quotes fixed is part of this comparison." />

For a lognormal model, write a terminal price using a standard normal random variable Z:

$$S_T=S_0\exp\left((a-\tfrac12\sigma^2)T+\sigma\sqrt T\,Z\right).$$

The drift **a** must be identified. Under Q, the **ex-dividend price drift is r − q**. Under a separately assumed physical probability law P, call the **capital-gain price drift μ**. In this lesson μ already describes price growth before adding the dividend yield; do not subtract q from it a second time. Some texts define μ as a total-return drift instead, which changes that notation.

For either declared drift a,

$$E[S_T]=S_0e^{aT},\qquad \operatorname{Med}(S_T)=S_0e^{(a-\sigma^2/2)T}.$$

The difference between mean and median comes from the right-skewed lognormal distribution. Neither is automatically today's spot. For S₀ = K = 100, σ = 0.20 and T = 1:

**Pitfall:** treating a Q probability as a frequency forecast, or treating a physical expected payoff discounted at r as automatically the market price. Appropriate state-dependent risk adjustments or pricing weights are part of valuation. Replication removes physical drift from this particular conditional formula; beliefs and preferences can still influence the market inputs taken as given.

A density assigns probability per price interval, a CDF integrates probability and an option payoff has currency units. Keep those objects distinct.

**Exercise is also different from profit.** If the original premium 10.45058357 is borrowed at continuous 5% until year 1, its repayment is $10.45058357e^{0.05}=10.98639645$. Ignoring other costs, the terminal financed profit is $\max(S_T-100,0)-10.98639645$. At Sₜ = 105 the call is in the money but profit is $5-10.98639645=-5.98639645$. Positive profit requires Sₜ above **110.98639645** in this stated financed setup.

## Add dividends to parity and to the hedge ratio

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A regular European call has continuous proportional dividend yield q greater than zero. Which expression gives its spot delta?" options={[
 {text:'exp(−qT) × N(d₁)',correct:true},
 {text:'N(d₂), because the hedge must equal the exercise probability'},
 {text:'N(d₁) for every possible dividend convention'},
]} explanation="Differentiating the q-adjusted price gives exp(−qT)N(d₁). The familiar N(d₁) form is the zero-dividend case." />

The **spot delta** is the derivative of the option price with respect to the current underlying price, holding the other model inputs fixed. In the continuous-dividend model,

$$\Delta_C=e^{-qT}N(d_1),\qquad \Delta_P=e^{-qT}\bigl(N(d_1)-1\bigr).$$

For q = 0 this reduces to N(d₁) for the call and N(d₁) − 1 for the put. With a positive proportional yield the dividend factor matters. [The official delta documentation](https://www.mathworks.com/help/finance/blsdelta.html) treats the yield input as part of the model sensitivity.

Delta is a **local sensitivity**, in shares per option share. A replicating long-call position holds positive delta shares and the corresponding financing position. To offset the stock exposure of a **short** call, buy those shares; to neutralize a **long** call, sell delta shares. For a contract multiplier of 100 and call delta 0.586851, the short-call stock offset is about $100\times0.586851=58.6851$ shares. It is not a promise that 58.6851% of actual contracts exercise. Rebalancing, dividend cash, financing, gaps and costs matter for the hedge's later result.

Put–call parity also carries the dividend factor:

$$C-P=S_0e^{-qT}-Ke^{-rT}.$$

First check the zero-dividend worked example. Its put is

$$P=C-S_0+Ke^{-rT}=10.45058357-100+95.12294245=5.57352602.$$

Now set **q = 2% continuous** and retain S₀ = K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 1. Then $d_1=0.25$, $d_2=0.05$, $N(d_1)=0.5987063257$ and $N(d_2)=0.5199388058$. The prepaid-stock factor gives $S_0e^{-qT}=98.01986733$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
C&=58.68511461-49.45810911=9.22700551,\\
P&=9.22700551-98.01986733+95.12294245=6.33008063,\\
\Delta_C&=e^{-0.02}(0.5987063257)=0.5868511461.
\end{aligned}
$$

The normal term 0.598706, spot delta 0.586851 and Q exercise probability 0.519939 are three different numbers.

**Pitfall:** discounting a fixed cash dividend as if it were the same as constant q. Discrete payments can produce ex-dividend jumps and early-exercise effects for an American contract. The continuous-yield formula is not an exact treatment of every such schedule. Similarly, a delta-neutral position at one price is not a completed hedge across future price moves.

With bid/ask, costs or exercise rights that differ, an apparent numerical mismatch needs a complete tradable cash-flow comparison.

## Handle expiry, zero volatility and the signs that need assumptions

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="At expiry S = K = 100. Should a calculator divide by σ√T and report a 50% future exercise probability because the option was at the money?" options={[
 {text:'No; the expiry payoff is already known and the regular normal formula is undefined there',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; every at-the-money contract must have a positive payoff in half its states after expiry'},
 {text:'Yes; replacing T = 0 with an arbitrary one-day maturity preserves the same contract'},
]} explanation="Expiry is a separate state of the contract. At an exact tie its call and put payoffs are zero; the event of finishing strictly in the money is false." />

The regular d formulas require **σ > 0 and T > 0**. At T = 0, return the known payoffs:

$$C=\max(S_0-K,0),\qquad P=\max(K-S_0,0).$$

For example, at S₀ = 105 and K = 100 the call pays 5 and the put 0; at S₀ = K both pay 0. The derivative with respect to spot is **undefined at the payoff kink** S₀ = K. Reporting a convenient 0.5 is a chosen convention, not the unique derivative of that function.

At **σ = 0 with positive T**, the Q forward path is deterministic: $S_T=S_0e^{(r-q)T}$. Price its known payoff with the matched discount factor:

$$C=\max(S_0e^{-qT}-Ke^{-rT},0),\qquad P=\max(Ke^{-rT}-S_0e^{-qT},0).$$

At S₀ = K = 100, q = 0, r = 5% and T = 1, this gives $C=100-95.12294245=4.87705755$ and P = 0. Using today's intrinsic value zero would omit the financing effect. With r = q = 0 as well, the deterministic terminal price equals the strike: strict call and put ITM probabilities are **both zero**. With positive variance approaching zero, the exercise-probability limit at this tie can approach one half; the discontinuous strict event explains the difference. There is no ordinary lognormal density or finite d value to draw in the exactly deterministic case.

**Check bounds with the same dividends and rates.** Under these European assumptions,

$$
\max(S_0e^{-qT}-Ke^{-rT},0)\le C\le S_0e^{-qT},\qquad
\max(Ke^{-rT}-S_0e^{-qT},0)\le P\le Ke^{-rT}.
$$

The discounted strike can exceed K at negative rates. A European put cannot exercise immediately, so its lower bound is not universally today's undiscounted intrinsic value K − S₀.

**Time passing needs a scoped sign.** Define **theta** as price change per unit of advancing calendar time, with spot and other inputs fixed; it is the negative of the derivative with respect to remaining time T. In the regular, zero-dividend BSM model with nonnegative r, a long European call has negative theta. That statement does not extend to every long option. For S₀ = 50, K = 100, r = 5%, q = 0, σ = 20% and T = 1, the European put is **45.12534187** with calendar theta about **+4.73841902 per year**. As expiry nears, the present value of its likely strike receipt increases. The put can have positive theta while retaining positive convexity; dividend and negative-rate conditions can also change familiar sign shortcuts.

### When boundary and sign checks matter

At expiry, near deterministic scenarios, when interpreting Greeks, and when validating a calculator. Do not clamp a legal zero time or volatility to an unrelated positive input. For tiny probabilities or deep tails, distinguish the mathematical formula from the precision of its numerical evaluation. This laboratory rejects positive log-return widths below 1e−8 because subtracting rounded normal-CDF values cannot reliably resolve such tiny premiums; it preserves the exactly zero-volatility boundary as a separate case. A sampled curve and a local derivative also have different accuracy requirements.
