A call and a put can reproduce an asset's future **price minus a bill**. Forget the bill and the strategy looks much cheaper than it is. Put–call parity is the discipline of writing down that bill, its payment date and the money needed to settle it.

We use invented prices in one currency **per underlying unit**. A call gives its holder the right to buy at strike K; a put gives the right to sell at K. European rights can be exercised only at the specified expiry. Our ideal examples put exercise, delivery and final cash at one matching date T. Actual contracts may have different fixing, expiry, premium and final settlement dates: check them before carrying this budget into a trade. Every promised option, asset and cash payment is assumed to perform.

S₀ is the asset price now and Sₜ its price at T. C and P are premiums paid now. **D is the price now of one certain currency unit paid at T**; its reciprocal R = 1/D is a gross financing multiplier over the whole interval. We start with S₀ = K = 100 and D = 0.95. This means 95 now buys 100 at T. It does **not** mean the interval's interest rate is 5%: 100/95 − 1 = **5.263158%**. When an annual rate is used below, its convention is stated.

## Complete the payoff before naming the synthetic asset

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A matching European call is bought and a put is sold, both with strike 100. If the final asset price is 60, what does this pair pay, before premiums and financing?" options={[
 {text:'−40: the put obligation remains',correct:true},
 {text:'60: it is exactly the same payoff as one share'},
 {text:'0: both positions have limited downside'},
]} explanation="The call expires without value; the short put loses 100 − 60 = 40. The combined payoff is the final asset price minus the strike." />

The two option payoffs fit together at the strike:

$$
\max(S_T-K,0)-\max(K-S_T,0)=S_T-K.
$$

Below K, the short put supplies the negative part; above K, the call supplies the positive part. **Call minus put is the payoff of a forward with delivery price K**, not a fully paid share. Adding K certain cash at T completes the share payoff. Equivalently, one share plus a put and one call plus K cash both pay $\max(S_T,K)$.

At final prices 60, 100 and 140, the option pair pays **−40, 0 and +40**. The share pays **60, 100 and 140**. The missing amount is 100 in every state. It costs 100D = 95 now to provide it at T. The law of one price therefore gives, under matching frictionless trading and funding assumptions,

$$
C+KD=S_0+P,
\qquad C-P=S_0-KD.
$$

With C = 12 and P = 7, the pair costs 5. The completed positions each cost **107**: call 12 + bond 95 = share 100 + put 7. Those are two different budget comparisons. A contract on 100 units multiplies **every** premium, asset quantity and strike cash flow by 100; it does not multiply only the attractive leg. [CFA Institute's option-replication reading](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/option-replication-using-put-call-parity) frames the equality for European options and a non-income-producing underlying.

**Pitfall:** calling the 5 debit “the price of stock”. It buys a claim with a 100 payment obligation embedded in its terminal payoff. A payoff diagram also excludes the initial premium and its financing.

### When the identity is useful

Use it to check a replication or translate a quoted call/put pair into a forward-like payoff. It does not require a forecast of Sₜ or the Black–Scholes distribution. The equality comes from matching payments in every admissible state, with the same rights and dates.

## Close the initial budget and every later obligation

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="The fair call-minus-put debit is 5. Borrow that debit at D = 0.95. Does the resulting zero-initial-cash position pay Sₜ − 100?" hint="The option payoff still contains the strike. The new loan adds another repayment." text="No. The loan owes 5/0.95 = 5.263157895 at T, so the fully financed payoff is Sₜ − 105.263157895. This equals a new fair forward's payoff, with delivery price S₀/D." buttonLabel="Include the debit financing" />

**Zero initial cash** means the purchases and sales, including borrowing or lending, sum to zero now. It does not mean the position has no obligations later. Financing the fair pair gives

$$
(C-P)/D=5/0.95=5.263157895,
\qquad S_T-100-5.263157895=S_T-105.263157895.
$$

The no-income fair forward delivery price is F₀ = S₀/D = 105.263157895. An option pair struck at 100 has a positive present value; a newly agreed fair forward has zero value at its own delivery price. Mixing those two strikes creates a false free lunch.

Now make the call **13**, keeping put 7, spot 100 and D = 0.95. A **conversion** sells the rich call (+13), buys the put (−7), buys the share (−100) and borrows 95, owing 100 at T. Initial cash is

$$
13-7-100+95=1.
$$

At T the four positions pay $-\max(S_T-100,0)+\max(100-S_T,0)+S_T-100=0$ in every state. To make this a zero-initial-cash budget, lend the 1 surplus now. It becomes 1/0.95 = **1.052631579** at T. At Sₜ = 60, for example, the call contributes 0, the put +40, the share +60 and the loan −100; those cancel. The additional cash account is the gain. At 140 the cancellation is −40 + 0 + 140 − 100 = 0 again.

If the call is too cheap, the **reversal** uses the opposite positions: buy call, sell put, short the share, and buy the strike cash claim. A negative initial surplus must be **borrowed**, not treated as an investment earning the lending rate. The ideal viewer assumes one common financing factor in either direction; the next sections relax the income and execution assumptions.

**Pitfall:** declaring arbitrage after comparing two price labels without showing how the asset can be shorted, cash raised and every liability settled. The [OIC parity explanation](https://www.optionseducation.org/advancedconcepts/put-call-parity) discusses conversions, reversals, spreads, borrowing and dividends alongside the price relationship.

### When to use the cash ledger

Whenever a synthetic position is called “free”, “fully funded” or “equivalent”. First balance the initial cash; then check intermediate obligations and final payments. A positive residual in a feasible ideal model explains the pricing restriction. It does not prove those assumptions are available in a live market.

## Remove income with the correct dated replication

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A known cash dividend of 6 arrives halfway through the year. Can one subtract 6 from spot today when deriving the prepaid delivery cost?" options={[
 {text:'Use the present value of the dated dividend, not necessarily 6',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes, because cash paid on different dates always has the same price'},
 {text:'No dividend can affect a European option pair'},
]} explanation="A dated loan can be repaid by the known dividend. The amount borrowed now is its present value under the applicable discount curve." />

An asset held from now may deliver income **before** T. A prepaid asset claim is different: pay once now to receive **one unit of the asset at T, excluding intervening cash income**. Call that cost A. The general European identity becomes

$$
C-P=A-KD.
$$

For known monetary dividends dᵢ at dates tᵢ, a replicable prepaid delivery costs

$$
A=S_0-\sum_i d_iD(0,t_i).
$$

Our invented one-year example has a certain dividend 6 at t = 0.5 and an explicitly **flat deterministic** discount curve D(0,t) = 0.95ᵗ. One terminal discount factor alone does not establish that curve. The dividend present value is

$$
6\sqrt{0.95}=5.848076607,
\qquad A=100-5.848076607=94.151923393.
$$

Buy the share for 100 and borrow 5.848076607 against the dividend date. The loan owes 6 at the half-year date; the 6 dividend repays it then, leaving **zero net interim cash** and one share at T. Thus C − P = 94.151923393 − 95 = **−0.848076607**. A negative pair value is compatible with positive individual option premiums. Its fair forward delivery price is A/D = **99.107287782**.

A **continuous proportional yield q** uses a different construction. Under the stated reinvestment model, start with e⁻ᑫᵀ shares and continuously reinvest the yield to finish with one share. With q = 3% and T = 1:

$$
A=S_0e^{-qT}=100e^{-0.03}=97.044553355,
\qquad C-P=2.044553355.
$$

The initial share count is 0.970445534; multiplying by e⁰·⁰³ gives one at T. The income buys shares in this construction; it is not also a cash payment left in your pocket. A proportional-yield model and a specified cash-dividend schedule are alternative assumptions, not two deductions to stack by default. [Naranjo's dividend replication notes](https://lorenzonaranjo.com/class-materials/derivatives/notes/impact-of-dividends.html) show the separate dated cash and proportional-yield arguments.

<PutCallFundingLab client:visible view="income"
  title="Separate the dividend loan from share reinvestment"
  tableLabel="Known dividend receipt, loan repayment and their present values" />

**Pitfall:** treating a dividend forecast as an enforceable certain payment available to repay a loan. Unknown dividends, entitlement dates, tax treatment and short-sale compensation can change the actual replication. A benefit of owning an asset that cannot be received as cash or used in this trade does not automatically become a financing inflow.

### When to use the prepaid asset

Use A to keep income out of the option/forward delivery claim. State the entitlement and payment schedule, the curve and the reinvestment assumptions. The same A/D gives a forward delivery price only under the corresponding feasible funding and delivery model.

## Replace midpoint equality with executable funding bands

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A midpoint call-minus-put differs from spot minus discounted strike by a few cents. Is that enough to identify a tradable profit?" hint="Write the prices paid to buy each leg and the prices received to sell it." text="No. Buying uses asks, selling uses bids. Borrowing and lending may use different factors, and stock loans and fees have their own amounts and dates. The complete executable budget can be negative even when a midpoint equality looks wrong." buttonLabel="Price the actual directions" />

A **bid** is the quoted price received for selling; an **ask** is the price paid for buying, for the stated size. Let Rₗ and Rᵦ be known whole-period lending and borrowing factors, Rᵦ ≥ Rₗ > 0. Let f be a fee paid **now** for either complete direction and H a known stock-loan charge paid **at T** in the reversal. We continue to assume no dividends and matching European delivery. The model's short borrow is secured for the interval and its obligation can be met; recall and execution uncertainty would break this simple budget. Final delivery or closure of the asset at the stated Sₜ is assumed feasible without further charges; terminal spreads and fees would need additional rows.

The conversion borrows K/Rᵦ now and owes K at T. Its initial surplus is

$$
\Pi_0^{\mathrm{conv}}=C_{bid}-P_{ask}-S_{ask}+K/R_b-f.
$$

The reversal buys enough final cash to cover **K + H**, not just K:

$$
\Pi_0^{\mathrm{rev}}=P_{bid}+S_{bid}-C_{ask}-(K+H)/R_l-f.
$$

Use spot 99.8/100.2, call 12.0/12.4, put 6.8/7.2, K = 100, Rₗ = 1.04, Rᵦ = 1.06, f = 0.2 and H = 0.5. Then

$$
\Pi_0^{\mathrm{conv}}=12-7.2-100.2+100/1.06-0.2=-1.260377358,
$$

$$
\Pi_0^{\mathrm{rev}}=6.8+99.8-12.4-100.5/1.04-0.2=-2.634615385.
$$

Neither direction offers a positive initial surplus. The resulting restrictions are a **band on different executable combinations**, rather than one midpoint equation:

$$
C_{bid}-P_{ask}\le S_{ask}-K/R_b+f=6.060377358,
$$

$$
C_{ask}-P_{bid}\ge S_{bid}-(K+H)/R_l-f=2.965384615.
$$

The selected sale receipt is 4.8, below the upper limit; the purchase cost is 5.6, above the lower limit. Increase call bid/ask to 14.0/14.4, keeping the other inputs: the conversion's surplus becomes **0.739622642 now**, or **0.769207547 at T** if lent at 1.04. Lower the call to 8.0/8.4 instead: the reversal yields **1.365384615 now**, growing to **1.42**. These are conditional cash budgets with assumed executable size and funding access, not observed opportunities. Negative surpluses are financed at Rᵦ; applying Rₗ to a borrowing shortfall would use the wrong account.

<PutCallFundingLab client:visible view="quotes"
  title="Use the executable quotes and funding of each direction"
  tableLabel="Complete conversion and reversal cash budgets at executable prices" />

**Pitfall:** counting H both in the funded final cash K + H and again as an initial cost. The final cash receives K + H, then K settles the strike-related position and H pays the stock lender. Fees paid now belong to a different row. Package execution, stale quotes, available size, stock-loan recall, dividends, settlement failures and collateral demands can all defeat an apparent price-only opportunity.

### When to use the band

Use it to explain why small midpoint deviations may persist. To investigate an actual trade, replace each assumption with executable terms and a full dated cash schedule. A displayed rate from an option pair is not proof that a particular investor can borrow at it.

## Extract a discount factor before quoting a rate

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Known prepaid asset A = 100, strike 100, European call 12 and put 7 imply D = 0.95. For a one-year interval, which is the effective annual financing rate?" options={[
 {text:'1/0.95 − 1 = 5.263158%',correct:true},
 {text:'1 − 0.95 = 5%'},
 {text:'0.95/1 − 1 = −5%'},
]} explanation="D is the current price of a certain future unit, not a gross growth factor. Growth is its reciprocal. Discount yield and return on the cash actually advanced have different denominators." />

Rearranging the European identity gives a **conditional implied discount factor**:

$$
D_{imp}=\frac{A-C+P}{K},\qquad K>0.
$$

With A = 100, C = 12 and P = 7, Dᵢₘₚ = 95/100 = 0.95. It prices a future unit. Its reciprocal R = 100/95 = 1.052631579 grows an initial unit over the interval. For a one-year interval the effective annual rate is **5.263157895%**, while the continuous annual rate is **−ln(0.95) = 5.129329439%**. For a positive interval of T years:

$$
i_{eff,annual}=D_{imp}^{-1/T}-1,
\qquad r_{continuous,annual}=-\ln(D_{imp})/T.
$$

Neither is the 5% discount relative to the final face amount. They describe the same ideal cash exchange using different conventions. If C = 7 and P = 12, D = 1.05: the period rate 1/1.05 − 1 = **−4.761905%** is negative. A discount factor above one is not automatically impossible for European pricing under negative financing rates.

If A − C + P ≤ 0, no **positive** discount factor emerges; do not put the number into a logarithm or print an invented rate. Even D > 0 is insufficient to validate all individual option bounds, market access or the assumption that A is known. At T = 0 a matching instantaneous identity cannot identify an annual rate; division by zero does not make the financing rate infinite. The preceding [European property arguments](https://lorenzonaranjo.com/class-materials/derivatives/notes/options-properties.html) require matching claims, not merely solvable algebra.

<PutCallFundingLab client:visible view="inference"
  title="Distinguish discount, gross growth and annual rates"
  tableLabel="Conditional implied discount, growth and rate conventions" />

**Pitfall:** interpreting the extracted number as a pure market interest rate when dividends, asset borrow, quote sides or cash dates are uncertain. Those terms can be bundled into what the pair appears to imply. Fixing one of them by assumption does not measure it.

### When implied financing helps

Use it as a conditional comparison among a matched option pair, the underlying's prepaid delivery cost and a financing curve. Report D, the cash dates and the rate convention before comparing quoted annual percentages.

## Read a European box as a dated loan

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A short European box receives 19 now and pays 20 at the common final cash date. Is the 19 credit income?" hint="The final payment is fixed under the stated contract assumptions." text="It is borrowing: 19 is received now in exchange for a 20 repayment later. The 1 difference is a financing cost before fees, not a guaranteed trading gain." buttonLabel="Follow both ends of the loan" />

A **long European box** at K₁ < K₂ combines: buy call K₁, sell put K₁, sell call K₂, buy put K₂. It subtracts one synthetic forward payoff from another:

$$
(S_T-K_1)-(S_T-K_2)=K_2-K_1.
$$

For strikes 90 and 110, the long box pays **20** at the common final cash date, whether Sₜ is 60, 100 or 140. At 100 the four legs pay 10 + 0 + 0 + 10 = 20; at 140 they pay 50 + 0 − 30 + 0 = 20. A short box reverses all signs and owes 20. The common prepaid-asset term cancels, so this ideal same-contract comparison does not require separately forecasting the dividend schedule as the one-pair financing extraction did.

At package premium 19, a long box advances 19 for 20 later; a short box borrows 19 and repays 20. Without fees the period rate is 20/19 − 1 = **5.263158%**. An invented fee of 0.1 at the premium cash date changes the amounts: the short receives 18.9 net, so its cost is 20/18.9 − 1 = **5.820106%**; the long pays 19.1, so its return is 20/19.1 − 1 = **4.712042%**. The same fee creates different net lending and borrowing rates.

For a separate fee-free timing example, suppose premium cash moves on **day 2** and the final 20 moves on **day 182**. The financing interval is 180 actual days. It is not automatically the number of days until the option's last trading time. On that declared clock:

$$
i_{simple,ACT/360}=(20/19-1)\frac{360}{180}=10.526316\%,
$$

$$
i_{effective,365}=(20/19)^{365/180}-1=10.961311\%,
\qquad r_{continuous,365}=\ln(20/19)\frac{365}{180}=10.401140\%.
$$

These are different annual quoting conventions for the same cash exchange. The [CME financing example](https://www.cmegroup.com/articles/2024/index-options-box-spreads-as-financing-tool.html) explicitly measures between premium and final cash dates and uses a stated day-count convention. Its particular product dates and historical margin examples are not universal contract terms.

<PutCallFundingLab client:visible view="box"
  title="Follow a European box and its actual cash clock"
  tableLabel="Four European box payoffs and the dated package cash exchange" />

The fixed final payment does not fix every intervening balance. If the package's unsigned mark rises from 19 to 19.6, a short box's liability mark rises **0.6**. That mark change alone excludes funding, fees and the changing value of the original cash received. It is neither a full financed P&L nor an automatic 0.6 cash call under every collateral agreement. Early closure uses the executable package price at that date. The [OIC/OCC box booklet](https://www.optionseducation.org/getmedia/2ae6c8bd-9a8e-4d2f-8168-19b6ff9e3589/listed-options-box-spread-strategies-for-borrowing-or-lending-cash.pdf) and [Cboe's 2026 SPX discussion](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/spx-box-spreads-what-every-advisor-should-know) identify exercise style, fees, collateral and implementation as part of the financing question. No tax, margin or access rule is assumed here for a particular account.

### When the box interpretation applies

When all four legs share appropriate European rights, underlying, multiplier and settlement schedule, and their cash obligations can be funded and performed. Different settlement conventions, mismatched legs, unavailable package execution or default risk can break the claimed loan equivalence. American exercise adds a separate timing problem.

## Keep American exercise rights inside the conclusion

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Can the European equality be imposed unchanged on American options merely because the final-date call and put payoff formulas match?" options={[
 {text:'No; early exercise and assignment can change the dates and rights being replicated',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; a matching final strike removes every earlier obligation'},
 {text:'Yes; American puts can never be exercised early'},
]} explanation="Matching a payoff at T is insufficient when the contract can generate obligations earlier. A dated replication must respect those rights as well." />

An American holder may exercise before expiry. Its writer can consequently face earlier assignment. The European argument compares payments at one date; it cannot simply erase an American contract's earlier exercise choice. With **no dividends and a common constant nonnegative financing rate**, a useful necessary interval is

$$
S_0-K\le C_A-P_A\le S_0-KD.
$$

These particular assumptions imply 0 < D ≤ 1. At S₀ = K = 100 and D = 0.95, the interval is **0 ≤ Cₐ − Pₐ ≤ 5**. A candidate Cₐ = 12, Pₐ = 8 has difference 4: it passes these necessary bounds while differing from the European no-income value 5. This is not proof that those two individual option prices belong to a valid full model. At zero financing the endpoints coincide in this no-income case. Dividends or negative rates require a fresh argument, not an unlabelled extension of the displayed interval. [Naranjo's American-option notes](https://lorenzonaranjo.com/class-materials/derivatives/notes/american-options.html) keep those rate and exercise assumptions explicit.

**Pitfall:** applying a European box's fixed-date borrowing rate to American legs. Exercise or assignment may accelerate a strike-related payment, change the asset position and create a funding need before the planned end date. A final payoff cancellation does not guarantee the original financing timetable.

### When bounds are the useful answer

When exercise rights differ from the clean European case. Start with a scoped restriction and an explicit exercise model. Do not force one equality onto every option style or label every difference from it mispricing.
