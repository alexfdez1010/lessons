An option screen says “20 vol”. That is a compact way to quote a price through a model. It does not mean someone has measured next year's volatility. To read that number, we need the contract, the model inputs, the price used and a check that the inverse actually exists.

We will use European calls and puts: the holder may exercise **only at expiry**, unlike an American contract. Prices are per underlying unit, before trading costs. S is current spot, K is the strike, T is remaining time in years, r is a matched **continuous annual financing rate**, q is a **continuous proportional dividend yield**, and σ is annualized volatility in the chosen Black–Scholes model. The preceding lessons supplied that model's assumptions and its pricing measure Q. We keep them; a quote inversion does not replace them with a physical forecasting law P.

## Identify what the fitted parameter actually fits

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A European call premium is supplied, but the proportional dividend yield used in the model changes. Must the fitted implied volatility stay unchanged?" options={[
 {text:'No; the volatility that reproduces a price depends on the other model inputs',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; implied volatility is a directly observed physical constant'},
 {text:'Yes; dividends cannot affect a European call price'},
]} explanation="Implied volatility is conditional on a model, its inputs and the selected premium. A different dividend/carry assumption can require a different fitted sigma for the same premium." />

**Implied volatility (IV)** is the σ that makes a stated option-pricing model reproduce a chosen premium M:

$$
V_{\mathrm{model}}(S,K,T,r,q;\sigma_{\mathrm{IV}})=M.
$$

For a fixed regular European contract, the inverse usually has one free parameter. That is an accounting of our **chosen inputs**, not a claim that every other input is a perfect observation. Strike and exercise style come from the contract. Dates need the actual expiry and settlement conventions. A quoted spot may be stale or have a spread. Financing comes from a selected curve or funding convention; a Treasury yield is not automatically the right number for every underlying and maturity. Future dividends or borrow costs may be estimates. A last option trade need not be executable now.

Consider S = K = 100, T = 1, r = 5% continuous and q = 0. In the model, σ = 20% gives a call premium **10.450583572**. Inverting that full price recovers 20%. Round the observed premium to 10.45 and the fitted number changes slightly. That is price precision showing up in the inverse, not a new observation of the future.

Hold the full 10.450583572 premium fixed but assume q = 2% instead: the fitted σ becomes **23.226039%**. Alternatively keep q = 0 and use r = 3%: the fitted σ becomes **22.680664%**. More than one combination of assumptions can reproduce the same price. An IV quote must travel with its model conventions. [MathWorks' European-IV interface](https://www.mathworks.com/help/finance/blsimpv.html) makes the rate, yield, years, option class and premium explicit inputs.

**Pitfall:** reading “implied” as “the market's physical mean volatility”. A current premium can encode risk compensation, hedging demand, supply constraints, costs and model mismatch. IV can contain forecasting information; evaluating that requires an empirical forecast test. Its definition alone establishes neither unbiasedness nor a guaranteed future value.

### When the fit is useful

For translating comparable option prices into a common quoting convention, comparing bids and offers, and marking a chosen model. Record the timestamp, side of the spread, exercise style, dividend/carry assumptions and day count. Comparing two unlabeled IV numbers can amount to comparing two different contracts through two different models.

## Check bounds and identification before looking for a root

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="At the exact expiry of an at-the-money European option, its payoff is zero. Can a matching zero premium identify a unique implied volatility?" options={[
 {text:'No; the expiry payoff is independent of volatility',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; it uniquely proves sigma is zero'},
 {text:'Yes; the next Newton step always identifies it'},
]} explanation="At T = 0 there is no remaining diffusion interval. Every sigma produces the same expiry payoff, so a matching premium leaves volatility unidentified." />

A numerical search is useful only if there is something to find. For **positive S, K and T**, define the discounted asset and strike amounts

$$
A=Se^{-qT},\qquad B=Ke^{-rT}.
$$

The European Black–Scholes call price moves from $L_C=\max(A-B,0)$ at σ = 0 toward upper limit $U_C=A$ as σ tends to infinity. A put moves from $L_P=\max(B-A,0)$ toward $U_P=B$. For finite positive σ, regular European call and put **vega**, the price sensitivity per full unit of σ, is

$$
\mathcal V=A\phi(d_1)\sqrt T>0.
$$

Here φ is the standard normal **density**, not its cumulative distribution. Positive vega makes this regular price function continuous and strictly increasing. Consequently:

- A premium **strictly between** L and U has one finite positive model IV.
- A premium **equal to L** is the zero-volatility boundary fit. Rounded or missing bids can make this a data issue; it does not prove a motionless physical future.
- A premium **equal to U** is an infinite-volatility limit, with no finite solution.
- A premium **outside** these bounds cannot be matched by this specified model and input set.

For the call with S = K = 100, T = 1, r = 5%, q = 0, $B=100e^{-0.05}=95.12294245$. Its bounds are **4.87705755** and **100**. A premium 4 is below the model lower bound. Premium 100 has no finite IV. Premium 10.450583572 is in the interior and has the 20% fit. A solver limited to σ ≤ 10% would miss that root even though it exists. A search cap is a numerical choice, not a theorem about the price domain.

At **T = 0**, the model range collapses to the intrinsic payoff: call $max(S-K,0)$ or put $max(K-S,0)$. A matching premium gives **unidentified IV**; a different premium is incompatible with that expiry payoff. Do not silently substitute σ = 0 for “unidentified”. The [pricing textbook's limit exercises](https://book.derivative-securities.org/Chapter_BlackScholes.html) distinguish the limits in time and volatility; our inverse must distinguish them too.

At positive time choose 0, an interior price, 100 and 101. At expiry the model range collapses to zero. An interior price such as 99 can also exceed the price reached by the finite 500% numerical cap; that is different from mathematical nonexistence." />

**Pitfall:** using the same bounds for an American premium, a discrete-dividend contract or a different settlement convention without adapting the model. A mismatch calls for checking the contract and inputs. It does not automatically prove that the market offers an executable arbitrage. Vega need not be strictly positive for every exotic payoff or at a degenerate boundary.

### When to reject an inverse

Before a root finder runs. Report whether the issue is a model bound, expiry identification, an unbracketed search cap or numerical precision. A display showing a volatility number in every state can conceal the most useful answer: this price and model do not identify that number.

## Use the bracket and the residual rather than trust a Newton jump

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A price function increases with sigma. Does that guarantee that Newton’s next guess is positive and inside the search interval?" hint="Newton follows a tangent, rather than staying halfway inside a bracket." text="No. A small or poorly conditioned slope can create an enormous step. Monotonicity gives uniqueness in the regular price domain; it does not guarantee convergence of an unprotected Newton iteration." buttonLabel="Inspect the numerical step" />

A **root** is a σ for which the residual $f(\sigma)=V(\sigma)-M$ is zero. **Bisection** begins with a lower and upper σ whose residuals have opposite signs, then repeatedly keeps the half containing a root. Continuity and a valid bracket support convergence; an arbitrary 1%–200% interval does not automatically bracket every price. A fixed iteration budget can also end before the requested accuracy is reached.

**Newton's method** uses a local tangent:

$$
\sigma_{n+1}=\sigma_n-\frac{V(\sigma_n)-M}{\mathcal V(\sigma_n)}.
$$

The units matter. Vega in this expression is **currency per full volatility unit**: changing σ from 0.20 to 0.21 is a change of 0.01, or one volatility percentage point. If a desk quotes vega per point, multiply that vega by 100 before using this equation with decimal σ.

Reproduce the earlier full-precision target M = 10.450583572 with an initial guess σ₀ = 0.30. The model gives price 14.231254786 and vega 37.943293312 per unit σ. The first step is

$$
\sigma_1=0.30-\frac{14.231254786-10.450583572}{37.943293312}
=0.2003599613.
$$

Repricing at that guess gives **10.464091409**, still **0.013507837** above the target. A plausible-looking 20.036% is not yet the exact fit. Reprice and inspect the next step. Practical solvers can combine a bracket with safeguarded Newton steps; they also need maximum iterations and explicit stopping rules.

<ImpliedVolatilityLab client:visible view="search" title="Compare a bracketed search with unprotected Newton steps" tableLabel="Trial volatility, repriced premium, residual and vega in each numerical iteration" chartLabel="European model premium, target price and the last displayed bisection bracket" caption="Initial target is generated at 20% in the model; Newton starts at 30%. The call has spot/strike 100, one year, continuous r = 5% and q = 0. Bisection brackets volatility between 0% and 100%; the shaded region is the last displayed bracket. The educational Newton trace stops if a step leaves that supported interval or vega becomes too small. Its price stopping tolerance is 0.00000001 currency here; a small residual alone does not establish accurate IV when vega is tiny. The readouts show guesses, not promised convergence." />

**Pitfall:** reporting only that a routine “finished”. A small **price residual** and a small **σ interval** answer different questions. Data uncertainty can dominate both. The main inverse here requires both a bounded volatility interval and a repricing tolerance, and reports an unresolved state near the evaluator's precision limit. The regular numerical evaluator in the pricing lesson also has an explicit positive-dispersion resolution; zero time is not a numerical substitute for a tiny positive time.

There is no need to promise that no special-case inverse exists. For example, with S = K and r = q = 0, the call satisfies $C/S=2N(\sigma\sqrt T/2)-1$, so an inverse-normal expression is possible. General contract inputs are normally handled numerically. The mathematical question and the software's finite precision remain separate.

### When each method helps

Use a reliable bracket to establish what the numerical search covers. Use Newton for speed when its slope and step are acceptable, with a fallback or an honest stop. Log the premium, fitted value, residual, cap, tolerances and solver status so that a failed inversion cannot masquerade as a market observation.

## Translate bid and ask uncertainty through vega

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Two synchronized premiums form an executable bid/ask interval. After applying the same valid increasing European model, should they imply one exact volatility or an interval?" options={[
 {text:'An IV interval, provided both endpoints have valid model fits',correct:true},
 {text:'One exact IV because every premium quote is a perfect observation'},
 {text:'The average of endpoint IVs must equal the IV of the midpoint price'},
]} explanation="A monotone inverse maps an ordered valid price interval to an IV interval. The inverse is nonlinear, so midpoint inversion and averaging endpoint IVs need not agree." />

The **bid** is the quoted premium at which the option can be sold; the **ask** is the premium at which it can be bought, subject to the quote's size and execution terms. Apply identical model conventions to both. For our S = K = 100, T = 1, r = 5%, q = 0 call:

$$
M_{\mathrm{bid}}=10.4\ \Rightarrow\ \sigma_{\mathrm{bid}}=19.865172814\%,
$$

$$
M_{\mathrm{ask}}=10.5\ \Rightarrow\ \sigma_{\mathrm{ask}}=20.131670165\%.
$$

A midpoint fit is a marking convention, not an offer to trade at that midpoint. Nor is it exactly the average of the two endpoint IVs. If an endpoint is below a model bound, or the bid exceeds the ask, report that problem rather than manufacturing an ordered pair of fitted IVs.

<ImpliedVolatilityLab client:visible view="quotes" title="Map a quoted premium interval into a model IV interval" tableLabel="Bid, midpoint and ask premiums with their separate IV fits and model statuses" chartLabel="European premium curve and the midpoint of the selected bid/ask inputs" caption="Initial invented quotes: bid 1,040 ticks and ask 1,050 ticks, with tick size 0.01 currency, so premiums are 10.4 and 10.5. The implied endpoint volatilities are 19.865173% and 20.131670%. Quote size, fees, credit and execution are not simulated. Crossed quotes or invalid endpoints keep their explicit status. Changing option type also changes the model used for the same premiums." />

Locally, the inverse sensitivity is

$$
\Delta\sigma\approx\frac{\Delta M}{\mathcal V}.
$$

At σ = 20% for the regular one-year call, vega is **37.524034692**, so a 0.01 premium change corresponds locally to $0.01/37.524034692=0.000266496$ in decimal σ, or **0.026650 percentage points**. Smaller vega makes the inverse more sensitive.

A separate deliberately tiny-premium example makes the problem visible. Keep S = 100 and r = 5%, q = 0, but use **K = 130 and T = 0.1**. At σ = 20%, call price is **0.00003770533645** and vega is **0.003637624793**. Increasing the premium by **0.00001** gives a fitted σ of **20.247978%**. The derivative estimate would give about **20.274905%**; it is a local approximation, not an exact finite-shock calculation. These fractional currency ticks are invented to isolate numerical sensitivity, not specifications of a listed product.

A small currency change can move fitted IV substantially; a rounded zero bid is a boundary input, not proof of zero physical volatility." />

**Pitfall:** treating the last decimal of a fitted IV as information when the premium is rounded, stale or wide, or using a European inversion on an American premium with exercise value. Dates, rate/dividend estimates, borrow constraints and model choice can matter more than the root finder's residual.

### When to report a range

When the price input is an interval or has material rounding uncertainty. Check the IV change from a realistic price tick, the relevant vega units and the contract model. “20.000000%” may describe the numerical precision of a calculation while overstating the precision of the market input.

## Read a smile as a set of fitted prices

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Three options on the same underlying and expiry each have a valid individual IV. Does that alone guarantee their combined price curve is free of cross-strike inconsistencies?" options={[
 {text:'No; separately fitted prices can still violate spread or convexity conditions',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; an IV number automatically makes every option portfolio consistent'},
 {text:'Yes; any smile is the true physical terminal distribution'},
]} explanation="Each inverse matches one premium. Cross-strike restrictions link several payoffs and require additional checks; a physical distribution is not identified by a few fitted sigmas." />

A **volatility smile or skew** is IV plotted across strikes for one expiry, under the chosen quoting model. A **volatility surface** adds the expiry dimension. The **moneyness** K/S compares strike with current spot; forward moneyness uses a matched forward instead, so record which convention you mean. Common equity-index skews and other smile shapes are useful descriptions, not permanent laws forcing every market or currency pair into one shape. [OIC's skew discussion](https://www.optionseducation.org/news/april-webinar-key-takeaways-understanding-volatility-and-options-skew) includes negative, positive and two-wing profiles.

For an invented same-expiry European call slice, use S = 100, r = q = 0 and T = 1. Strikes **80, 100 and 120** are assigned IVs **28%, 20% and 18%**, producing premiums **23.009308775, 7.965567455 and 1.598730377**. That downward IV slope describes these prices. It does not prove that the physical left tail has a particular frequency.

The call prices must also make sense together under common frictionless European contract assumptions. A lower-strike call pays at least as much as a higher-strike call. For a 20-unit strike spacing and zero rates, each vertical-spread price must lie between **0 and 20**. A **butterfly** holding one call at 80, short two at 100 and long one at 120 has a nonnegative terminal payoff. Its price therefore must be nonnegative under these ideal assumptions:

$$
C(80)-2C(100)+C(120)\ge0.
$$

The initial three premiums pass those necessary checks. Independent IV changes can make them fail. An individual IV is a quote translation; it does not enforce a coherent whole surface. Execution costs and bid/ask sides belong in an actual trading budget, and a few discrete checks do not validate every intermediate strike or expiry.

**Annualized volatility and total dispersion are different.** Define model total variance $w=\sigma^2T$. A quarter-year σ of 20% gives $w_1=0.20^2(0.25)=0.01$. A one-year σ of 18% gives $w_2=0.18^2(1)=0.0324$. Annualized volatility falls while total model variance grows. Inferring a forward variance or checking calendar arbitrage requires consistent moneyness, financing, dividends and surface assumptions; subtracting two arbitrary fixed-strike IVs is not that calculation.

**Pitfall:** saying a nonflat IV curve directly measures the “true” physical tails. Option prices reflect pricing weights and contractual cash flows. A set of σ values fitted strike by strike is not itself a full physical probability model. Reciprocal currency quotes do not force symmetric option smiles.

### When to compare surface points

When the model, underlying, timestamps and contract conventions match. Record strike or delta conventions, expiry and quote side. Fit a surface with suitable price consistency checks; do not equate a smooth drawing with an executable price system or a physical forecast.

## Keep realized variation, variance premia and VIX in their own units

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="An option-implied variance measure is 0.04 and the assigned physical expected variance is 0.0324. Does a positive ex-ante difference guarantee realized variance will finish below 0.04?" hint="An expectation is not the subsequently observed outcome." text="No. The ex-ante variance premium is 0.0076 in variance units under these assumptions. A realized variance of 0.09 would produce an ex-post implied-minus-realized spread of −0.05. Both can occur together." buttonLabel="Separate the two comparisons" />

**Realized volatility** is a statistic computed from a specified observed return record. Its value depends on sampling, returns, centering, denominator, annualization and horizon. For a separate measurement example, take four invented daily log returns **−0.01, +0.01, −0.01, +0.01**. Their mean is zero. Using the population mean square and an explicitly assumed 252-session year,

$$
\widehat v_{\mathrm{annual}}=\frac{252}{4}\sum_{i=1}^4r_i^2
=252(0.0001)=0.0252,
$$

so the annualized volatility estimate is $\sqrt{0.0252}=15.874508\%$. A sample-variance denominator of three would give a different estimate. Four observations do not establish a reliable future forecast. Match the estimator and time conventions before comparing a realized statistic with an implied measure.

A **variance risk premium**, under the convention here, is an ex-ante difference for the **same future variance quantity V and horizon**:

$$
\operatorname{VRP}_0=\mathbb E^Q_0[V]-\mathbb E^P_0[V].
$$

Q supplies pricing weights; P supplies physical probabilities. The physical conditional mean must be estimated or assumed, so this premium is not directly observed from one future outcome. [Bali and Zhou's FEDS working paper defines that distinction](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2011/201145/index.html). Some empirical studies use other proxies or signs; state the convention rather than silently switching definitions.

In an **independent invented same-horizon example**, suppose annualized pricing-measure expected variance is **0.04** and physical expected variance is **0.0324**. The premium is **+0.0076 annualized return-variance units**. Their square roots are 20% and 18%; the two-percentage-point volatility difference is **not** that variance premium. If the future realized variance is **0.09**, the ex-post spread is $0.04-0.09=-0.05$. A positive conditional premium does not guarantee a positive realized short-option P&L.

<ImpliedVolatilityLab client:visible view="variance" title="Separate variance expectations from the future observation" tableLabel="Assigned Q and P expected variances, measured realized variance and their distinct differences" chartLabel="Annualized return-variance expectations, realized value and signed variance differences" caption="Initial invented same-horizon annualized variances: Q 0.04, P 0.0324 and future realized 0.09. It is not a physical probability range, a single-option IV fit or a replication of live VIX." />

The **Cboe VIX** is an annualized volatility index designed to summarize approximately 30-calendar-day S&P 500 option-implied variation. Its methodology aggregates a strip of SPX/SPXW option quotes and interpolates variance across specified expiries before taking a square root. It is **not** the Black–Scholes IV of one at-the-money call. Its “model-free” construction avoids requiring one constant σ for all strikes; it still has replication, data-selection, rate, timing and interpolation conventions. The [official VIX methodology](https://cdn.cboe.com/api/global/us_indices/governance/Volatility_Index_Methodology_Cboe_Volatility_Index.pdf) and [mathematical methodology](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/Cboe_Volatility_Index_Mathematics_Methodology.pdf) specify those rules.

Nor can a square root and an expectation exchange places. In a simple law assigning equal probabilities to variance values $0.10^2$ and $0.30^2$, mean square-root variance is **20%**, while square root of mean variance is $\sqrt{(0.01+0.09)/2}=22.360680\%$. A quoted square-root variance scale is not automatically the physical mean of future volatility. A VIX reading of 20 is annualized; it does not promise a 20% monthly move, a direction or a probability band.

**Pitfall:** defining a variance premium as any raw IV-minus-realized-vol gap, universalizing its historical sign, or promising profit when realized volatility exceeds a purchased option's IV. A regular vanilla's positive vega is a **local price sensitivity holding other inputs fixed**. Its actual P&L also depends on the price path, strike, time, funding, hedging, costs and execution. An unhedged out-of-the-money call can expire worthless even during a volatile period. The separate hedging course studies the full dynamic budget.

### When each measure answers your question

Use IV for model-conditional quote translation, a specified realized estimator for measurement, a same-horizon Q/P variance comparison for an explicitly defined premium, and VIX for its own option-strip index convention. Forecasting and trading questions need additional assumptions and validation. A precise label is often more informative than another decimal.
