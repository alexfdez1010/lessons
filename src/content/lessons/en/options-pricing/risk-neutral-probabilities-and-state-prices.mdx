Two analysts disagree about the chance of a stock rise: one says 70%, the other 30%. They can still use the same replication price in a model with the same quoted stock price, funding and future payoff states. Their disagreement changes a forecast. It does not change what an identical stock-and-cash payoff costs to assemble from those fixed inputs.

The earlier lessons built the binomial tree. This lesson goes beneath its q weights: **what does one unit paid only in one future state cost today?** That question exposes the discounting, financing and uniqueness assumptions hiding inside the shortcut.

Our central case is an **invented one-year period**. Stock today is 100 currency units, and at year end it is exactly 140 or 80. Both states are possible. One unit placed in the riskless bank becomes **R = 1.10** at year end: R is a **gross multiplier for the whole period**, so the stated effective period return is 10%. It is not an unspecified APR to divide into smaller periods. We assume no dividends, defaults, taxes or trading costs; fractional shares; unrestricted shorting with use of sale proceeds; and the same borrowing and lending rate. These are model assumptions, not guaranteed trading facilities.

## Price a claim on one state

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Two claims each pay one currency unit at year end: the first only in the up state, the second only in the down state. If bank growth is R = 1.10, what should their combined price be in the model?" options={[
 {text:'1 / 1.10, about 0.909091 today',correct:true},
 {text:'Exactly 1 because there are two probabilities'},
 {text:'1.10 because that is what cash grows into'},
]} explanation="Together the claims pay one in either state. That is the payoff of a riskless unit at year end, whose present price is 1/R. The prices already contain discounting." />

A **state** is one specified possible outcome at the comparison date. A **unit state claim**, also called an Arrow–Debreu claim, pays one currency unit if that state occurs and zero otherwise. Its **state price** is the cost **today** of that dated payoff. It is a price, rather than an estimate of how often the state occurs. [Sharpe's Stanford teaching presentation](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/art/rrsurvey/handout.htm) uses this unit-state-payoff construction.

Call the up-state price $\pi_u$ and down-state price $\pi_d$. Buying both gives one unit at year end, so $\pi_u+\pi_d=1/R$. Buying 140 up-state units and 80 down-state units copies one share's year-end value. Their cost must reproduce the share's current price:

$$
\pi_u+\pi_d=\frac1{1.10}=\frac{10}{11},\qquad
140\pi_u+80\pi_d=100.
$$

Multiply the first equation by 80 and subtract it from the second: $60\pi_u=100-800/11=300/11$. Thus

$$
\pi_u=\frac5{11}\approx0.454545,\qquad \pi_d=\frac5{11}\approx0.454545.
$$

One up-only unit costs about 0.454545 now. One down-only unit costs the same in this example. Their sum is 0.909091, not one. The equality of the two state prices follows from these chosen market inputs; it does not tell us the physical probabilities.

### Construct the unit claims from investable payoffs

The up-only unit can be replicated by **1/60 of a share** and initial cash **−40/33**, meaning borrowing. Its cost is $100/60-40/33=5/11$. At year end the cash debt is $(40/33)(11/10)=4/3$:

| Replication of one up-only unit | Up state | Down state |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Share value | 140/60 = 7/3 | 80/60 = 4/3 |
| Debt repayment | −4/3 | −4/3 |
| Net payoff | 1 | 0 |

The down-only unit uses **−1/60 of a share** and initial cash **70/33**. It costs $-100/60+70/33=5/11$. The bank pays $(70/33)(11/10)=7/3$, canceling the short share's 7/3 liability in the up state and leaving $7/3-4/3=1$ in the down state. A state claim need not already be a listed instrument; the model constructs it from the traded share and bank.

**Pitfall:** treating virtual model states as a complete list of what a real stock can do. The exact replication above is conditional on exactly these two terminal outcomes and feasible financing. A third possible stock value can break a two-point payoff match.

### When state prices help

When valuing several claims on the same specified states. Solve the unit-state prices once, then multiply each claim's state payoffs by them. Keep the payment date and currency attached to every unit.

## Normalize state prices into pricing probabilities

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="If each state price is 5/11 and gross bank growth is 11/10, how do you obtain a normalized up-state pricing weight?" options={[
 {text:'Multiply the state price by R: (5/11) × (11/10) = 1/2',correct:true},
 {text:'Divide the state price by R again'},
 {text:'Replace it with the analyst’s 70% forecast'},
]} explanation="The state-price sum is 1/R. Dividing each state price by that sum is the same as multiplying by R, producing weights summing to one." />

**Risk-neutral probabilities** are the normalized weights that reproduce traded prices after one riskless discount in this model:

$$
q_u=R\pi_u,\qquad q_d=R\pi_d,\qquad q_u+q_d=1.
$$

Here both are 1/2. They price a terminal claim with payoffs $X_u,X_d$ by

$$
X_0=\pi_uX_u+\pi_dX_d
=\frac{q_uX_u+q_dX_d}{R}.
$$

These are two representations of the **same** price. State prices already discount; q weights do not. The [CFA Institute's one-period model](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/valuing-derivative-one-period-binomial-model) connects replication with a discounted risk-neutral expectation.

A call with strike 100 pays 40 in the up state and zero in the down state. Using state prices gives $(5/11)\times40+(5/11)\times0=200/11\approx18.181818$. Using q gives $(0.5\times40+0.5\times0)/1.10=200/11$, the same result.

<StatePriceExplorer client:visible title="Use either discounted state prices or normalized q weights" caption="The initial claim pays 40 or zero. State prices already include discounting; normalized q weights require exactly one division by whole-period R. A raw algebraic weight outside the strict consistency domain is not repaired by clipping it into the probability interval." />

For the share itself, the q-weighted future value is $0.5\times140+0.5\times80=110$. Dividing by R gives $110/1.10=100$. This is what “risk-neutral” means here: the discounted expected traded payoff under q reproduces its price. It does not mean that the share's outcome is riskless or that investors need not care about losses.

**Pitfall:** discounting twice. Taking the state-price result 200/11 and dividing by R again gives $2{,}000/121\approx16.528926$, which fails to match the replication cost. Conversely, using q but omitting discounting gives 20, also a different number.

### When to choose a representation

Use state prices for a payoff budget across states, or q for a familiar discounted expectation. State which representation you are using before applying a discount factor. When R = 1 the numeric sums coincide, but prices and probabilities still have different meanings.

## Finance the replica before announcing arbitrage

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="The call's replicated cost is 200/11, but it trades at 22 in the ideal model. After selling the call and buying the financed replica, what must you do to show a zero-initial-cash strategy with a terminal profit?" options={[
 {text:'Record the stock purchase and loan, then lend the remaining initial surplus',correct:true},
 {text:'Call the entire 22 a profit and omit the stock purchase'},
 {text:'Count the borrowed principal as additional investment income'},
]} explanation="The sold claim creates an obligation. Its replica must be financed explicitly. Lending the remaining proceeds converts the initial surplus into a dated terminal receipt while leaving net initial cash flow zero." />

The introductory lesson already derived delta. We need only the bridge to a **complete cash budget**. For payoffs 40/0 and stock prices 140/80:

$$
\Delta=\frac{40-0}{140-80}=\frac23,\qquad
B_0=\frac{0-(2/3)80}{1.10}=-\frac{1{,}600}{33}.
$$

The replica costs $(2/3)100-1{,}600/33=200/11$. It owns 2/3 share and borrows 1,600/33 initially; it repays $1{,}600/33\times11/10=160/3$ at year end. In the up state, $280/3-160/3=40$; in the down state, $160/3-160/3=0$.

Now suppose an otherwise identical claim has a **market quote of 22**. Sell one claim for 22, buy the replica and lend the surplus $22-200/11=42/11$. The [CFA Institute's replication assumptions](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/valuation-contingent-claims) include investable hedges, unrestricted use of short-sale proceeds and common borrowing/lending conditions; those assumptions are doing the work here.

| Cash-flow leg, in currency units | Today | Up state, year end | Down state, year end |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Sell one claim | +22 | −40 | 0 |
| Buy 2/3 share | −200/3 | +280/3 | +160/3 |
| Borrow cash for the replica | +1,600/33 | −160/3 | −160/3 |
| Lend the initial surplus | −42/11 | +4.20 | +4.20 |
| **Total net cash flow** | **0** | **+4.20** | **+4.20** |

The surplus deposit becomes $(42/11)(11/10)=4.20$ at year end. Every stock, claim and financing obligation is included before we call that terminal amount profit. Displayed decimals are rounded; the fraction arithmetic is exact in the ideal example.

<StatePriceExplorer client:visible view="replication" title="Trace all positions and funding in the discrepancy trade" caption="All claim, share, borrowing/lending and surplus-deposit flows are included. Positive table entries are receipts and negative entries payments at that date/state. The initial example has a claim quote of 22; changing payoffs or the quote recomputes the replica and the trade direction. No costs or financing restrictions are assumed." />

If the claim instead costs 16, reverse the positions: buy the claim, short its replica and lend $200/11-16=24/11$. Initial net cash remains zero and the terminal surplus is $(24/11)(11/10)=2.40$ in either state. If the quoted payoff is riskless 44/44, its replica is just bank cash and its fair price is 40; the same budget logic works without a stock holding.

**Pitfall:** an upfront receipt from selling an option is not profit without its future obligation. Nor is borrowing income. If margin, borrow fees, bid/ask, execution timing or extra states prevent the matched positions, the frictionless table has not proved a capturable real-world trade.

### When to demand a full budget

Whenever an arbitrage argument says “sell the expensive one and buy the cheap one.” Ask for positions, cash sources, payment dates, all possible terminal states and the fate of any initial surplus. A positive forecast return is a different claim from state-by-state arbitrage.

## Separate physical probabilities from state discount factors

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Keep all stock/cash prices and terminal payoffs fixed, but assume physical up probability p = 70%. Does dividing the call's physical expected payoff by R necessarily reproduce its replication price?" options={[
 {text:'No; physical probabilities need the appropriate state risk adjustment',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; knowing p removes every need to price risk'},
 {text:'No; mathematical expectations are undefined for option payoffs'},
]} explanation="A physical expected payoff exists. The issue is whether its weighting and discounting price risk consistently with the traded assets. Physical p alone is not the normalized pricing weight q." />

A **physical probability** describes how often an outcome occurs under a proposed real-world model. Here p = 0.70 is an explicit invented assumption, not something inferred from q. It gives expected terminal stock $0.7\times140+0.3\times80=122$, or a 22% expected period return from 100. Cash earns 10% in this example. That difference is a forecast under p, not a guaranteed realized excess return.

The call's physical expected payoff is $0.7\times40+0.3\times0=28$. Dividing by R gives $28/1.10=280/11\approx25.454545$, which differs from the replicated 200/11. Even perfect knowledge of p would not justify a riskless discount without a risk adjustment.

A **state discount factor** $m_s$ combines the time and state-dependent risk adjustment. With strictly positive physical state probabilities, define

$$
m_u=\frac{\pi_u}{p},\qquad m_d=\frac{\pi_d}{1-p}.
$$

For p = 0.70, $m_u=(5/11)/0.7=50/77\approx0.649351$ and $m_d=(5/11)/0.3=50/33\approx1.515152$. Now a physically weighted, **state-adjusted** expectation reproduces the price:

$$
X_0=p\,m_uX_u+(1-p)m_dX_d.
$$

For the call, $0.7(50/77)40+0.3(50/33)0=200/11$. For one terminal unit in both states, $0.7(50/77)+0.3(50/33)=10/11$. The stock also prices at 100 under those same factors. A factor can exceed one: it is not itself a probability, and its interpretation includes the price of payment in that state.

<StatePriceExplorer client:visible view="beliefs" title="Change physical beliefs while holding the quoted market fixed" caption="Only the assumed physical p moves in this view. State prices and q stay fixed because the quoted stock, terminal states and bank growth are held fixed. State discount factors adjust so the physically weighted price identity still holds. These factors are derived algebraically here, not estimated investor preferences." />

Move p to 0.30. Physical expected stock becomes $0.3\times140+0.7\times80=98$ and expected claim payoff becomes 12. The factors swap: the up factor is 50/33 and down factor 50/77; the call still prices at 200/11. No-arbitrage alone does not require every risky asset to have a positive physical expected excess return.

**Pitfall:** “q is not a forecast” does not mean observed market prices are unrelated to beliefs. Beliefs and risk preferences can influence those prices in an equilibrium model. Our slider is a conditional exercise that holds the market inputs fixed. Also, p can numerically equal q; that equality by itself does not establish that all investors are risk-neutral.

### When to use each probability

Use an explicit physical model for scenario frequencies and expected realized outcomes. Use a pricing measure or a consistent state discount factor for valuation. Confusing the two can turn a market-implied price into an unsupported forecast probability.

## Diagnose an inconsistent market instead of clipping q

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Suppose R = 1.40 with the same stock prices 100 today and 140/80 at year end. Is the boundary harmless because an algebraic q equals one?" options={[
 {text:'No; shorting the share and lending the proceeds pays zero up and 60 down, with zero initial net cash',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; a probability of one proves the up state is physically certain'},
 {text:'Yes; replace q by 99% and continue pricing'},
]} explanation="Both terminal states remain possible by assumption. A nonnegative payoff in both and a positive payoff in the possible down state is arbitrage under the financing assumptions. The boundary cannot be repaired by clipping a weight." />

Positive state prices for both possible outcomes require

$$
S_d<S_0R<S_u\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{S_d}{S_0}<R<\frac{S_u}{S_0}.
$$

Here the strict interval is $0.80<R<1.40$. If cash growth falls to the lower boundary 0.80, buy a share for 100 using 100 borrowing. The initial purchase and loan receipt net to zero. The loan requires 80 at year end: the stock pays 80 or 140, leaving **zero down and 60 up**. At R = 0.60 it leaves **20 down and 80 up**.

At the upper boundary R = 1.40, short a share for 100 and put that 100 into the bank. Initial net cash is zero. The bank pays 140; closing the short requires 140 up or 80 down, leaving **zero up and 60 down**. At R = 1.60 it leaves **20 up and 80 down**. The positive result need not occur in every state for an arbitrage definition requiring no loss in any possible state and a gain in at least one possible state.

<StatePriceExplorer client:visible view="domain" bankGrowth={1.4} title="Check the stock and cash before pricing another claim" caption="The initial setting is the upper equality boundary. Move R through 0.80 and 1.40 and inspect the complete stock/cash budget. These are financial consistency examples: the interface retains raw algebraic weights as diagnostics and withholds the arbitrage-free claim price, rather than clipping q into a probability." />

At R = 0.60, the algebraic up weight is $(60-80)/(140-80)=-1/3$. At R = 1.60 it is $(160-80)/60=4/3$. At the boundaries it is zero or one. These are symptoms of stock and cash prices that cannot both be arbitrage-free with two possible states and the stated trades. They are not estimates of a negative physical chance or proof that one terminal state is impossible.

**Pitfall:** a program that silently changes −1/3 to zero or 4/3 to one can still print a neat option value. It has changed the weighting without making the quoted stock and cash prices consistent. A financial contradiction should be explained; malformed inputs such as identical terminal stock prices are a different problem because the two-state replication denominator vanishes.

### When to check the domain

Before trusting a tree, a risk-neutral simulation or a probability recovered from prices. Check time/compounding units, dividends, funding conventions and the assumed state support. If a model's prices contradict its own assumptions, changing a displayed probability does not repair the model.

## Ask whether traded payoffs make the price unique

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Add a third possible terminal stock price, 100, while keeping the traded stock and bank. Must their two current prices determine the price of a claim paying one only at that middle state?" options={[
 {text:'No; two independent traded payoffs can leave one state-price degree of freedom across three states',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; set all three physical probabilities to one third'},
 {text:'Yes; the old two-state formula remains exact without another assumption'},
]} explanation="Two price equations do not generally determine three state prices. A choice of extra probabilities or a new payoff model is extra information, not a consequence of those two prices alone." />

A market is **complete** relative to its modeled states if traded instruments can replicate every state-contingent payoff. Stock and cash span two distinct stock states: any two required payoffs can be matched by the unique straight line $\Delta S+B_0R$. That explains why the two-state claim price is pinned down.

Now allow **three possible year-end stock prices: 80, 100 and 140**. Keep stock price 100 and bank growth 1.10. Let the middle-state price be $t$. Solving only the stock and bank price equations gives a family:

$$
\pi_m=t,\qquad \pi_u=\frac5{11}-\frac t3,\qquad
\pi_d=\frac5{11}-\frac{2t}{3}.
$$

The three state prices sum to 10/11. They reproduce the stock because

$$
80\left(\frac5{11}-\frac{2t}{3}\right)+100t+
140\left(\frac5{11}-\frac t3\right)=100.
$$

The coefficient of t cancels: $-160/3+100-140/3=0$. To keep all three state prices strictly positive, $t>0$; the down-state price requires $t<15/22$, which is tighter than the up-state condition. Thus the admissible family is **$0<t<15/22$**.

| Chosen positive vector | t = 0.10 | t = 0.40 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Down-state price | 0.387879 | 0.187879 |
| Middle-state price | 0.100000 | 0.400000 |
| Up-state price | 0.421212 | 0.321212 |
| Price of a riskless terminal unit | 10/11 | 10/11 |
| Reproduced stock price | 100 | 100 |
| Middle-only unit claim price | 0.10 | 0.40 |

<ThreeStatePriceFamily client:visible />

Both vectors price the two originally traded assets correctly, but the middle-only claim has different prices. It cannot be replicated by stock and cash: a straight-line payoff that is zero at stock 80 and 140 must be zero everywhere, so it cannot also equal one at 100.

The call with strike 100 pays 0/0/40 across these three states. Its selected price is $40\pi_u=200/11-(40/3)t$, not one unique number. As t moves through the open admissible interval, that price ranges over the open interval **$(100/11,200/11)$**. A separately traded independent claim and its price can supply another equation. A modeling choice can also select a valuation, but that is an additional assumption.

**Pitfall:** treating absence of arbitrage as a universal promise of a unique pricing probability. Uniqueness depends on which payoffs are spanned and which information is supplied. Calibrating a model or selecting one consistent vector does not turn its probabilities into measured real-world frequencies.

### When incompleteness matters

When a claim has a payoff that available hedges cannot reproduce, or when a model introduces more relevant states or risks than traded instruments span. Ask what extra quote, dynamic-trading assumption, risk model or preference rule selected the price. The answer should be visible alongside the output.
