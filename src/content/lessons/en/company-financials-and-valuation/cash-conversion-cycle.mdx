A shop can sell at a profit and still struggle to pay its supplier. The goods may sit on a shelf for weeks, and the customer may pay weeks after the sale. The supplier's invoice has its own due date. The **cash conversion cycle (CCC)** brings those three clocks together.

You already know the balance sheet, income statement and operating cash flow. This lesson uses them to estimate how long cash is committed to inventory and customer credit, after allowing for supplier credit. Inventory is goods held for sale; trade receivables are amounts owed by customers; trade payables are amounts owed to suppliers. The examples describe an invented retailer, use a 365-day year and exclude VAT and other sales taxes consistently.

## Inventory days measure the wait before a sale

**Days inventory outstanding (DIO)** estimates the average time inventory remains before being sold. Picture the stockroom as a waiting room: more goods waiting relative to the daily cost of goods sold means a longer implied stay.

$$\text{DIO}=\frac{\text{average inventory}}{\text{annual COGS}}\times365$$

**Cost of goods sold (COGS)** is the cost assigned to goods sold during the period. Inventory is recorded on a cost basis, so use COGS rather than sales revenue in this ratio. With annual COGS divided by 365, the denominator becomes a cost-per-day measure.

### Worked example

Our retailer has average inventory of **€60,000** and annual COGS of **€365,000**:

1. Daily COGS = €365,000 ÷ 365 = **€1,000 per day**.
2. Inventory days = €60,000 ÷ €1,000 per day = **60 days**.
3. If average inventory falls to €50,000 with the same daily COGS, DIO falls to **50 days**.

Assume this reduction comes from carrying fewer goods at unchanged unit costs, with no inventory write-down. It means €10,000 less inventory on average. It releases that amount from this working-capital component under the stated steady-sales assumptions; it does not by itself create €10,000 of profit.

| Average inventory | Daily COGS | DIO |
|---|---|---|
| €60,000 | €1,000 | 60 days |
| €50,000 | €1,000 | 50 days |
| €60,000 | €2,000 | 30 days |

The last row shows why a falling DIO need not mean the stockroom became smaller: faster sales at cost can also lower the ratio.

### When to use it

Compare inventory days with the same business over time and with genuinely similar operations. Too much stock can tie up cash or become obsolete; too little can cause stockouts and lost sales. A write-down can reduce inventory's recorded value without goods selling faster. Read the ratio alongside product mix, stock availability and inventory write-downs.

## Receivable days measure the wait after a sale

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A retailer doubles its credit sales revenue but still collects each customer invoice after the same delay. What should happen to its average receivables under a steady sales pattern?"
  options={[

    { text: 'They should fall roughly by half' },
    { text: 'They should remain unchanged' },
  ]}
  explanation="The same collection delay applied to twice as much credit business leaves roughly twice as much revenue awaiting payment. The monetary balance and the delay are different measures."

**Days sales outstanding (DSO)** estimates how long the business waits to collect credit sales. A **credit sale** lets a customer pay later; it is not a payment by credit card automatically treated as unpaid customer credit. A sale creates revenue and a receivable, while collection turns that receivable into cash.

$$\text{DSO}=\frac{\text{average trade receivables}}{\text{annual credit sales}}\times365$$

Use the relevant **credit sales**, since cash sales do not generate those receivables. If only total revenue is available, state the approximation; a changing cash-versus-credit sales mix can distort the comparison. Keep sales-tax treatment consistent between numerator and denominator. [ACCA, performance appraisal ratios](https://www.accaglobal.com/uk/en/student/exam-support-resources/fundamentals-exams-study-resources/f7/technical-articles/appraisal-questions.html)

### Worked example

Average trade receivables are **€90,000**, and annual credit sales are **€730,000**:

1. Daily credit sales = €730,000 ÷ 365 = **€2,000 per day**.
2. DSO = €90,000 ÷ €2,000 per day = **45 days**.
3. With inventory sold at day 60, the representative collection date is day 60 + 45 = **day 105**.

Suppose collection improves to 35 days with the same daily credit sales. Average receivables become 35 × €2,000 = **€70,000**, a reduction of **€20,000**. This is twice the money released by the ten-day inventory reduction above, because receivables use sales revenue while inventory uses cost.

<DaysOfCashCycle client:visible receivableDays={35}
  title="Collecting ten days earlier"
  caption="Compare collection delay at 35 and 45 days while inventory days stay at 60. Customer collection moves between day 95 and day 105. A sale and the cash collection remain separate events. This is an average-ratio illustration, not a forecast for every invoice."
/>

### When to use it

Use DSO to investigate collections, credit terms and cash tied up in customer balances. The average can hide a cluster of overdue or disputed invoices. An **ageing schedule**, which groups invoices by how long they have been outstanding or overdue, adds information the single ratio misses. A lower DSO achieved by denying sensible credit may also cost sales.

## Payable days measure the supplier financing period

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A supplier agrees to a longer payment term, with everything else unchanged. What happens to the buyer's cash conversion cycle?"
  options={[
    { text: 'It becomes shorter', correct: true },
    { text: 'It becomes longer' },
    { text: 'It cannot change' },
  ]}
  explanation="The supplier finances the goods for longer, delaying the buyer's cash payment. That shortens the gap from payment to customer collection."
/>

**Days payable outstanding (DPO)** estimates the delay before paying trade suppliers. It is a financing clock: the buyer has the goods before cash leaves to settle the related invoice.

$$\text{DPO}=\frac{\text{average trade payables}}{\text{annual credit purchases}}\times365$$

Trade payables belong with the relevant **credit purchases**, not sales revenue and not unrelated borrowing. COGS is often used as a proxy when purchases are unavailable, but purchases and COGS are not identical. For a simple retailer with no inventory write-downs or other stock adjustments:

$$\text{purchases}=\text{COGS}+\text{ending inventory}-\text{beginning inventory}$$

Only the credit portion belongs in the credit-purchases denominator. Manufacturing costs and other accounting adjustments need further reconciliation; do not apply the simple retail identity blindly.

### Worked example

Average trade payables are **€40,000**, and annual credit purchases are **€365,000**. The example retailer buys entirely on credit and has unchanged beginning and ending inventory, so purchases equal COGS here.

1. Daily credit purchases = €365,000 ÷ 365 = **€1,000 per day**.
2. DPO = €40,000 ÷ €1,000 per day = **40 days**.
3. If suppliers agree to 50 days, average payables become 50 × €1,000 = **€50,000**: €10,000 more supplier financing.

For a separate growing retailer, COGS of €365,000 and an inventory increase of €36,500 imply purchases of **€401,500**, assuming no other stock adjustments and all purchases on credit. Using €40,000 payables gives DPO = €40,000 ÷ (€401,500 ÷ 365) ≈ **36.36 days**. Substituting COGS would give 40 days and overstate the actual purchase-based estimate.

### When to use it

Check whether longer DPO reflects agreed terms or unpaid overdue invoices. Stretching suppliers beyond their terms can trigger lost discounts, disrupted supply or demands for cash on delivery. The ratio can improve while the commercial relationship deteriorates. Supplier financing also needs to remain available when sales slow. ## Combine the clocks and interpret the signed gap

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A retailer collects from customers before paying its supplier. What does a negative cash conversion cycle establish?"
  options={[
    { text: 'Collection precedes supplier payment', correct: true },
    { text: 'The business has no liquidity risk' },
    { text: 'Every sale must be unprofitable' },
  ]}
  explanation="A negative cycle describes the order of those operating cash events. It does not settle profitability, debt payments or other cash obligations."
/>

The **operating cycle** runs from acquiring inventory to collecting the sale proceeds. Add inventory days and receivable days. The **cash conversion cycle** subtracts supplier financing:

$$\text{CCC}=\text{DIO}+\text{DSO}-\text{DPO}$$

For our original retailer:

| Clock | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | €60,000 ÷ €365,000 × 365 | 60 days |
| Receivables | €90,000 ÷ €730,000 × 365 | 45 days |
| Payables | €40,000 ÷ €365,000 × 365 | 40 days |
| Operating cycle | 60 + 45 | 105 days |
| Cash conversion cycle | 60 + 45 − 40 | **65 days** |

On the representative timeline, the supplier is paid at day 40 and the customer pays at day 105. The gap is **105 − 40 = 65 days**. The ratios estimate average durations across overlapping business activity; they are not a claim that every item follows exactly that schedule.

### A negative cycle

Consider a cash-sale retailer with DIO **15 days**, DSO **0 days** and agreed DPO **45 days**. CCC = 15 + 0 − 45 = **−30 days**. Collection occurs at day 15, while supplier payment comes at day 45. Customers' cash arrives 30 days earlier.

<DaysOfCashCycle client:visible inventoryDays={15} receivableDays={0} payableDays={45}
  title="Collection can come before supplier payment"
  caption="Here customer cash arrives at day 15 and supplier payment occurs at day 45, giving a signed cycle of −30 days. Increase inventory or collection delays to move the gap through zero. The model omits wages, taxes, debt service and other cash needs."
/>

Negative CCC can support a business, but slowing sales, excess inventory or shorter supplier terms can reverse the timing advantage. Cash owed to suppliers also remains a liability. A negative cycle is not a solvency certificate.

### Days and cash amounts use different bases

For the original retailer, average operating working capital in these three accounts is €60,000 inventory + €90,000 receivables − €40,000 payables = **€110,000**. Multiplying CCC of 65 days by daily COGS of €1,000 gives €65,000, which does **not** reproduce €110,000. Receivables are on a sales basis of €2,000 per day, while inventory and the stated purchases are on €1,000-per-day bases.

Calculate an estimated cash release one component at a time. Ten fewer inventory days release about €10,000; ten fewer receivable days release about €20,000; ten more agreed payable days provide about €10,000 of financing. These illustrations hold the relevant daily flows constant and ignore other effects. They are not three interchangeable euro amounts merely because all three changes shorten CCC by ten days.

### When to use it

Use CCC to locate delays and build questions for a cash forecast, then check the underlying invoices, inventory and payment agreements. Compare like businesses and consistent periods. Beginning-and-ending averages are a rough estimate; monthly or daily averages better represent seasonal peaks when available. A year-end stock reduction can flatter DIO while hiding months of cash tied up earlier in the year. The measure also omits wages, capital expenditure, taxes and debt service. [CFA Institute, working capital and liquidity](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/working-capital-and-liquidity)
