A company earns €100 a year after operating tax. Management can keep the business steady or reinvest €40 to expand it. That expansion might earn €6 more each year. It might earn only €2. Both announcements contain the word “growth”; the cash committed is the same.

The useful question is what the new operating profit earns on the capital that produces it. We will connect that return to cash flow and value, with dates written down. All companies and figures below are invented. A model result is conditional on its forecasts, rather than a promised return on a share.

## Operating profit and cash available are different amounts

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A business earns €100 of after-tax operating profit and reinvests €40 net in operations. How much operating free cash flow remains under the stated bridge?"
  options={[{text:'€60',correct:true},{text:'€100, because reinvestment is still an asset'},{text:'€140, by adding all investment'},{text:'€40, the amount spent'}]}
  explanation="Net operating reinvestment uses cash that is therefore unavailable for distribution in that period: 100 − 40 = 60. Its future benefits must be forecast separately."
/>

**NOPAT** means *net operating profit after tax*: operating profit after the tax attributable to operations, before financing costs such as interest. In a simple case, operating profit before interest and tax (**EBIT**) is €125 and its operating tax rate is 20%:

$$
\text{NOPAT}=125(1-0.20)=100.
$$

This simplified tax calculation assumes that rate applies to operating profit; actual cash taxes, losses and accounting adjustments can require a fuller reconciliation.

**Net operating reinvestment** adds investment in long-lived operating assets and non-cash operating working capital, after allowing for depreciation and amortisation (**D&A**):

$$
I=\text{capital expenditure}-\text{D\&A}+\Delta\text{operating working capital},\qquad
\text{FCFF}=\text{NOPAT}-I.
$$

Capital expenditure buys or improves long-lived assets. D&A allocates their cost over accounting periods. Operating working capital here includes items such as inventory and customer receivables less non-interest-bearing operating payables; it excludes excess cash and financing debt. A positive increase ties up additional cash. This is where the [cash conversion cycle](/company-financials-and-valuation/cash-conversion-cycle) can affect a growth forecast.

| Annual bridge | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| NOPAT | €100 |
| Capital expenditure | €45 |
| Less D&A | −€20 |
| Increase in operating working capital | +€15 |
| Net reinvestment: 45 − 20 + 15 | €40 |
| Free cash flow to the firm: 100 − 40 | €60 |

**FCFF**, free cash flow to the firm, is operating cash available to the business's capital providers after operating reinvestment, before financing distributions. It is not necessarily the cash dividend. The [CFA Institute overview](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/free-cash-flow-valuation) distinguishes FCFF from equity cash flows and notes that analysts must calculate them from financial information.

Calling a cash-flow-statement subtotal “operating cash flow” does not automatically make that subtotal minus capital expenditure equal to FCFF. Check interest, tax and accounting classifications before combining a cash-flow measure with a cost of capital.

### When to use this bridge

Use it to reconcile an earnings forecast with the investment and cash collection needed to deliver it. The ratio $k=I/\text{NOPAT}$ is an **operating reinvestment rate**: here $40/100=40\%$. It differs from the fraction of net income retained after dividends. Retained earnings might fund excess cash or debt repayment; operating investment can also be financed externally.

## New capital must arrive before its new profit

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="The first year's NOPAT is already forecast at €100. A €40 investment made at the end of that year earns 15% annually from year 2. What is year 2 NOPAT if existing profit is maintained?"
  options={[{text:'€106: add 40 × 15% = €6',correct:true},{text:'€115: apply 15% to all €100'},{text:'€140: treat the investment as profit'},{text:'€94: subtract the return from profit'}]}
  explanation="The 15% return applies to the €40 of new capital. Its €6 annual operating profit begins in year 2, giving 100 + 6 = 106."
/>

A bakery does not get next year's extra bread merely by writing “grow 6%” in a spreadsheet. It needs the ovens, stock and payment terms that can produce and collect those sales. **Incremental ROIC**, return on new invested capital, relates the additional after-tax operating profit to the capital needed for it.

Set **year 1 NOPAT at €100**, already given. Reinvest $I_1=40$ **at the end of year 1**. Let the new capital earn an annual after-tax return $r_{\text{new}}=15\%$ from year 2, with existing operations maintained:

$$
\Delta\text{NOPAT}_2=I_1r_{\text{new}}=40(0.15)=6,\qquad
\text{NOPAT}_2=100+6=106.
$$

Since $I_1=k\text{NOPAT}_1$, the resulting annual growth rate is

$$
g=\frac{\Delta\text{NOPAT}_2}{\text{NOPAT}_1}=kr_{\text{new}}=0.40(0.15)=0.06.
$$

This is a deliberately specific model: the return starts next year, persists, and applies to the new investment; old profit is maintained. At constant $k$ and incremental return, year 2 reinvestment is $106(0.40)=42.40$, so year 3 NOPAT is $106+42.40(0.15)=112.36$. Cash available in year 2 is $106-42.40=63.60$.

[Damodaran's growth framework](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/valquestions/growth.htm) connects operating growth with reinvestment and return on capital. The simple product requires stable operating assumptions; it is not an identity that explains every observed change in earnings.

If year 1 reinvestment were €20 at the same 15% new-capital return, additional year 2 NOPAT would be 20 × 0.15 = €3. Growth would be 3/100 = 3%, and first-year FCFF would be 100 − 20 = €80. A higher cash payout today comes with less growth in this model.

### When to use the growth relation

Use it to check whether the reinvestment in a forecast can support its expected operating growth. Put investment dates and operating lags into a detailed project model if new factories take three years to open. Applying a current return immediately to today's investment would advance the profits incorrectly.

## Economic profit charges for the capital already committed

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An operation earns €80 NOPAT on €1,000 of opening operating capital. Its annual cost of capital is 10%. What is its economic profit under this convention?"
  options={[{text:'−€20: 80 − 0.10 × 1,000',correct:true},{text:'€80, because accounting profit is positive'},{text:'€180, by adding the capital charge'},{text:'10%, because economic profit is a rate'}]}
  explanation="The €100 opportunity-cost charge exceeds the €80 operating profit. Economic profit is an amount, −€20; operating ROIC is the separate rate 80/1,000 = 8%."
/>

**Invested operating capital** is capital committed to operations: operating assets less non-interest-bearing operating liabilities, measured consistently. Excess cash is not operating capital in this convention, and borrowing is a financing source rather than an operating payable.

For this section, use **beginning-of-year invested capital**, $IC_{t-1}$. Other analyses use average capital or make accounting adjustments; identify the convention before comparing ratios. **ROIC** for the period is $\text{NOPAT}_t/IC_{t-1}$.

The **weighted average cost of capital**, $w$ or WACC, is the required return for the firm's financing mix and operating risk, matched to these after-tax operating cash flows. It is an opportunity cost. **Economic profit** is operating profit after a charge for that capital:

$$
\text{EP}_t=\text{NOPAT}_t-wIC_{t-1}
=(\text{ROIC}_t-w)IC_{t-1}.
$$

With €1,000 opening capital, €150 NOPAT and 10% WACC, ROIC is $150/1{,}000=15\%$. The capital charge is $0.10(1{,}000)=100$, giving economic profit $150-100=50$. If NOPAT is €80 instead, ROIC is 8% and economic profit is $80-100=-20$.

The charge is not an extra cash invoice. It measures what capital providers require for bearing risk. Subtracting it from FCFF and then discounting that reduced FCFF at WACC would count the required return twice. [ACCA's economic value added explanation](https://www.accaglobal.com/uk/en/student/exam-support-resources/professional-exams-study-resources/p4/technical-articles/economic-value-added.html) develops this opportunity-cost approach and the accounting adjustments that practical measures may need.

The same charge can be applied to a new investment. Reinvesting €40 at the end of year 1 produces €6 of additional annual NOPAT from year 2 at a 15% return. At 10% WACC, the annual charge on that €40 is €4. Incremental annual economic profit is €6 − €4 = €2. Compare the two bars, then move the new-capital return across the cost of capital.

### When economic profit helps

Use it to question whether a division earns enough for the resources committed to it. Economic profit for one period is a performance measure, not a complete company valuation. A recently opened plant can have low current profit while later forecast cash flows still justify its investment. The project [NPV and IRR lesson](/company-financials-and-valuation/irr-and-conflicting-project-rankings) provides the dated cash-flow comparison.

## Growing earnings can raise, preserve or reduce value

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A new €40 investment earns €2 of extra annual operating profit forever from next year. At a matched 10% cost of capital, what is its NPV at the investment date?"
  options={[{text:'−€20: 2/0.10 − 40',correct:true},{text:'+€2, because profit grows'},{text:'+€40, because the investment becomes an asset'},{text:'€0, because every positive return is sufficient'}]}
  explanation="The €2 annual perpetuity is worth €20 at that date. Paying €40 for it gives NPV 20 − 40 = −€20 under these assumptions."
/>

Keep the new investment date separate from today. A €40 investment at the end of year 1 that earns €6 annually from year 2 has, **at the end of year 1**, value $6/0.10=60$ before its €40 cost, so project NPV is €20. At date 0 that NPV is $20/1.10=18.18$. A 15% new-capital return exceeds the 10% required return. At a 5% return, profit is only €2, its value is €20 and the investment-date NPV is −€20.

Now compare whole-business policies. Fix the same **first-year NOPAT of €100**, annual WACC of 10% and reinvestment share of 40%. Each policy maintains the existing business and reinvests at every year-end; the chosen new-capital return lasts forever. Since FCFF grows at $g=kr_{\text{new}}$,

$$
V_0=\frac{\text{FCFF}_1}{w-g}
=\frac{\text{NOPAT}_1(1-k)}{w-kr_{\text{new}}},\qquad g<w.
$$

The numerator is **year 1** FCFF, not an extra-grown year 2 figure. Without growth and with no net reinvestment, the maintained operation produces €100 a year, worth $100/0.10=1{,}000$.

| New-capital return | Growth: 40% × return | First-year FCFF | Operating value at date 0 | Versus €1,000 no-growth value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 15% | 6% | €60 | 60/(0.10 − 0.06) = €1,500 | +€500 |
| 10% | 4% | €60 | 60/(0.10 − 0.04) = €1,000 | €0 |
| 5% | 2% | €60 | 60/(0.10 − 0.02) = €750 | −€250 |

The middle policy grows earnings while creating no additional value relative to the maintained no-growth alternative. New projects earn exactly their required return. In the bottom policy, reinvestment funds earnings growth whose future cash is worth less than its cost.

Increase the reinvestment share: annual profit growth rises, but operating value falls relative to the maintained no-growth alternative. These operating values exclude excess cash and financing claims. They are not share prices or total shareholder returns. A market price could already reflect a better growth forecast than yours. Value creation relative to an operating alternative does not establish that a traded share is cheap.

### When this comparison is useful

Use it to challenge a forecast that raises growth without funding it, or a target that rewards expansion regardless of return. It deliberately holds risk and the first-year earnings base constant. Different business risks, project lives, shutdown costs or funding constraints need a fuller comparison; do not substitute a reported ROIC spread for a complete project NPV.

## Historical returns and retained earnings do not identify the next opportunity

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Old operating assets earn 20%, but a proposed expansion is expected to earn only 7% at a 10% matched cost of capital. Which return matters for that new investment decision?"
  options={[{text:'The expansion’s forecast cash flows and 7% incremental return',correct:true},{text:'Always the old 20%, applied unchanged to all future spending'},{text:'The fraction of net income retained after dividends alone'},{text:'Whichever historical rate makes growth fastest'}]}
  explanation="A strong existing business can have weak new opportunities. Historical average returns may inform the forecast, but do not establish the return on the next investment."
/>

A mature store might earn €200 on €1,000 of opening operating capital: average operating ROIC is 20%. A new location costing €100 may earn only €7 per year. Its incremental return is 7%, even though the old stores still look excellent. At 10% required return, the new location's perpetuity has value $7/0.10=70$ and NPV $70-100=-30$ at its investment date.

Growth can also come from existing assets. Suppose profit rises from €100 to €110 by using spare capacity more efficiently, with no net new investment that year. Growth is $(110-100)/100=10\%$ while $k=0$. That does not refute the earlier calculation: its maintained-existing-profit assumption no longer holds. [Damodaran's growth value drivers](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/littlebook/growthvaluedrivers.htm) explicitly separates efficiency growth and discusses investment lags and capacity already installed. A temporary improvement cannot be repeated without limit.

Accounting measurement also matters. Expensing research immediately, capitalising it and then amortising it, or writing down old assets can change reported profit and capital. Consistent adjustments can improve comparison; picking whichever denominator gives a flattering ROIC cannot.

### When to look beyond the simple product

Use separate forecasts when margins are recovering, new capacity is still idle or projects have long lead times. A reinvestment rate can exceed 100% with external financing or be negative when assets and working capital are released. If NOPAT is near zero or negative, its reinvestment-rate ratio becomes unstable or unhelpful. Forecast explicit operating cash flows instead of forcing those cases through this lesson's positive-profit simulator.

## A perpetual formula has a boundary and a time convention

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A constant-policy model gives growth of 10% and a cost of capital of 10%, with positive first-year FCFF. What should a growing-perpetuity calculation report?"
  options={[{text:'No finite value under that perpetual specification',correct:true},{text:'Zero value, since the two rates cancel'},{text:'A negative but valid business value'},{text:'The five-year cash-flow sum as the complete business value'}]}
  explanation="The discounted positive payments do not sum to a finite amount when perpetual growth reaches or exceeds the discount rate. A finite forecast remains calculable, but it does not include everything after its end."
/>

With $r_{\text{new}}=20\%$ and $k=50\%$, growth is $0.20(0.50)=10\%$. At 10% WACC, first-year FCFF is €50 and

$$
\frac{\text{FCFF}_t}{(1+w)^t}
=\frac{50(1.10)^{t-1}}{(1.10)^t}
=\frac{50}{1.10}
$$

for every year. Five such payments have present value $5(50/1.10)=227.27$. Infinitely many do not have a finite sum. Reporting a negative value when growth exceeds WACC would misuse the formula outside its convergence domain.

<ReinvestmentGrowthValue client:visible incrementalRoic={0.2} reinvestmentRate={0.5} title="The forecast still works when the perpetuity does not" />

A finite horizon and a terminal value answer different questions. Five discounted FCFF payments omit all later cash flows and any disposal or liquidation proceeds. In a multistage valuation, move to an economically supportable stable phase, specify its return and reinvestment requirements, and apply its terminal value **at the forecast endpoint** before discounting it to today. Changing only terminal growth while leaving investment unchanged can violate the operating story.

Even within the valid domain, small assumptions matter. With first-year FCFF €60 and WACC 10%, perpetual growth of 5.9%, 6.0% and 6.1% gives values $60/0.041=1{,}463.41$, $60/0.040=1{,}500$ and $60/0.039=1{,}538.46$. This local mathematical sensitivity holds the cash-flow numerator fixed; a full operating scenario must also reconcile the growth, return and reinvestment assumptions that produce it.

### When to stop trusting the single number

Use scenario ranges and dated forecasts when return advantages fade, competitive entry changes margins or high growth lasts only a few years. Match currency, inflation basis, annual periods, taxes and financing perspective across cash flows and discount rates. Growth, incremental return and WACC are estimates; decimal places do not make them observed facts.
