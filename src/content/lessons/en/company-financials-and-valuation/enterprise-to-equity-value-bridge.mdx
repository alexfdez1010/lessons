An operating DCF values a business at **€1,000 million**. There are **50 million ordinary shares**. Dividing those two numbers gives €20. It also gives all the operating value to ordinary shareholders before asking who else owns a claim on it.

After adding excluded assets and removing the other claims in our worked case, the common-equity pool is **€730 million**, or **€14.60 per current share before existing option allocation**. The arithmetic is simple; deciding which claims belong in it does most of the work.

All companies, amounts and security terms below are invented. Values refer to the **same valuation date**, currency and stated business perimeter. We extend the [DCF lesson](/company-financials-and-valuation/dcf-valuation-step-by-step); we do not revalue its different company or reuse its forecast.

## Define the operating value before building the bridge

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An operating DCF excludes excess cash and values cash available before financing distributions. Is its €1,000 million result already the value of the parent’s ordinary shares?" options={[
 {text:'No. Excluded assets and claims belonging to others still need reconciliation',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes. Every euro of operating value belongs to ordinary shareholders'},
 {text:'Yes, provided the number is divided by any reported EPS denominator'},
]} explanation="The starting value describes the assets and cash flows forecast. Shareholders receive a residual claim, not automatic ownership of debt, preferred claims or the interests of outside subsidiary owners." />

**Operating asset value**, written $V_{op}$, is the present value of the operations represented in the forecast. Here it comes from **free cash flow to the firm**, FCFF, discounted at a matching **weighted average cost of capital**, WACC. FCFF is before financing distributions. **Common equity** is the claim of ordinary shareholders after the other relevant claims have been allocated.

The [CFA Institute's FCFF and FCFE explanation](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/free-cash-flow-valuation) distinguishes valuing equity directly from common-equity cash flow and reaching it indirectly from a firm-level valuation. You cannot switch between those perspectives halfway through a calculation.

For our explicitly defined perimeter,

$$
E_{pool}=V_{op}+C_{excess}+A_{excluded}-D-P-NCI-L.
$$

$C_{excess}$ is separately valued excess cash; $A_{excluded}$ is other non-operating asset value omitted from the forecast. $D$ is financing debt value, $P$ preferred equity value, $NCI$ outside owners’ interests in consolidated subsidiaries and $L$ other relevant claims not already reflected in operating cash deductions. $E_{pool}$ is the parent common-equity pool **before allocating the existing employee options introduced later**.

**Enterprise value**, EV, is used with several conventions in practice. A quoted-market recipe often adds financing claims to market capitalisation and subtracts specified cash. This lesson starts with a defined operating DCF perimeter: required operating cash is already inside that perimeter; only excluded excess cash is added. Reconcile definitions before comparing two figures both called EV.

### Work through every signed item

All amounts in this bridge are **millions of euros**; share quantities are **millions of shares**.

| Item | Change | Running amount |
|---|---:|---:|
| Operating assets in the DCF | +1,000 | 1,000 |
| Excluded excess cash | +80 | 1,080 |
| Separately valued excluded investment | +60 | 1,140 |
| Financing debt claim | −300 | 840 |
| Preferred equity claim | −50 | 790 |
| Outside subsidiary owners | −40 | 750 |
| Other claim excluded from the operating forecast | −20 | 730 |

Thus $E_{pool}=1{,}000+80+60-300-50-40-20=730$. With 50 current shares and temporarily no existing options, $730/50=14.60$ euros per share. This is a conditional model value, not a promise about the next exchange quote or a buyer's transaction price.

### When to use the bridge

Use it after a whole-business operating valuation or before constructing a matching enterprise multiple. Write down what the starting value includes. If you instead discounted FCFE after the relevant financing claims, subtracting the same debt value again would charge equity twice.

## Add only assets outside the operating forecast

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Total cash is 120. Required operating cash of 40 is already represented in the operating valuation, and the remaining 80 is separately recoverable at its stated value. What cash addition matches that perimeter?" options={[
 {text:'80, subject to the stated value and access assumptions',correct:true},
 {text:'120, then another 80 because it is excess'},
 {text:'Zero, because cash can never belong to shareholders'},
]} explanation="120 − 40 = 80 is excluded excess cash in this example. Adding the 40 already included in operations would duplicate value. A different starting definition can require a different adjustment." />

Think of valuing a bakery and then checking a separate investment account. If the operating forecast already includes the ovens, adding their purchase cost again does not create another bakery. The same check applies to operating cash, investments and property.

In our example, total cash is **120**, of which **40** is needed for operations and already reflected in $V_{op}$. Excluded excess cash is $120-40=80$. Interest income on that excluded cash is also omitted from the operating forecast. Otherwise the analyst must remove the income or reconcile the capitalised asset value before adding the cash separately.

Our investment value of **60** represents the parent's **30% ownership of another company's equity worth 200**, with no additional valuation adjustments: $0.30(200)=60$. It is an unconsolidated investment excluded from operating FCFF. Adding the investee's full enterprise value, rather than the owned equity interest, would import other owners’ assets and financing claims.

[Damodaran's NYU explanation of reaching value per share](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/New_Home_Page/littlebook/valuepershare.htm) makes the inclusion question explicit: cash and cross-holdings need separate treatment when their value has not already entered the cash-flow model.

### Access, taxes and dates can change the addition

Do not classify every restricted amount as worthless or every cash balance as freely distributable. Suppose, separately, 50 of the excess 80 is usable now and 30 is recoverable only at the end of year 3. Under an invented **5% annual discount rate appropriate to that claim**, no intervening income and no other loss,

$$
C_{excess}=50+\frac{30}{1.05^3}\approx75.9151.
$$

The revised pool is $730-80+75.9151\approx725.9151$. The rate is an assumption for this dated cash claim, not automatically the business's WACC. If the 30 also earned and paid interest, those receipts would need valuation too.

Alternatively, if a separately established recovery tax or cost had present value 20 and was absent from all other forecasts, the original 80 addition would become 60 and the pool 710. This is a different scenario from the delayed receipt; do not stack both adjustments unless both actually apply.

### When to investigate the asset separately

Use this analysis for surplus cash, investment stakes, unused property and other assets absent from the operating stream. Check ownership, restrictions, taxes, valuation date and any income already forecast. A book carrying amount is a starting disclosure, not automatic evidence of current recoverable value.

## Match each financing or other claim to the forecast

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A pension-related payment stream with present value 20 has already reduced the operating DCF from 1,000 to 980. Should the bridge also subtract that same 20 as a separate claim?" options={[
 {text:'No. That would count the same economic cost twice',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes. Any number described as a liability must be subtracted again'},
 {text:'No. Pension obligations can never affect equity value'},
]} explanation="The obligation matters once. Either the relevant payments reduce the operating forecast or an excluded claim is valued separately; ensure the two treatments describe the same economic case." />

**Debt value** is the value of the financing claim on the valuation date, not necessarily its accounting balance or the principal printed on the bond. In our invented case, debt has book carrying amount 320, contractual principal 330 and an estimated market claim value of **300**. We use 300 because that is the claim value matched to the valuation. Replacing it with 330 reduces the pool to 700; that is a changed assumption, not a rounding correction. An actual acquisition may require a different contractual payoff amount.

**Preferred equity** has rights different from ordinary shares. Here it is a non-convertible claim separately valued at **50** and excluded from the common pool. Its legal seniority, dividends, conversion and redemption terms require examination; the word “preferred” is not enough to value it.

The **other claim of 20** is a separately estimated present value of a pension shortfall that is absent from the operating forecast. We do not claim every reported pension number equals a cash payment today. The same-value alternatives are

$$
1{,}000+140-300-50-40-20=730
$$

and, if the same 20 cost is already reflected in operations,

$$
980+140-300-50-40=730.
$$

Using operating value 980 **and** subtracting 20 again would produce 710 by duplicating the obligation.

### Ordinary operating liabilities are not automatically extra deductions

The bakery owes a supplier 100. Its operating forecast already models the related purchases, payments and working capital. Subtracting 100 again solely because it appears under “liabilities” can duplicate a cost. Examine leases, provisions, customer advances and deferred taxes in the same way: their treatment depends on the forecast, discount rate and claim definition, not on placing every liability into a debt bucket.

Borrowing is also a transaction with two sides. If the company borrows 50 and still holds all 50 as excluded cash, the static bridge has debt 350 and cash 130:

$$
1{,}000+130+60-350-50-40-20=730.
$$

Borrowing has not created 50 of common-equity value in this simple no-cost identity. Changing financing can nevertheless alter risk, taxes, costs and operating decisions; this arithmetic holds operating value fixed and is not a leverage policy.

### When to use a claim schedule

Create a dated schedule of claims with amount, valuation basis, priority, related cash flows and where each has already been recognised. Keep ordinary operating payables separate from financing debt. Reconcile net debt to its components: subtracting debt net of cash and then adding the same cash again also counts cash twice.

## Remove outside subsidiary ownership only from the matching perimeter

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A consolidated subsidiary has operating value 250, debt 50 and no other assets or claims. The parent owns 80% of its ordinary equity. What value belongs to outside owners in this model?" options={[
 {text:'40: 20% of the subsidiary equity value of 200',correct:true},
 {text:'50: 20% of its operating value of 250'},
 {text:'200, because the parent owns the whole business for accounting purposes'},
]} explanation="Subsidiary equity is 250 − 50 = 200. Outside owners hold 20% of that equity, so NCI is 40. Consolidating operations does not give the parent every ownership claim." />

A **subsidiary** is an entity controlled by a parent. **Consolidation** presents the group as one economic entity; it is not the same as owning every ordinary share. **Non-controlling interests**, NCI, are the subsidiary equity interests belonging to outside owners. The [IFRS Foundation's IFRS 10 overview](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ifrs-10-consolidated-financial-statements/) bases consolidation on control. A share percentage alone does not settle every control assessment.

Assume our operating DCF includes **100%** of a controlled subsidiary's operating cash flows. Parent operations are worth 750 and subsidiary operations 250, giving group operating value 1,000. Parent debt is 250 and subsidiary debt 50, giving group debt 300. There are no other subsidiary assets or claims in this simple example.

The subsidiary equity is $250-50=200$. The parent's 80% interest is $0.80(200)=160$; outside owners’ 20% interest is $0.20(200)=40$. The **40 is a valuation estimate**, not automatically the book NCI balance. A book balance of 30 would not justify silently giving the remaining 10 to parent shareholders.

### Reconcile the two routes

| Adjustment | Consolidated route | Parent operations plus owned subsidiary equity |
|---|---:|---:|
| Operating value included at the start | 1,000 | 750 |
| Parent's owned subsidiary equity added | 0 | +160 |
| Excluded cash and investment | +140 | +140 |
| Debt removed | −300 | −250 |
| Preferred claim | −50 | −50 |
| Outside subsidiary equity removed | −40 | 0 |
| Other excluded claim | −20 | −20 |
| Parent common-equity pool | 730 | 730 |

The second route has already reduced subsidiary operating value by its own debt and kept only the parent's share. Deducting the subsidiary debt or NCI again would be wrong. Conversely, using all 1,000 of operating value and omitting NCI would overstate the parent pool by 40.

### When the perimeter check matters

Use it with group accounts, sum-of-parts valuations and enterprise multiples. If an EV numerator includes all of a consolidated subsidiary, the EBITDA denominator must use the corresponding perimeter. An unconsolidated investment you **own** is an asset to add when excluded; an outside owner's interest in a subsidiary you **consolidate** is a claim to remove. The similar words describe opposite sides of ownership.

## Allocate existing options before reporting value per share

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Before existing options, the equity pool is 730 million and there are 50 million current shares. Ten million options can be exercised immediately for €10 each. If all are exercised in the stated model, which numerator and denominator belong together?" options={[
 {text:'(730 + 100)/60: add exercise cash and the issued shares',correct:true},
 {text:'730/60, ignoring the exercise cash'},
 {text:'(730 − 100)/50, treating cash paid into the company as a cost'},
]} explanation="Ten million options at €10 bring in €100 million and issue ten million shares. The model must still verify that exercise is worthwhile; it assumes immediate exercise, no costs or taxes, and no remaining time value." />

A **share** is a unit of an equity claim. For valuation at one date, use the current ownership claims and share count **at that date**. If the pool is 730 and current shares are 50, $730/50=14.60$ before the existing options. Dividing instead by a historical weighted average of 48 would give $730/48\approx15.2083$, answering neither the current ownership allocation nor a coherent EPS question.

**Earnings per share**, EPS, divides an accounting period's attributed earnings by the matching period-weighted denominator. [IAS 33's official overview](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-33-earnings-per-share.html/) explains basic and diluted EPS and their reconciliations. The EPS weighted average is not automatically today's valuation denominator. A reported diluted EPS denominator also follows its own instrument and dilution rules; it is not a universal instruction to count every imaginable future share in a DCF.

### An immediate-exercise model with every cash movement shown

An **option** grants a right to buy shares at an **exercise price**, or strike. Let $N=50$ million current shares, $M=10$ million options and $K=10$ euros per share. The pool of 730 is before allocating those existing options. Assume all are available to exercise **now**, with no taxes, costs or remaining time value. If exercise is worthwhile, the post-exercise price is

$$
P=\frac{E_{pool}+MK}{N+M}
=\frac{730+10(10)}{50+10}
=\frac{830}{60}\approx13.8333.
$$

The result is above the strike of 10, consistent with exercise. Option holders pay 100 and receive ten million shares worth approximately 138.3333. Their **net intrinsic claim** is approximately $138.3333-100=38.3333$ million. Current holders retain $50(13.8333)\approx691.6667$ million.

The identical claim-subtraction representation is

$$
\frac{730-38.3333}{50}\approx13.8333.
$$

Use either the exercise cash and new shares, **or** the net claim deduction and current shares. Subtracting that claim and then using all 60 shares while omitting the exercise cash would allocate the same option cost again.

<OptionExerciseBridge client:visible />

The algebra can also express the same immediate-exercise result with an equivalent incremental count $M(1-K/P)$. Here it is approximately $10(1-10/(830/60))\approx2.771084$ million, giving $730/52.771084\approx13.8333$. This is an algebraic representation of our current-value model, not a claim that this is the company's reported IAS 33 denominator.

### Existing unexpired options need their own value

If the strike were 20, immediate exercise would not be worthwhile and this **exercise-now** model would leave 14.60 per share. An unexpired option could still have time value; “not exercised now” does not prove “worth zero”.

Suppose a separate, internally consistent option valuation assigns the existing options a **full net claim value of 45**. Then current ordinary holders receive $(730-45)/50=13.70$ per share under that different valuation. Do not also add 100 of exercise proceeds or replace 50 by 60: the valued option claim already includes its exercise economics. Damodaran's NYU explanation treats existing employee options as a separate claim before dividing by actual outstanding shares.

### When to inspect the capitalisation table

Check current ordinary classes, treasury shares, options, warrants and convertible instruments. A conversion scenario must remove the corresponding old claim if it issues shares in its place; keeping both charges the same security twice. Forecast future employee compensation consistently as well: an existing option overhang and future grants are not automatically the same cost or the same shares.

## Audit the bridge in both directions

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="The defined common pool is 730, debt 300, preferred 50, NCI 40, other excluded claims 20, excess cash 80 and excluded investments 60. What operating value reverses this same bridge?" options={[
 {text:'1,000: 730 + 300 + 50 + 40 + 20 − 80 − 60',correct:true},
 {text:'1,280, by adding the excluded assets again'},
 {text:'730, because common equity and operating value must be identical'},
]} explanation="Reverse each sign. A bridge that does not reconcile back to its starting value has a definition, perimeter or arithmetic problem." />

An **audit trail** records where each value came from and where it has already entered the model. A bridge is more useful when a reviewer can reverse it:

$$
V_{op}=E_{pool}+D+P+NCI+L-C_{excess}-A_{excluded}
=730+300+50+40+20-80-60=1{,}000.
$$

Suppose matching consolidated EBITDA, operating earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, is 100. The defined operating multiple is $1{,}000/100=10$ times. If you use only parent EBITDA of 80 with that consolidated numerator, you obtain 12.5 times from mismatched ownership coverage. A different company or multiple can also define leases, cash and non-operating assets differently; labels alone do not establish comparability.

### Test whether a cost was charged twice

| Economic item | One coherent treatment | A double-counting warning |
|---|---|---|
| Excluded investment | Remove its stream and add its separate value | Capitalise its stream and add its whole value again |
| Pension payment stream | Model it in operations or deduct an excluded claim | Lower operations for it and deduct it again |
| Outside subsidiary owners | Remove NCI after valuing the full consolidated subsidiary | Add only the owned equity stake and still deduct NCI |
| Existing option | Add exercise cash and shares, or remove its net claim | Deduct its claim and also apply the full exercise dilution |
| Financing cash | Use gross debt and excess cash, or a consistent net amount | Subtract debt already net of cash and add that cash again |

### A negative subtraction is a diagnostic, not a negative share quote

If operating value in the first model fell to zero with every other input held fixed, the arithmetic residual would be $0+140-410=-270$. Dividing by 50 gives −5.40 mechanically, but it is not a meaningful negative exchange price for a fully paid limited-liability ordinary share. Distressed equity, claim recoveries, priority and optionality need an appropriate model; treating every senior claim as fully payable while operating value collapses can itself be inconsistent. The bridge exposes the problem rather than resolving it by hiding the negative residual.

### When to stop and rebuild the model

Stop when the perimeter cannot be reconciled, a key claim has no defensible value, access assumptions conflict with the forecast, or the result depends on a distressed payoff structure the model does not represent. A longer list of adjustments is not automatically more accurate. Keep only claims and assets that belong to this valuation, dated consistently and counted once.
