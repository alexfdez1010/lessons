A spreadsheet reports “free cash flow: €52 million”. Before discounting it, ask who that cash is available to and which financing costs have already been deducted. The answer can change both the discount rate and the value you calculate.

A **discounted cash flow**, or DCF, estimates value from future cash flows and explicit assumptions about their timing and risk. We will build a whole-business operating valuation, then reach common equity. Every company, rate and amount below is invented. A model can give a precise conditional answer without making its forecasts certain.

## Decide whose cash flows the model values

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="You forecast cash available to all financing providers before debt payments. Which valuation pairing is consistent?" options={[{text:'FCFF discounted at a matched weighted average cost of capital',correct:true},{text:'Cash after interest and debt repayments discounted at WACC without adjustment'},{text:'Revenue discounted at the loan interest rate'},{text:'Net income multiplied by the number of forecast years'}]} explanation="Cash flow to the firm and cash flow to common equity describe different claims. Match the cash-flow perspective with the required return before adding any numbers." />

**Free cash flow to the firm**, FCFF, is operating cash available to the providers of capital after operating reinvestment, before financing distributions. It excludes new borrowing, debt principal repayments, financing interest and dividends. Discounting it at a compatible **weighted average cost of capital**, WACC, estimates the value of the operating assets represented by those cash flows.

**Free cash flow to equity**, FCFE, instead measures cash available to common shareholders after relevant financing flows. It is discounted at the **cost of equity**, the required return for that equity risk. The [CFA Institute's free-cash-flow valuation summary](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/free-cash-flow-valuation) sets out both approaches and the need to interpret the financial statements before calculating either measure.

In a simplified one-period case with FCFF €60, interest €10, a fully usable 20% tax deduction and net new borrowing €5,

$$
\text{FCFE}=\text{FCFF}-\text{interest}(1-t)+\text{net borrowing}
= 60-10(0.80)+ 5= 57.
$$

Net borrowing means borrowing received minus principal repaid. It changes equity cash without creating extra operating profit. Discounting the €57 as if it were the same firm's €60 FCFF mixes the financing perspectives.

### The rate must match the stream

A simple WACC with market-value equity weight $E/(D+E)$, debt weight $D/(D+E)$, required equity return $r_e$ and debt return $r_d$ is

$$
w=\frac{E}{D+E}r_e+\frac{D}{D+E}r_d(1-t).
$$

With 70% equity at 10%, 30% debt at 5% and a fully usable 20% interest tax deduction, $w= 0.70(0.10)+ 0.30(0.05)(0.80)= 8.2\%$. The tax adjustment assumes the deduction is available; a firm with tax losses, changing debt or material interest limits may require a fuller method. The formula does not mean adding debt always reduces the required return: changing financing can change the risks and component costs too.

For the separate worked operating DCF below, assume an agreed **10% annual WACC**. Do not silently carry over 8.2%. Rates and cash flows must match currency, inflation basis, tax treatment, risk and timing. A forecast in nominal euros needs a nominal euro rate; a real forecast excludes inflation and needs a real rate.

### When to choose the perspective

Choose it before forecasting. This lesson uses a non-financial operating business. Banks and similar businesses can make debt, interest and working capital part of their operating product, requiring a more suitable equity or sector-specific framework. A single WACC is also an assumption, not a rule that all years and business segments share identical risk.

## Build FCFF from operating profit and reinvestment

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="EBIT is €125, operating tax is 20%, D&A €20, capex €45 and the increase in operating working capital €15. What is FCFF?" options={[{text:'€60',correct:true},{text:'€100, because after-tax profit is already cash'},{text:'€140, by adding all investment'},{text:'€52, after also deducting debt interest without a financing adjustment'}]} explanation="NOPAT = 125 × 0.80 = 100; FCFF = 100 + 20 − 45 − 15 = 60. Interest is not deducted from this operating cash stream." />

**EBIT** is operating earnings before interest and tax. **NOPAT**, net operating profit after tax, measures operating profit after the tax attributable to operations, before financing. **D&A**, depreciation and amortisation, allocates the cost of long-lived assets to accounting periods. **Capital expenditure**, or capex, is investment in those assets.

**Non-cash operating working capital** here includes operating items such as inventory and customer receivables less non-interest-bearing operating payables. It excludes excess cash and financing debt. An increase consumes cash; a decrease releases it. The operating bridge is

$$
\text{FCFF}=\text{EBIT}(1-t)+\text{D\&A}
-\text{capex}-\Delta\text{operating working capital}.
$$

Equivalently, net operating reinvestment is $I=\text{capex}-\text{D\&A}+\Delta WC$, so FCFF is NOPAT minus $I$. With the pretest figures, $I= 45-20+ 15= 40$ and FCFF is $100-40= 60$.

### Why the cash-flow subtotal needs checking

Suppose annual interest is €10, fully tax deductible at 20%, current tax equals cash tax and there are no other adjustments. Pretax income after interest is $125-10= 115$; tax is $115(0.20)= 23$ and net income is €92.

If reported operating cash flow (**CFO**) includes the interest payment, CFO is $92+ 20-15= 97$. Then CFO minus capex is $97-45= 52$, **not €60 FCFF**. Add back after-tax financing interest, $10(1-0.20)= 8$, to obtain €60.

If that same interest payment is classified in financing instead, CFO in this simplified case rises by €10 to €107. CFO minus capex becomes €62. But cash tax still contains the €2 interest tax saving: remove that financing benefit to obtain €60. Merely adding back €8 again would be wrong. The [IFRS Foundation's IAS 7 overview](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-7-statement-of-cash-flows/) explains the cash-flow categories and notes changes introduced with IFRS 18. Read the reporting regime, period and actual classification rather than assuming one universal subtotal.

### When to use this reconciliation

Use it before putting a cash-flow-statement number into an enterprise DCF. Reconcile operating taxes and investment once; do not subtract the working-capital increase a second time if your starting CFO already includes it. The later [reinvestment and growth lesson](/company-financials-and-valuation/reinvestment-growth-and-economic-value) expands how new capital can support operating growth.

## Forecast dated operating cash flows and discount each once

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A €60 FCFF receipt is due at the end of year 1. At a 10% annual rate, what is its date-zero present value?" options={[{text:'€54.55 approximately: 60/1.10',correct:true},{text:'€66, by compounding it forward'},{text:'€60/1.10⁵, regardless of its date'},{text:'€6, by taking ten per cent'}]} explanation="The cash arrives one annual period from date zero. Divide once by 1.10. Later annual receipts use their own exponents." />

Set the valuation date to **0**, immediately before year 1. Every explicit FCFF is received at that year's end. All following amounts are **millions of euros**. Start with a separate operating forecast:

| Year | NOPAT | Capex less D&A | Increase in operating working capital | Net reinvestment | FCFF |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 100 | 25 | 15 | 40 | 60 |
| 2 | 110 | 28 | 17 | 45 | 65 |
| 3 | 120 | 30 | 20 | 50 | 70 |
| 4 | 130 | 33 | 22 | 55 | 75 |
| 5 | 140 | 35 | 25 | 60 | 80 |

Each row satisfies FCFF = NOPAT minus net reinvestment. These are business assumptions to investigate, not five numbers generated by an automatic promise that cash grows at a particular rate. Revenue, margins, collection, inventory, maintenance and new capacity must support them. Net reinvestment is positive in all five rows even though D&A is non-cash in the current period.

For example, year 3 FCFF is $120-(30+ 20)= 70$. Discount it three periods: $70/1.10^3= 52.5920$. The explicit forecast's present value is

$$
PV_{\text{explicit}}=\frac{60}{1.10}+\frac{65}{1.10^2}+\frac{70}{1.10^3}
+\frac{75}{1.10^4}+\frac{80}{1.10^5}= 261.7562.
$$

### When to use a longer forecast

Use enough explicit years to model the transition to a defensible stable phase. A five-year horizon is an example, not a universal optimum. More detail does not establish better knowledge; a short horizon does not justify skipping a factory's later construction costs. Include negative cash flows and project obligations on their actual dates.

## Terminal value starts after the explicit forecast ends

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="The last explicit payment is at year 5 end. The first normalized stable-phase FCFF is €105 million at year 6 end. Where does 105/(10% − 3%) sit?" options={[{text:'At year 5 end, one period before the first stable receipt',correct:true},{text:'At date zero without further discounting'},{text:'At year 6 end after its first payment'},{text:'At year 1 end, because it is the next forecast'}]} explanation="A growing-perpetuity value is measured one period before its first cash receipt. The terminal value here sits at year 5 and must be discounted five periods to date zero." />

A **terminal value** represents cash flows after the explicit forecast, measured at its endpoint. For positive first stable FCFF at year $n+ 1$ and subsequent perpetual annual growth $g$,

$$
TV_n=\frac{\text{FCFF}_{n+ 1}}{w-g},\qquad g<w.
$$

The familiar numerator $\text{FCFF}_n(1+g)$ is appropriate only when the forecast actually makes that next cash flow equal to the final explicit one grown at $g$. A transition in operating margins, investment requirements or taxes can make it different.

In our example, independently forecast **year 6 NOPAT of 150**. For the stable phase beginning there, assume 3% annual operating-profit growth thereafter and a 10% after-tax return on new operating capital. The stable reinvestment share is $3\%/10\%= 30\%$, so year 6 net reinvestment is $150(0.30)= 45$ and **FCFF is 105**. Year 7 NOPAT becomes $150+ 45(0.10)= 154.5$ and FCFF is $154.5(0.70)= 108.15= 105(1.03)$. The first stable cash flow is consistent with the subsequent growth mechanism.

This forecast includes a transition from year 5's investment and profit profile. Multiplying its FCFF of 80 by 1.03 would give 82.4, not the stated normalized 105. Neither number can replace the other without changing the operating assumptions. [Damodaran's stable-growth explanation](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/valquestions/a11.htm) connects required reinvestment with growth and return on capital rather than treating terminal growth as free cash.

At a **10% WACC** and **3% terminal growth**,

$$
TV_5= 105/(0.10-0.03)= 1{,}500,\qquad
PV(TV_5)= 1{,}500/1.10^5= 931.3820.
$$

The year 5 FCFF of 80 is outside that terminal value; it is already in the explicit sum. The year 6 FCFF of 105 is inside the terminal value; adding it again separately would count it twice.

### The convergence condition is strict

If positive terminal cash grows at the same rate as the discount rate, every discounted payment has the same positive magnitude and their infinite sum is not finite. If growth exceeds that rate, the discounted payments grow. A negative output from blindly dividing by $w-g$ is not a valid valuation under those positive-perpetuity assumptions. A real company is not thereby “worth infinity”; the specification fails to give a finite value and needs revising.

### When to use a different ending

Use explicit liquidation proceeds or a finite run-off when operations end rather than persist. Use a multistage transition if growth and returns are not yet stable. An exit multiple is an alternative terminal assumption tied to market comparables; do not add it on top of a perpetuity representing the same later operations.

## Operating value needs a bridge to common equity

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An operating FCFF valuation excludes excess cash and financing flows. It gives €1,193.14 million. There is €50 million excess cash, €300 million debt and no other claims or non-operating assets. What is common equity value?" options={[{text:'€943.14 million',correct:true},{text:'€1,543.14 million, by adding the debt'},{text:'€1,193.14 million, automatically the market cap'},{text:'€893.14 million, after omitting the excess cash'}]} explanation="Equity = operating value + separately valued excess cash − debt = 1,193.14 + 50 − 300 = 943.14. This simple bridge assumes there are no other claims." />

Add the explicit and terminal present values, using unrounded calculations:

| Component | Value at its own date | Date-zero present value, million euros |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Year 1 FCFF | 60 at year 1 end | 54.5455 |
| Year 2 FCFF | 65 at year 2 end | 53.7190 |
| Year 3 FCFF | 70 at year 3 end | 52.5920 |
| Year 4 FCFF | 75 at year 4 end | 51.2260 |
| Year 5 FCFF | 80 at year 5 end | 49.6737 |
| Terminal value | 1,500 at year 5 end | 931.3820 |
| Operating value | | **1,193.1382** |

Here the terminal portion is $931.3820/1{,}193.1382= 78.06\%$ of operating value. That is a calculation for this case, not a universal percentage for every DCF. A finite asset or mature run-off can have a very different split.

Our **simple** bridge assumes all operating cash flows belong to the same consolidated asset scope and only debt and common equity finance it. If excess cash of 50 was excluded from FCFF and its interest income, add it once. Subtract debt valued consistently at 300:

$$
\text{common equity value}= 1{,}193.1382+ 50-300= 943.1382.
$$

With ten million applicable shares and no further dilution, implied value is $943.1382/10= 94.3138$ euros per share. Operating values were in millions of euros, and the share count in millions, so the units cancel to euros per share.

If preferred stock, non-controlling interests, pension-like claims, other non-operating assets or dilutive instruments exist, reconcile those too rather than assuming they vanish. Use a share count and claim treatment consistent with the equity value being divided; do not both subtract the full value of a claim and independently dilute for that same claim without a consistent method.

### Avoid counting financing twice

We did not subtract annual debt principal repayments or interest from FCFF. Debt is addressed through the matched cost of capital and the equity bridge. If you instead value FCFE directly at the cost of equity, that calculation already values the equity claim; subtracting the same debt again is wrong. Likewise, cash already represented in the valued cash-flow stream must not be added again as a separate asset.

Both timing and financing bridges can duplicate amounts if their boundaries are not clear." />

### When to use the bridge

Use it before comparing operating value with a quoted equity market capitalisation or per-share price. A debt repayment is not a second operating expense. If debt market value differs materially from its face value, or claims have complex priority, the bridge needs the relevant valuation and contractual details rather than one unexamined balance-sheet subtraction.

## Sensitivity shows dependence on assumptions, not forecast probabilities

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A grid gives operating values between €900 million and €1,600 million for selected rate assumptions. What does that establish?" options={[{text:'The model’s outputs for those selected assumptions',correct:true},{text:'A calibrated probability that the true value lies inside that range'},{text:'A legal minimum and maximum market price'},{text:'Proof that all other operating assumptions are correct'}]} explanation="A sensitivity grid varies specified inputs while holding others fixed. It is not a confidence interval unless a separate justified probability model supplies that interpretation." />

The grid below values **the same five explicit FCFFs and normalized year 6 FCFF of 105**. Each cell discounts all cash flows at its selected annual rate and applies its growth rate only to the cash flows after that first terminal payment. The first selected pair is 10% and 3%, reproducing €1,193.1382 million operating value.

<DcfSensitivityExplorer client:visible cornerLabel="WACC / g" forecastCashFlows={[60,65,70,75,80]} baseCashFlow={105} discountRates={[0.08,0.09,0.10,0.11,0.12]} growthRates={[0.01,0.02,0.03,0.04,0.10]} currency="EUR" valueSuffix="m" caption="Amounts are in millions of euros (m). Select a cell. The explicit annual cash flows and the first normalized terminal cash flow stay fixed while the discount rate and subsequent perpetual growth change. At fixed 10% WACC, raising subsequent terminal growth from 3% to 4% makes $TV_5= 105/0.06= 1{,}750$, so operating value becomes $261.7562+ 1{,}750/1.10^5= 1{,}348.3685$. The grid deliberately holds the first normalized terminal FCFF fixed. A complete operating scenario should also test whether the reinvestment and return assumptions that support a new growth rate remain coherent.

At 10% growth and 10% WACC, the grid displays no finite perpetuity value, while the explicit five-year present value remains calculable. An invalid cell is a model-domain result, not a price forecast.

A range selected from this grid can omit the eventual outcome. Growth, margins, tax, reinvestment, failure risk and financing can all differ from the forecasts; varying only two rates does not exhaust those uncertainties. A reverse DCF asks what assumptions would be needed to support a stated price. It does not prove that the market actually believes that one scenario.

Reporting €94.31 per share is acceptable as a conditional calculation. Claiming that those cents measure how closely the forecast will match reality is not. Show the assumptions, sensitivity and material omitted cases so the reader can assess the limits.
</Reveal>

### When to use scenarios alongside the grid

Use linked downside, base and upside operating cases when revenue, margins and investment move together. If assigning probabilities, state and justify them separately. A terminal-heavy result deserves scrutiny of the terminal business assumptions; it is not automatically incorrect or automatically reliable.
