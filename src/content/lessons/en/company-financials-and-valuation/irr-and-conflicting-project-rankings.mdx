Two project proposals arrive with large percentages on the cover. One says **40%**; the other says **34.16%**. Choosing the larger number feels efficient. Before you do, ask how much cash each needs, when it pays, and what alternative use of the money you are giving up. Those details can reverse the ranking.

You have already discounted cash flows in [the time-value course](/money-time-value/net-present-value-decisions) and built a [company DCF](/company-financials-and-valuation/dcf-valuation-step-by-step). Here we turn the calculation around: hold the signed payments fixed and find a rate that makes their net present value zero. Then we decide how much that rate can tell us.

Our invented projects use dollars, annual dates and effective annual rates. All payments are **incremental net cash flows** from the investor's viewpoint: receipts positive, outlays negative. They include the stated project costs and omit unspecified taxes, fees, financing flows and risk differences. A real comparison must model those consistently. No forecast percentage here is a promise.

## IRR is a rate that solves an equation

Think of a balance with today's outlay on one side and discounted future receipts on the other. **Internal rate of return (IRR)** is a discount rate that makes the two sides balance. Equivalently, it is a **root**: an input at which a function equals zero.

For cash flow $C_t$ at date $t$, net present value at an assumed opportunity-cost rate $k$ is:

$$
\operatorname{NPV}(k)=\sum_{t=0}^{n}\frac{C_t}{(1+k)^t}.
$$

An IRR $i$ satisfies:

$$
\sum_{t=0}^{n}\frac{C_t}{(1+i)^t}=0,\qquad i>-1.
$$

The **opportunity-cost rate** $k$ is the return required for giving up a comparable alternative, with a consistent risk and cash-flow basis. You choose or estimate it outside the project's equation. The IRR $i$ is an output from the dated amounts. Both are rates per cash-flow period. An annual schedule needs an effective annual rate; monthly periods need a monthly rate. For irregular calendar dates, use actual elapsed-year fractions and disclose the day-count convention rather than silently calling every row one year.

### Work the rate, then work the value

Project **Early** requires \$100 now and forecasts a single \$140 receipt at the end of year 1:

$$
0=-100+\frac{140}{1+i}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1+i=\frac{140}{100}=1.4
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad i=40\%.
$$

Substitute it back: $-100+140/1.4=0$. At an assumed opportunity-cost rate of 10%, however, the same schedule has:

$$
\operatorname{NPV}(10\%)=-100+\frac{140}{1.10}=27.272727\ldots.
$$

The project has a 40% forecast cash-flow IRR and adds an estimated \$27.27 of date-zero value **at the assumed 10% rate**. These are two different outputs with different units.

Move the rate control. The receipt stays \$140; only its present value changes. The zero crossing at 40% is a break-even discount rate for the stated forecast, not a control that makes the receipt grow.

### When the familiar acceptance rule works

For a **conventional investment** with a strictly negative initial payment, no later negative payments and at least one strictly positive later receipt, NPV decreases strictly as the rate rises above −100%. Each positive later receipt is divided by a larger discount factor. As the rate approaches −100%, that present value grows without bound; as the rate grows without bound, NPV approaches the negative initial payment. There is exactly one root in this domain.

Under those conditions, $i>k$ is equivalent to positive NPV at $k$. For an independent, feasible project, positive NPV supports acceptance within the model. It does not settle a choice between mutually exclusive projects or make uncertain receipts safe. With a later outlay, even the decreasing-profile argument can fail; we will calculate that failure below. A financing schedule with cash received first and repaid later also reverses the usual investment intuition.

## Scale can overturn the percentage ranking

A 20% return on a small cash amount can add less value than 15% on a larger amount. Percentage rankings remove scale; a company's cash budget does not. Suppose exactly one copy of each following project is available, both have the same modeled risk, and both pay once after one year.

For a single later receipt, the IRR is receipt divided by outlay, minus one:

$$
i_S=\frac{1{,}200}{1{,}000}-1=20\%,\qquad
 i_L=\frac{11{,}500}{10{,}000}-1=15\%.
$$

At a common assumed rate of 10%:

$$
\operatorname{NPV}_S=-1{,}000+\frac{1{,}200}{1.10}=90.909091\ldots,
$$

$$
\operatorname{NPV}_L=-10{,}000+\frac{11{,}500}{1.10}=454.545455\ldots.
$$

### Choose among the alternatives that actually exist

If the projects are **mutually exclusive**, taking one prevents taking the other. With both feasible and a 10% opportunity-cost rate appropriate to both, Large adds more modeled present value. If both are independent and funding for both is available, the relevant alternative may be taking both, not forcing a ranking.

If the budget is only \$1,000 and additional funding is unavailable, Large is infeasible. Its higher NPV does not pay its initial bill. Nor may we quietly invent ten copies of Small. If ten genuinely independent identical copies become available with sufficient funding, their combined NPV is $10\times90.909091=909.09091\ldots$, changing the feasible set. Replication is an assumption about opportunities, not a reward for finding an attractive percentage.

### When to use it

Use NPV to compare the value of **feasible cash-flow alternatives** at an appropriate explicit rate. Under a hard budget with several indivisible projects, compare affordable combinations and their combined NPV; choosing the largest individual NPV or highest IRR can miss the best combination. Also consider project interactions, capacity, liquidity and uncertainty. A rate suited to one risk level need not suit another. [CFA Institute's capital-allocation overview](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/capital-investments-and-capital-allocation) frames project value alongside cash-flow consistency and other decision considerations.

A receipt that arrives late travels through more discount periods. Raise the assumed rate and it loses present value faster. An **NPV profile** plots a fixed project's NPV against that rate. A **crossover rate** makes two profiles equal; it need not equal either project's IRR.

Consider Early again and a second project, **Late**. Both require \$100 now. Early pays \$140 at year 1 and then ends. Late pays \$180 at year 2. We compare one available copy of each, on a date-zero value basis. There is no automatic repetition of Early in year 1 and no unmentioned replacement project or terminal payoff.

### Derive the crossover from the extra cash flows

Set the two NPVs equal. The common initial costs cancel:

$$
\frac{140}{1+k^*}=\frac{180}{(1+k^*)^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 140(1+k^*)=180,
$$

$$
k^*=\frac{180}{140}-1=\frac{2}{7}\approx28.5714\%.
$$

At that rate, both NPVs are $80/9=8.888889\ldots$. The crossover is below both project IRRs. Both projects therefore still have positive NPV when their **relative** values become equal.

Another route is to subtract Early's schedule from Late's: date 0 becomes zero, year 1 is −140 (the receipt forgone), and year 2 is +180 (the receipt gained). The **incremental NPV** of choosing Late instead of Early is:

$$
\Delta\operatorname{NPV}(k)=-\frac{140}{1+k}+\frac{180}{(1+k)^2}.
$$

At 10%, it is $-127.272727+148.760331=21.487604\ldots$, favoring Late. At 30%, it is $-107.692308+106.508876=-1.183432\ldots$, favoring Early. Its root is the same crossover. Below the crossover, Late is higher; above it, Early is higher, for this pair and the domain $k>-1$.

### When to use it

Use profiles and incremental cash flows when project timing differs and rankings conflict. State the rate that represents the relevant opportunity cost, then compare value there. Selecting a rate merely to make a preferred project win reverses the logic. For a continuing service that requires replacements, build a common service horizon and explicit replacement assumptions; the one-copy comparison above does not establish that a short-lived machine can serve forever. [OpenStax's project-comparison chapter](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance-2e/pages/16-5-choosing-between-projects) distinguishes mutually exclusive choices, scale, different lives and limited resources.

## Later outlays can produce two IRRs or none

  question="A project pays a large receipt, then requires a cleanup payment. A spreadsheet displays one IRR. What should you check?"

    { text: "Whether the first root found must be the only mathematical solution" },

A later payment can pull the present value in the opposite direction from a receipt. Discounting a cleanup liability more heavily reduces its present burden. Consequently, increasing the rate need not lower net value at every point. A sign-changing schedule is called **unconventional** here when negative flows occur after receipts have begun. That is a warning to inspect the equation, not proof that multiple roots must exist.

### Two different rates can both balance the schedule

A project pays −100 now, +230 at year 1 and −132 at year 2. The last payment is a real modeled obligation, not a rounding adjustment:

$$
\operatorname{NPV}(k)=-100+\frac{230}{1+k}-\frac{132}{(1+k)^2}.
$$

Set $x=1+i$. In our rate domain, $x>0$. Multiplying the zero-NPV equation by $x^2$ gives:

$$
-100x^2+230x-132=-(10x-11)(10x-12)=0.
$$

Thus $x=1.1$ or $x=1.2$: the IRRs are **10% and 20%**. Check both against the original payments:

$$
-100+\frac{230}{1.1}-\frac{132}{1.1^2}=0,\qquad
 -100+\frac{230}{1.2}-\frac{132}{1.2^2}=0.
$$

### A valid cash-flow schedule may have no real root

Now use −100, +50 and −10 at the same dates. Setting NPV to zero would require:

$$
-100x^2+50x-10=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad100x^2-50x+10=0.
$$

The **discriminant** of a quadratic $ax^2+bx+c$ is $b^2-4ac$. It determines whether its roots are real. Here it is:

$$
(-50)^2-4(100)(10)=2{,}500-4{,}000=-1{,}500<0.
$$

There is no real IRR. We can also establish the sign without relying on a solver:

$$
-100x^2+50x-10=-100(x-0.25)^2-3.75<0.
$$

Since $x^2>0$, dividing by it leaves NPV negative for every $i>-1$. At the ordinary assumed rate of 10%, we can still calculate its value:

$$
\operatorname{NPV}(10\%)=-100+\frac{50}{1.1}-\frac{10}{1.1^2}=-62.809917\ldots.
$$

The displayed range alone cannot prove that no other root exists. The algebra above establishes the result for the whole stated domain. In other cases, a mathematical root may be negative, outside a relevant economic domain, or one of several roots. A solver's initial guess can affect which root it reports; failure to converge does not itself prove absence. For higher-degree or irregularly dated schedules, inspect the profile, validate candidate roots by substitution and retain NPV at the chosen opportunity-cost rate.

### When to use it

Check for later capital calls, decommissioning, tax payments and other negative flows after initial receipts. Include each on its correct date. The zero-NPV definition is still meaningful, but “accept if the displayed IRR exceeds the hurdle” is unreliable for an arbitrary signed schedule. [OpenStax's IRR chapter](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance-2e/pages/16-3-internal-rate-of-return-irr-method) illustrates a project with later conversion costs and two zero crossings.

## Cash-flow IRR and terminal wealth answer different questions

<ThinkReveal client:visible
  prompt="Early still pays 140 after one year. You then hold that receipt for a further year at zero interest. Has the original project's forty-per-cent IRR changed?"
  hint="Which payments belong to Early, and which belong to your later use of its receipt?"
  text="Early's signed schedule remains −100 now and +140 at year 1, so its IRR remains 40%. Holding the receipt afterwards changes the investor's wealth at year 2. That separate outcome requires an explicit reinvestment assumption."

A cash receipt can leave the project and become part of a different decision. The project's IRR equation contains the original payments; it does **not** contain a guarantee that distributions can be reinvested at that same rate. To turn an early payout into terminal wealth, specify what happens after the payout. [Ares' quantitative research discussion](https://www.ares.com/news-and-insights/irrational-thinking) separates this project-specific equation from the investor's subsequent reinvestment choices. Some textbook descriptions compress those two questions into a “reinvestment assumption”; keeping the cash-flow boundary explicit avoids that ambiguity.

### Keep the project fixed and vary the later investment

Early costs \$100 at date 0 and pays \$140 at date 1. For a date-2 wealth comparison, suppose the investor reinvests **the entire receipt**, makes no further contributions or withdrawals and incurs no taxes or fees. Let $q$ be the effective rate earned during that extra year:

$$
W_2=140(1+q).
$$

The annualized growth of the original \$100 to that date-2 wealth is:

$$
g_W=\sqrt{\frac{W_2}{100}}-1.
$$

### When to use it

Use cash-flow IRR to summarize the stated dated project schedule, subject to its root limitations. Use a terminal-wealth calculation when the question concerns wealth at a common future date and explicitly model reinvestment, uninvested cash, additional funding and withdrawals. If forecasts change, recompute both; a forecast IRR can differ from the IRR of realized cash flows. A high IRR alone does not establish scale, total profit, portfolio performance or investment skill.

A practical decision starts by identifying feasible alternatives and complete incremental payments. Match the rate to the cash-flow period and risk basis, calculate NPV, check conflicting profiles and roots, and stress the uncertain inputs. Report the chosen rate and funding constraints with the result. “Highest percentage” leaves too much of that decision unwritten.
