A bond can quote a yield of **6.557377%** while its modeled expected return is **5.278689%**, and still lose money if it defaults. The yield is calculated from promised payments. The expectation weights what you might actually receive. A payment promise is useful arithmetic; it is not cash in your account.

We will price one invented bond, keep the same purchase price when calculating returns, and then examine how recovery, time and joint defaults change the analysis. Earlier pricing lessons supplied fixed cash flows to a discounting model; they did not establish that every government or corporate payment is certain.

A **credit spread** is a quoted risky yield minus a specified reference yield, using comparable conventions. If those yields are 7% and 4%, the gap is **3 percentage points = 300 basis points**. One basis point is 0.01 percentage point. This subtraction defines the quote; it does not identify the probability of default.

Match currency, dates, compounding, and the relevant cash-flow/option assumptions before comparing. A same-maturity government yield may be a benchmark proxy, rather than a universal default-free fact. A coupon-bond YTM gap, a spread over a zero curve and an option-adjusted spread are different measurements. [The Cleveland Fed describes default risk alongside liquidity, tax and option effects on observed spreads](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/economic-commentary/2007/ec-20070301-credit-spreads-and-subordinated-debt).

### A cash-flow test of the shortcut

Use a one-year **zero-coupon bond**, meaning no intermediate interest payment. It promises 100 at year one. In the only default state it pays 40 instead, also at year one. All amounts share one invented currency; there are no costs, taxes, early recovery payments or exchange-rate changes. The reference asset pays an effective one-year rate r = 4% and is default-free **within this model**.

For this first calculation, give default a 2% weight both when pricing and when forecasting. Expected cash is

$$
\begin{aligned}0.98(100)+0.02(40)&=98.8,\\P_0=\frac{98.8}{1.04}&=95.\end{aligned}
$$

The **promised yield** solves 95(1 + y) = 100, giving y = 100/95 − 1 = **5.263158%**. Its spread is **1.263158 percentage points**. Expected lost face is 0.02(100 − 40) = **1.2 units**, or 1.2% of face. These last two percentages have different denominators and are not equal.

### When to use it

Use a matched spread to compare quotes and monitor repricing. Do not label the entire difference “extra return earned”, or assume that any spread left after subtracting a physical loss fraction is a uniquely identified risk premium. Liquidity, state pricing and benchmark choices can all matter.

## Price with Q; evaluate physical outcomes with P

Think of two sets of weights attached to the same payment tree. **P, the physical probability measure**, describes a model of how often the states occur. **Q, a risk-neutral pricing measure**, weights the states for a valuation model discounted at its matched reference rate. “Risk-neutral” describes that pricing representation; it does not mean investors feel no risk. Q need not equal P, and a credit market need not uniquely identify either without additional model assumptions.

Equivalent measures agree on which events are impossible. The zero-probability endpoints in the controls flag a purely formal calculation if only one measure rules out default. This support condition is part of the [positive-density pricing construction in MIT’s finance notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-450-analytics-of-finance-fall-2010/219406be97b96bc540e0be6aa778c0b7_MIT15_450F10_lec01.pdf). Reweighting states does not validate their real-world probabilities.

Here the recovery rule is fixed, Q default weight q is supplied, and the valuation rule is

$$
P_0=\frac{100[1-q(1-R)]}{1+r},\qquad R=0.40.
$$

[CFA Institute distinguishes risk-neutral expectations used for credit valuation from physical default inputs](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/credit-analysis-models). Our two-state example is a deliberately small valuation model, not an estimate of a traded issuer.

### Keep the price denominator in every return

Set physical PD p = 2%, but pricing PD q = 4%. Then the Q-weighted payment is 0.96 × 100 + 0.04 × 40 = 97.6, so price is **97.6/1.04 = 93.846154**. The physical expected payment remains 98.8.

The expected physical return also follows from weighting the two realized returns: 0.98 × 6.557377% + 0.02 × (−57.377049%) = **5.278689%**. Thus expected excess over the 4% reference is **1.278689 percentage points**, while the quoted spread is **2.557377 percentage points**. Both use the purchase price, not face, in their return denominator.

For this exact one-year payment convention, the spread follows algebraically from price:

$$
s=y-r=\frac{(1+r)q(1-R)}{1-q(1-R)}.
$$

It is not exactly q × LGD, and physical p does not enter the price unless the model links it to q. A decomposition into expected default loss and compensation for risk can be useful **under stated definitions and an identified model**. [The New York Fed’s 2026 decomposition, for example, estimates its loss component using a particular model](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/02/estimating-the-term-structure-of-corporate-bond-risk-premia/); subtracting two labels is not enough to estimate that component.

### When to use it

Use Q for this specified pricing exercise and P for physical scenario expectations. The expectation is neither a guaranteed outcome nor the bond’s promised YTM. If you sell before maturity, replace the final payment in your holding-period cash budget with the sale proceeds: rates, spreads and liquidity can change the sale price even without a default. With coupons, include their actual receipt and reinvestment rules rather than recycling this zero-coupon formula.

## Expected loss needs a horizon, exposure and conditional severity

<MCQ

  pretest

An **exposure at default (EAD)** is the amount to which a loss fraction applies. **Loss given default (LGD)** is the fraction of that specified exposure lost when default occurs. In our face-recovery example, EAD is promised face 100 and LGD = 1−R = 60%. The physical one-year expected lost face is

$$
EL=100\times0.02\times0.60=1.2.
$$

This is a nominal expected shortfall of the promise at year one, before discounting. It is neither the realized purchase-price loss nor a loss automatically repeated every year. With fixed exposure and uncertain severity, the precise identity is

$$
EL=EAD\times\Pr_P(D)\times\mathbb E_P[LGD\mid D].
$$

D denotes default over the stated horizon. Conditioning on D matters: loss severity is sampled among defaults. If exposure itself is random, the starting identity is $EL=\mathbb E_P[\mathbf1_D\,EAD\,LGD]$; additional factorization needs appropriate assumptions.

The total loss fraction is **0.8 × 0.01 × 0.4 + 0.2 × 0.10 × 0.8 = 0.0192**, or 1.92%. Marginal PD is 0.8 × 1% + 0.2 × 10% = 2.8%. Averaging the two LGDs without conditioning gives 0.8 × 40% + 0.2 × 80% = 48%, but multiplying 2.8% × 48% = **1.344%** is wrong for this model.

An average loss is not a stress limit; its accuracy also depends on estimated probabilities and severities.

## An intensity is a rate; cumulative PD is a probability

  client:visible

A **default intensity** λ has units of inverse time. Given survival to t, the probability of default during a short interval Δt is approximately λΔt; it is not a dimensionless “instantaneous probability”. For constant λ, surviving many short intervals yields

$$
\begin{aligned}S(T)&=e^{-\lambda T},\\PD(0,T)&=1-e^{-\lambda T}.\end{aligned}
$$

Specify the measure. A physical intensity λᴾ describes a physical model; a pricing intensity λᵠ describes a pricing model. They are not interchangeable. At λᵠ = 0.02 per year, Q cumulative default is **1−e⁻⁰·⁰² = 1.980133%** at one year, **1−e⁻⁰·¹ = 9.516258%** at five and **1−e⁻⁰·² = 18.126925%** at ten. λT is a short-horizon approximation, not the exact probability.

### A spread-to-intensity shortcut needs another contract model

A commonly used **credit-triangle approximation** is $s\approx\lambda^Q(1-R)$. For 120 bp = 0.012 per year and R = 40%, it implies λᵠ≈0.012/0.60 = **0.02 per year**. This view uses a flat intensity, stated recovery and a continuous-premium approximation. A market CDS has payment dates, accrual, upfront and recovery conventions; a cash bond has its own promised payments. [The CDS lesson develops those distinctions](/credit-derivatives-and-securitization/cds-spreads-and-hazard-rates).

Do not silently substitute the triangle for our exact recovery-at-year-one cash-bond formula. Nor does increasing cumulative PD force upward-sloping spreads: a constant-intensity triangle can produce a **flat** spread while cumulative PD rises. Time-varying conditional hazards, recovery, liquidity, discounting and optionality can produce other curve shapes. A distressed issuer may have high near-term conditional risk, but the direction of its entire spread curve requires analysis.

### When to use it

Use survival functions to convert a specified hazard path into horizon probabilities. Use the triangle as a labeled approximation or an introduction to a fuller pricing model. A quoted spread alone does not identify physical PD; the recovery assumption and valuation conventions already affect the implied Q result.

## Joint default changes the tail without automatically changing the mean

Two umbrellas do not help much if both have the same hole. Default dependence affects how portfolio losses arrive together. But **linearity of expectation** still holds: total expected loss is the sum of individual expected losses, regardless of dependence, when those individual loss distributions are held fixed.

Take two exposures of 100, each with physical PD = 2% and fixed conditional LGD = 60%. One default loses 60. Each expected loss is 100 × 0.02 × 0.60 = 1.2, so total expected loss is **2.4**.

For independent defaults, both-default probability is 0.02² = 0.0004, and exactly-one probability is 2 × 0.02 × 0.98 = 0.0392. Expected aggregate loss is 60 × 0.0392 + 120 × 0.0004 = 2.4. With perfectly common defaults, it is 120 × 0.02 = 2.4 again. Yet the chance of losing 120 rises from **0.04% to 2%**, a factor of 50.

The control mixes these two joint laws, so it preserves each PD and the expected loss at every setting. It is a coupling-mixture parameter, not a universal “asset correlation” or a fitted market dependence estimate. The loss distribution changes, including a zero-probability middle state at the common-default endpoint.

### When to use it

Model dependence when studying joint stress losses, concentration, tranche cash flows and capital needs. Do not conclude that correlation alone invalidates PD times conditional LGD or increases a fixed-marginal portfolio’s mean. In a changed recession scenario, the individual PDs and severities can change **as well as** their dependence; those are separate channels.

A **credit rating** is an agency’s opinion about relative creditworthiness under its definitions. Its scale is not a universal numerical PD table. The [SEC’s ratings bulletin explains that ratings are not guarantees and do not cover all market, liquidity or price risks](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/updated-8). Check the agency, rated claim, horizon, date and meaning before using one.

A concrete screen: suppose two one-year claims both promise 100 and are offered at 95. An agency letter is identical. Your invented physical assumptions give A: p = 2%, recovery 40%; B: p = 4%, recovery 40%. Their expected payments are **98.8 and 97.6**, so physical expected purchase returns are **98.8/95 − 1 = 4%** and **97.6/95 − 1 = 2.736842%**. Same promised yield and rating label, different modeled outcomes. This does not establish which assumptions are right.

### When to use it

Use the sequence to expose missing inputs and compare consistent scenarios. Include estimation uncertainty, costs, liquidity and funding before making a real decision. Neither a positive expected excess return nor a high rating proves a trade is suitable or guarantees payment. A rate hedge also leaves credit-spread and default risks unless those exposures are addressed separately.

## Put the payment, probability and denominator together

A spread is a quote against a specified reference. Value the actual cash-flow states using a defined pricing model; evaluate physical expected returns with separately stated physical assumptions. Recovery has a base and a date. Conditional severity makes expected-loss arithmetic precise, and dependence controls joint tails without breaking linearity of expectation.
