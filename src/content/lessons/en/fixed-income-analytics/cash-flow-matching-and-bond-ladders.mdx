A bill due on Tuesday cannot be paid with a bond maturing on Friday. Even if Friday's payment is enormous. **Cash-flow matching** starts with that stubborn calendar: arrange asset receipts to meet specified liabilities, the payments you owe, on their actual due dates.

You already know how to value a bond and hedge rate sensitivity. Here the question is operational: **which money is available when a payment must leave?** A ladder of maturities helps organise that question, but its rungs still need amounts, currencies and funding rules.

Think of three labelled envelopes: year 1, year 2 and year 3. The liability schedule is €100 in each. A security promising €300 only in year 3 puts all the notes in the last envelope. Total promised receipts and total liabilities both equal €300; the first two envelopes remain empty.

Throughout the central example, time zero is **just after any previous coupon**, prices are **full purchase prices per original-face-100 unit**, and years 1–3 are exact annual payment dates. Receipts settle and become spendable **before** the same-date liability is paid. Everything is in euros. Ignore tax, trading costs and defaults initially; add stated exceptions later. These are invented securities, not market quotes.

That last zero does not repair the missed deadlines. Without borrowing, selling other assets or changing the payment agreement, this plan needs **€200 of initial cash**, held at the explicitly assumed 0 % rate. Then the actual funded cash balances are €100, €0 and €200 after the three payments. The reserve is extra outside capital; it is not free interest earned by the late bond.

**Pitfall.** An aggregate surplus can coexist with an earlier cash shortage. Check prefixes of the calendar, not just its final sum.

### When to use it

Start here whenever payments have deadlines: debt service, insurance claims with specified dates or a known spending plan. First list the currency, settlement time and amount. A vague “yearly income target” can hide an intrayear mismatch.

A coupon bond is several dated receipts bundled into one security. Buying its final principal also buys its earlier coupons. Solve from the last date backwards so those coupons do not get counted twice.

Assume divisible holdings for this theoretical calculation. Let $q_A,q_B,q_C$ be units bought. Each row in the cash-flow matrix is a date; each column is a bond:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}100&5&4\\0&105&4\\0&0&104\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}q_A\\q_B\\q_C\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}100\\100\\100\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At year 3 only C pays, so $q_C=100/104=25/26$. At year 2, C already supplies $4(25/26)=3.846154$ euros. B must supply the remaining €96.153846:

$$
q_B=\frac{100-4(25/26)}{105}=\frac{250}{273}.
\qquad
q_A=\frac{100-5(250/273)-4(25/26)}{100}=\frac{250}{273}.
$$

The date-1 check is $91.575092+4.578755+3.846154=100$ before displayed rounding. Date 2 is $96.153846+3.846154=100$; date 3 is $104(25/26)=100$. Initial purchase cost is:

$$
98q_A+99q_B+100q_C=\frac{75500}{273}=276.556777\text{ euros}.
$$

This is an **exact promised-flow replica in this three-security universe**, not a proof that it is the cheapest feasible funding plan with surplus cash, other securities, short positions, taxes or lot restrictions. Optimising those choices is a separate problem with an objective and constraints.

Changing them recomputes the equality candidate. A negative quantity is flagged rather than presented as an available long-only purchase." />

For liabilities €10, €100 and €200, the no-surplus equality requires $q_A=-570/27300$. That rules out **that equality candidate** if shorting is forbidden. It does not rule out all long-only funding: buying zero A, one B and two C produces €13, €113 and €208, leaving cash €3, €16 and €24 at 0 % after the stated liabilities.

Actual zero-coupon instruments can isolate a date more directly. For example, [TreasuryDirect explains that each US STRIPS principal or interest component becomes a separate security with one maturity payment](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/strips/). Currency, issue rules and tradable sizes still matter; the fractional euro holdings above are a teaching assumption.

### When to use it

Use backward matching when liabilities are specified and enough appropriately dated instruments exist. Coupons make it a system of payments, not “buy one bond maturing in each year”.

## 3. Carry the cash and fund the costs

Exact equality is one possible plan. A **funded cash ledger** can also use earlier surpluses. Define $B_t$ as cash **after** the date-$t$ liability, $R_t$ as the bond receipts, $L_t$ as the liability and $g_t$ as the agreed cash gross factor from the previous date:

$$
B_t=g_tB_{t-1}+R_t-L_t,\qquad B_t\geq0.
$$

The cash rate is a stated assumption, not a return supplied by a bond-pricing yield. With zero cash interest and one whole unit of each bond, receipts are €109, €109 and €104. Pay €100 each time: $B_1=0+109-100=9$, $B_2=9+109-100=18$, $B_3=18+104-100=22$. The securities cost €297, more than the exact fractional replica.

Rounding the exact quantities to two decimals gives $(0.92,0.92,0.96)$. Receipts become €100.44, €100.44 and €99.84. The final receipt alone is short, but cash carried forward yields balances €0.44, €0.88 and €0.72, so the plan covers all three deadlines at 0 %. Rounding every quantity down to $(0.91,0.91,0.96)$ instead gives €99.39, €99.39 and €99.84; cumulative deficits require **€1.38 of initial reserve**.

For arbitrary positive gross factors, put $G_t=\prod_{j=1}^t g_j$. Discount each prefix net cash flow back to time zero:

$$
B_0^{\min}=\max\left(0,\max_{k=1,2,3}\left[-\sum_{t=1}^{k}\frac{R_t-L_t}{G_t}\right]\right).
$$

This is the reserve needed **for the specified fixed holdings and carry assumptions**. It is not an optimiser over bond holdings. The visual supplies that reserve first, then displays the funded ledger. Its tiny published round-off bound removes floating-point noise only; it is not an allowed payment shortfall or a market rounding rule.

Costs need their own funding. For the exact fractional replica, an invented €1 fee per positive bond position adds €3: $276.556777+3=279.556777$, within a €280 budget. At €2 per position the same holdings cost €282.556777 including fees: the budget is short €2.556777. Paying fees does not magically reduce them out of the coupons while preserving the match. Trading minimums, denomination rules and price changes can alter the feasible plan.

### When to use it

Use the ledger after sizing any candidate. It catches timing gaps, rounding effects and acquisition costs before a plan is called funded. Taxes would require their own dated cash flows and jurisdiction-specific assumptions.

## 4. A duration match is a different promise

<MCQ

  {
    "text": "Yes; equal PV moves late principal into earlier cash",

**Cash matching asks for money on each date. Duration matching asks about a local value sensitivity.** The [CFA Institute's LDI discussion](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/liability-driven-index-based-strategies) treats cash-flow and duration matching as distinct implementation approaches and explains the maintenance and curve risks of immunization. Here is a concrete distinction.

Use a separate flat **4 % annual-effective** curve. A single €100 liability in year 2 has present value $V=100/1.04^2=92.455621$ and Macaulay duration 2 years. Invest half that present value in a year-1 zero and half in a year-3 zero. Their faces are:

$$
F_1=\frac{V}{2}(1.04)=48.076923,\qquad
F_3=\frac{V}{2}(1.04)^3=52.
$$

Initial asset PV equals liability PV; asset Macaulay duration is $0.5(1)+0.5(3)=2$. Under this annual yield coordinate, both modified durations equal $2/1.04=1.923077$. That matches the initial first derivative for a parallel change in this yield. It does not place €100 of bond receipts at year 2: **neither zero matures then**.

If the early €48.076923 earns 4 % for the next year, it becomes €50. If the remaining zero can then be sold at a 4 % yield, its €52 year-3 claim sells for $52/1.04=50$. Together they pay €100. The sold claim belongs to the buyer; the original owner cannot also count €52 in year 3.

Change the assumptions: reinvest at 0 % and sell the remaining one-year claim at 8 %. Available year-2 cash is $48.076923+52/1.08=96.225071$: a **€3.774929 shortfall**. Or decline to sell: only the carried early payment is available. No borrowing is assumed to fill the gap.

<CashFlowMatchingLab client:visible mode="duration" cashPct={4} title="Separate initial sensitivity from future spendable cash" caption="The initial PV and duration match stays fixed. Controls alter future cash carry, the sale yield and whether the year-3 claim is sold at year 2. These are scenarios, not guaranteed trading terms." />

### When to use it

Choose a sensitivity hedge to manage the rate exposure it measures; choose a dated funding plan to cover deadlines under specified payment assumptions. Neither label excuses checking credit, settlement, nonparallel curve moves or future trading needs. The preceding immunization and key-rate lessons provide the sensitivity tools; this ledger tests a different requirement.

## 5. Decide whether a ladder is spent or renewed

A **bond ladder** holds bonds with staggered maturities. Its rungs provide successive decision dates. At a maturity you might spend the proceeds on a liability or reinvest them into a new longer rung. Those are different cash plans. [Fidelity's explanation of ladders](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/investment-products/fixed-income-bonds/bond-investment-strategies) describes staggered maturities and successive reinvestment decisions; it does not make future purchase terms known today.

Take a separate initial €100. A stipulated one-year investment at 4 % pays €104. If it is rolled for another year at a future rate $u$, the terminal amount is:

$$
W_2=100(1.04)(1+u).
$$

Under a flat 4 % annual-effective pricing curve, a direct two-year zero promising €108.16 would cost $108.16/1.04^2=100$. If that promise is paid, it funds this date without the intermediate reinvestment decision. Its market price can still change before maturity, and its payment still depends on the issuer and contract. The implied one-year forward rate of 4 % from that flat curve is a pricing relationship; it does **not** forecast the rate at which the rolled plan will actually reinvest.

### When to use it

A ladder can spread maturity and reinvestment dates rather than concentrate them. Check whether each rung's proceeds are earmarked for spending or renewal. A perpetual rolling programme is not the same as a fixed portfolio that runs down against a finite liability schedule.

## 6. Stress the promises and the target

The equality in section 2 is about **promised** cash flows. A liability is not cancelled because an asset misses a payment. The [MSRB's risk guide](https://www.msrb.org/Education/Municipal-Bond-Investment-Risks) distinguishes failure to pay principal or interest on time, price risk and reinvestment risk for municipal bonds; those are useful distinctions, not a guarantee for our invented bonds.

First stress the asset. Keep the original fractional quantities. B pays its €5 year-1 coupon, then defaults at year 2. Stipulate immediate recovery of **40 % of original face €100**, replacing its whole scheduled €105 final payment. Recovery per B unit is €40, so the missing receipt per unit is €65, not €60:

$$
\text{year-2 shortfall}=(105-40)\frac{250}{273}=59.523810.
$$

Total year-2 receipts fall to €40.476190. Nothing here estimates a default probability or expected loss; it is one specified stress path. Different recovery dates or accrued-interest rights would give a different ledger.

Now stress the liability separately. Suppose the €100 base grows at an assumed 3 % per year, while the original nominal assets still pay €100 on each date. Liabilities become €103, €106.09 and €109.2727; individual deficits are €3, €6.09 and €9.2727. At 0 % cash carry, the extra initial reserve is $3+6.09+9.2727=18.3627$. This is an invented indexation rule, not an inflation forecast or a model of anyone's personal spending basket.

<CashFlowMatchingLab client:visible mode="stress" title="Keep the funding test when assumptions change" caption="Start with the original exact nominal replica. Choose either a specified B default or a growing liability. The scenarios are separate; the visual does not infer probabilities or combine them silently." />

**Pitfall.** “Held to maturity” does not mean “every promise is fulfilled”. Interim market prices can move even when payments are ultimately made. Exact dated nominal receipts, if paid and available on time, can fund matched nominal liabilities without an interim sale; they do not eliminate default, contractual calls, tax, currency or changing-liability risk. The central bonds are option-free; a callable instrument needs its actual possible payment schedule, not our fixed matrix.

### When to use it

A usable plan states what is promised, what is assumed and what happens if either side changes. Review payment calendars and operational cutoffs as well as prices. Recheck the plan after changed liabilities, acquisitions, defaults or a decision to renew a rung.
