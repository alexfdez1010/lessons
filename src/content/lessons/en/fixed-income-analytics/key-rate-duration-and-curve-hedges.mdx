A book’s total DV01 is zero. Short rates fall, long rates rise, and the book makes or loses money anyway. The hedge cancelled one direction of movement: a parallel shift. A curve has more than one direction.

[Duration and DV01](/fixed-income-analytics/duration-macaulay-modified-dv01) established the yield coordinate and units; [immunization](/fixed-income-analytics/immunization-and-hedging) identified the shape risk left over. This lesson measures it. All prices, cash flows and sensitivity matrices below are invented. We assume fixed payments are received in full, exclude credit and execution effects, and separate exact valuation from first-order hedge estimates.

A **key rate duration (KRD)**, also called partial duration, measures sensitivity to a specified local change of the yield curve. Imagine a flexible ruler held at several points: moving one point bends nearby sections. The locations and the bending rule both matter.

We choose **continuously compounded zero rates** $z_j$ at 2, 5 and 10 years, initially 3%, 4% and 5%. A zero rate discounts one payment at its own date. For a fixed cash flow $C_t$,

$$\mathrm{PV}_t=C_te^{-t z(t)}$$

Here $t$ is time in years. We linearly interpolate **zero rates**, not bond prices, discount factors or par yields. Between the 2- and 5-year nodes,

$$z(t)=b_2(t)z_2+b_5(t)z_5,\quad b_2(t)=\frac{5-t}{3},\quad b_5(t)=\frac{t-2}{3}$$

At year 4, $b_2=1/3$ and $b_5=2/3$, so

$$z(4)=\frac13(0.03)+\frac23(0.04)=0.03666667\ldots$$

Between 5 and 10 years, use the corresponding linear weights. Before the first node or after the last, this example keeps the nearest endpoint zero rate flat. Thus the node basis weights always sum to one. These are explicit modelling choices, not a universal market convention.

Specify the curve, compounding, nodes, interpolation, extrapolation and bump definition before comparing bucket reports. A zero-rate-node delta is different from a delta obtained by bumping a traded par quote and recalibrating the curve. ## Derive KRD and bucket DV01 in consistent units

Let $P=\sum_t C_te^{-tz(t)}$ and hold the cash flows fixed. Under our linear zero-rate basis, $\partial z(t)/\partial z_j=b_j(t)$. The chain rule gives

$$\frac{\partial P}{\partial z_j}=-\sum_t t\,\mathrm{PV}_t b_j(t)$$

Define derivative KRD and the positive-long **bucket DV01** convention by

$$D_j=-\frac1P\frac{\partial P}{\partial z_j},\qquad q_j=-10^{-4}\frac{\partial P}{\partial z_j}=P D_j10^{-4}$$

$D_j$ is relative price sensitivity per unit **decimal annual continuous zero rate**. $q_j$ is EUR per basis point for this claim. With the stated nonnegative cash flows and interpolation weights, each $q_j$ is nonnegative. For a signed net book, a bucket can be negative.

### Allocate the year-4 payment to two nodes

The year-2 money sensitivity receives the direct year-2 flow and one-third of the year-4 flow:

$$-\frac{\partial P}{\partial z_2}=2(47.08822668)+4(43.17908950)\frac13\approx151.74857269$$

The year-5 node receives two-thirds of the year-4 flow:

$$-\frac{\partial P}{\partial z_5}=4(43.17908950)\frac23\approx115.14423867$$

The year-10 node receives the last flow’s sensitivity:

$$-\frac{\partial P}{\partial z_{10}}=10(636.85719270)\approx6368.57192698$$

Divide by the initial price for KRD, or multiply by 0.0001 for EUR per bp:

| Node | Derivative KRD | Bucket DV01, EUR per bp |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2 years | 0.20869682 | 0.01517486 |
| 5 years | 0.15835560 | 0.01151442 |
| 10 years | 8.75857140 | 0.63685719 |
| Sum | **9.12562382** | **0.66354647** |

These are sensitivities to the chosen continuous **spot curve**, not automatically the modified duration of one quoted YTM.

### Why the derivative sum works, and finite bumps only approximate it

Because $\sum_jb_j(t)=1$, the sum of node derivatives equals the derivative of a parallel shift of this same zero curve:

$$\sum_jD_j=\frac{\sum_t t\,\mathrm{PV}_t}{P}=D_{\text{parallel}}$$

A numerical central-bump estimate with decimal $h$ is

$$D_j^{(h)}=\frac{P(z-h e_j)-P(z+h e_j)}{2hP(z)}$$

Here $e_j$ moves just node $j$, with the declared interpolation rule, and one bp means $h=0.0001$. It approximates the derivative as $h$ shrinks. The sum of **finite-bump** KRDs need not equal a finite combined parallel bump exactly: nonlinear terms and mixed-node exposure remain. Nor does a collection of shocks that fails to reconstruct a parallel change have the derivative-sum identity.

### When to use it

Use analytic derivatives or sufficiently small, validated bumps for local risk. Check currency, sign, compounding and rate units before adding buckets. A one-bp exposure of EUR 0.66354647 implies about **EUR −6.635465** for a +10 bp parallel change; exact repricing gives **EUR −6.603289**. That finite difference is curvature, not an arithmetic error. [CFA defines key rate sensitivity as a specific maturity’s benchmark-curve change](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/curve-based-and-empirical-fixed-income-risk-measures).

## Level, slope and curvature are different shock directions

For node changes measured in **basis points**, the first-order currency change is

$$\Delta P_{\text{linear}}\approx-\sum_j q_j\Delta z_j^{\mathrm{bp}}$$

The minus sign belongs to our convention: a positive bucket DV01 loses value when that node’s rate rises. Do not multiply by 0.0001 again; $q_j$ already contains it.

Define three illustrative shapes at nodes 2/5/10. They are chosen scenarios, not statistically estimated principal components:

| Shape | Node shocks in bp | Linear P&L for our fixed stream, EUR | Exact fixed-flow P&L, EUR |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Level | (+10, +10, +10) | −6.635465 | −6.603289 |
| Slope | (−10, 0, +10) | −6.216823 | −6.184954 |
| Curvature | (−5, +10, −5) | +3.145016 | +3.153100 |

For curvature, the arithmetic is

$$\Delta P\approx-\left[0.01517486(-5)+0.01151442(10)+0.63685719(-5)\right]\approx3.145016$$

### A zero total DV01 can hide shape risk

Consider **separate invented net books**, with signed bucket DV01 in EUR per bp:

| Net book | Bucket DV01 at 2/5/10 years | Total DV01 | P&L for the slope shock | P&L for the curvature shock |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | (100, 0, −100) | 0 | +2,000 EUR | 0 EUR |
| B | (100, −200, 100) | 0 | 0 EUR | +3,000 EUR |

For A, slope P&L is $-[100(-10)+0(0)-100(10)]=2000$ EUR. For B, curvature P&L is $-[100(-5)-200(10)+100(-5)]=3000$ EUR. Both have zero first-order P&L for an equal +10 bp shift, yet their exposures to other directions differ. Gains in the displayed scenarios turn into losses when those shocks reverse.

### When to use it

Inspect the vector, not just its sum, when asset and liability cash flows sit at different maturities. Level, slope and curvature are useful summaries, but they need not span every movement of a finer curve. [CFA distinguishes these shaping risks and the role of key rate durations](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/yield-curve-strategies).

## Solve a hedge in several buckets

A single hedge quantity can satisfy one general linear constraint. Matching three independent bucket exposures usually needs enough independent instrument risk directions, or an explicit approximate objective and constraints.

Take a **different book** with $q=(100,300,200)^\top$ EUR per bp. The following invented sensitivities describe **one long unit** of each hedge. They are supplied risk numbers, not prices or the cash flows used above:

| Rate bucket | Book | Hedge 1 | Hedge 2 | Hedge 3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 years | 100 | 20 | 0 | 0 |
| 5 years | 300 | 5 | 40 | 0 |
| 10 years | 200 | 0 | 10 | 50 |

Let $H$ have buckets as rows and instruments as columns, and $n$ be signed hedge units. Target zero linear node exposure:

$$q+Hn=0,\qquad Hn=-q$$

Solve one row at a time:

$$20n_1=-100\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_1=-5$$

$$5(-5)+40n_2=-300\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_2=-6.875$$

$$10(-6.875)+50n_3=-200\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_3=-2.625$$

All three positions are shorts. Substitution gives residual bucket DV01 $(0,0,0)$ before costs under the supplied sensitivities. Consequently any combination of **these three node shocks** has zero first-order P&L. This is not an exact finite-move valuation hedge: no hedge cash flows or convexities have been specified.

### Whole units leave a measurable residual

The algebra allows divisible units. If execution requires whole units, rounding to $(−5,−7,−3)$ produces

$$q+Hn_{\text{rounded}}=(0,-5,-20)^\top\ \mathrm{EUR/bp}$$

For the +10 bp level shock, the residual P&L is $-[0(10)-5(10)-20(10)]=250$ EUR. Reversing the shock loses about EUR 250. Choose an explicit rounding, cost and residual-risk objective; rounding each position independently is a demonstration, not a claim of optimal execution. Actual cash bonds, swaps and futures also have financing, margin, lot-size and basis conventions. [CME illustrates instrument-specific key rate adjustments rather than matching face values alone](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/articles-and-reports/case-study-key-rate-duration-adjustment).

### When to use it

Use a common risk-coordinate system for the book and all hedges, solve the chosen target, and check each residual bucket. Add funding and cash constraints separately: $Hn=-q$ matches sensitivities, not initial cash costs. Revalue finite scenarios and rebalance as sensitivities change.

## Independent hedges and a small residual are separate checks

Two different instrument names can carry nearly the same risk vector. Then their large opposing positions may cancel the fitted buckets while amplifying small input changes and trading costs.

To expose this, replace the third hedge by an invented blend

$$h_3(a)=(1-a)h_2+a h_3,\qquad 0\le a\le1$$

This control changes a hypothetical sensitivity column; it does not describe a freely adjustable real contract. At $a=0.01$, the third column is $(0,39.6,10.4)^\top$. The same book now requires approximately

$$n=(-5,253,-262.5)^\top$$

The residual is still essentially zero, but the gross position size is much larger. Increasing the book’s long-node DV01 from 200 to 201 changes the third hedge quantity by **−2 units** here, compared with **−0.02 units** for the original independent matrix.

At $a=0$, columns 2 and 3 are identical and $H$ is singular. For this particular book, the first two rows force the combined quantity of those hedges to −6.875; their long-node hedge is then only −68.75, which cannot cancel the book’s +200. There is no exact three-bucket solution. More generally, rank loss can leave no solution or many solutions; a unique inverse is unavailable.

A condition number diagnoses sensitivity of the chosen linear system. The visual reports

$$\kappa_\infty(H)=\lVert H\rVert_\infty\lVert H^{-1}\rVert_\infty$$

when the matrix is numerically invertible. Here the infinity norm is the maximum absolute row sum. It is 3 for the original matrix and rises sharply as columns become redundant. [NIST explains conditioning and why a small residual alone need not imply an accurate solution](https://dlmf.nist.gov/3.2.iii). The number depends on the scaling of rows and instrument units; compare it under a consistent convention.

### When to use it

Check rank, conditioning, gross positions and perturbation sensitivity before accepting the residual. A constrained least-squares hedge can trade off residual risk against position limits and costs, but its weights, objective and remaining exposure must be reported. A hedge cannot eliminate risks that its instruments do not span. Curve-node matching also leaves credit spreads, basis, financing and finite-move curvature outside the linear target.

## Reconstruct the hedge

Define the rates and interpolation before the buckets. Calculate node derivatives in consistent units, test several shock directions, and keep finite repricing beside the local estimate. Solve the book-plus-hedge vector, then test rounding, rank, conditioning and costs. A zero sum or a tiny fitted residual answers only part of the question.
