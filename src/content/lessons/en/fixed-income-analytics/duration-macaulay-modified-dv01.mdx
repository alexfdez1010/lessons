A bond’s cash-flow balance point is 2.83 years. Does that mean a 1% yield rise costs 2.83%, or €2.83? Neither conclusion follows until the yield convention, price and sensitivity measure are specified. Duration helps turn a rate move into a price estimate; units and signs do much of the work.

We use invented **option-free fixed-rate bonds**, with positive scheduled cash flows held unchanged. Settlement is immediately after a coupon date, so there is no accrued interest and clean and dirty prices coincide. Default, fees, tax and changes in cash-flow expectations are excluded. These are yield-based sensitivities to a bond’s own quoted yield, not a complete description of credit or yield-curve risk.

Let face value be $F$, annual coupon rate $c$, maturity $T$ years and $m$ equally spaced payments per year. Here **the yield-compounding frequency also equals $m$**. The quoted yield $y$ is a nominal annual yield, expressed as a decimal and compounded $m$ times annually. There are $N=mT$ payments, on a regular schedule with no stub periods:

$$t_k=\frac{k}{m},\qquad CF_k=\frac{Fc}{m}+F\mathbf{1}_{\{k=N\}}$$

The indicator adds principal only at the final payment. With $1+y/m>0$, the price is

$$P(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{CF_k}{(1+y/m)^k}$$

The period index $k$ counts payments; $t_k$ measures **years**. Confusing the two doubles a semiannual Macaulay duration accidentally.

### Compare annual and semiannual clocks

For face €1,000, coupon rate 6%, maturity three years and nominal annual yield 6%:

| Stated frequency | Coupon each period | Periodic discount yield | Scheduled times in years |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Annual, m=1 | €60 | 6% | 1,2,3 |
| Semiannual, m=2 | €30 | 3% | 0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5,3 |

Both price at par under their respective matching coupon and yield conventions. They are different payment schedules, and the annual effective yield differs: annual compounding gives 6%; semiannual compounding gives $(1.03)^2-1=6.09\%$.

### When to use it

Establish the quote before calculating duration. Coupon frequency alone does not universally specify yield compounding; we deliberately align them here. Real settlement dates, day counts, irregular periods and accrued interest require the instrument’s full pricing conventions. The [RBI primer gives a semiannual Macaulay-to-modified example](https://m.rbi.org.in/commonman/english/scripts/FAQs.aspx?Id=711); its market-specific details should not be treated as universal contract terms.

## Macaulay duration is a weighted average of payment times

Imagine each scheduled payment as a weight on a timeline. Its weight is its **present value**, not its face cash amount. The **Macaulay duration** is the time at which those weights balance:

$$PV_k=\frac{CF_k}{(1+y/m)^k},\qquad w_k=\frac{PV_k}{P},\qquad D_{\mathrm{Mac}}=\sum_k t_kw_k$$

The weights sum to one. Macaulay duration measures **years until payment, averaged by present value**. It is not a literal date when all invested capital is returned.

### Derive the three-year annual example

Take the annual bond: €60 at years one and two, and €1,060 at year three, discounted at 6% annually.

| k and time in years | Cash flow | Present value | Time × present value |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | €60 | €56.6037736 | €56.6037736 years |
| 2 | €60 | €53.3997864 | €106.7995728 years |
| 3 | €1,060 | €889.9964400 | €2,669.9893200 years |
| Sum | — | **€1,000.0000000** | **€2,833.3926664 years** |

$$D_{\mathrm{Mac}}=\frac{2833.3926664}{1000}\approx2.8333926664\text{ years}$$

The early coupons pull the average before year three. A zero-coupon bond has all positive PV weight at its single maturity payment, so $D_{\mathrm{Mac}}=T$. More generally, the average lies between the earliest **positive** payment time and maturity. It is strictly below maturity when a positive payment arrives earlier; a one-payment bond can equal maturity even if that final payment includes a coupon.

### When to use it

Use Macaulay duration to understand the timing of discounted cash flows and as one ingredient in specified immunization models. A duration-matching strategy requires further assumptions about liabilities, reinvestment and the rate changes being hedged; an average payment time alone is not a repayment guarantee.

## Modified duration differentiates price with respect to the quoted yield

The weighted time becomes a price sensitivity when we differentiate the pricing formula, keeping every scheduled cash flow fixed:

$$P'(y)=-\sum_k\frac{k}{m}\, CF_k(1+y/m)^{-k-1}=-\frac{P D_{\mathrm{Mac}}}{1+y/m}$$

Define **modified duration** as the negative relative price slope:

$$D_{\mathrm{mod}}=-\frac{P'(y)}{P}=\frac{D_{\mathrm{Mac}}}{1+y/m}$$

It is a local sensitivity to **this decimal nominal annual yield**. Modified duration is often quoted in years too, but its role here is a derivative rather than an average payment time. The numeric value 2.673 is not 2.673%=0.02673 in the price-change formula.

### Convert a yield move into a relative price move

For the annual example,

$$D_{\mathrm{mod}}=\frac{2.8333926664}{1.06}\approx2.6730119495$$

A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point: **1 bp =0.01 percentage points =0.0001 in decimal yield**. A rise from 6% to 6.5% is +50 bp, so $\Delta y=0.005$, not 0.5 or 0.50.

The first-order approximation is

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P}\approx-D_{\mathrm{mod}}\Delta y=-2.6730119495(0.005)\approx-0.0133650597$$

That is **−1.33650597%**, or about **−€13.3651 per €1,000 bond**, giving an approximate price of €986.6349. The minus sign links a positive yield shock to a falling price for these fixed positive cash flows.

### Frequency changes the conversion

For the semiannual version, the six discounted flows sum to €1,000 and their time-weighted present values sum to **€2,789.8535936 years**. Therefore $D_{\mathrm{Mac}}\approx2.7898535936$ years and

$$D_{\mathrm{mod}}=\frac{2.7898535936}{1.03}\approx2.7085957219$$

Do not simply reuse the annual bond’s Macaulay value: its payment times and weights changed. Nor should you divide the semiannual value by 1.06. The correct denominator is 1+y/m for the stated quote.

A different yield coordinate has a different derivative. For the same semiannual cash flows, the equivalent effective annual yield is $a=(1+y/2)^2-1=0.0609$. Pricing as $\sum CF_k(1+a)^{-t_k}$ gives sensitivity $D_a=D_{\mathrm{Mac}}/(1+a)\approx2.6297045844$. It describes a change in **effective annual yield**, not a change in the nominal semiannual quote. Both are valid when paired with their own yield changes.

### When to use it

Use modified duration for a local percentage price estimate, with the same yield definition used to calculate it. A 100 bp rise means $\Delta y=0.01$; multiplying by a modified duration of 7 gives an approximate 7% price decline. It does not mean that duration itself is a 7% return. The [CFA Institute distinguishes yield-duration measures from sensitivities to a benchmark curve](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/yield-based-bond-duration-measures-and-properties).

## DV01 converts the slope into currency per basis point

A **DV01** translates relative sensitivity into a currency amount. The acronym traditionally means “dollar value of one basis point”; here the invented bonds are in euros. We choose a **positive magnitude for a long plain bond**:

$$\operatorname{DV01}=-P'(y)\,10^{-4}=P D_{\mathrm{mod}}\,10^{-4}$$

Under this convention, the **signed first-order price change for a +1 bp rise** is **−DV01**. Some systems report that signed change instead, or use a finite bump. Check the shock direction, sign convention, valuation method and position unit before combining numbers called DV01. Names such as PV01 can also refer to curve-bump conventions; the label alone does not define the calculation.

### Scale a single bond into a position

For the annual example,

$$\operatorname{DV01}=1000(2.6730119495)(0.0001)\approx0.2673011949\text{ euros per bp}$$

For 10,000 identical bonds, face and market value are each €10 million **in this par example**. Position DV01 is 10,000 ×€0.2673011949 ≈ **€2,673.011949 per bp**. A +25 bp shock gives approximate P&L

$$\Delta V\approx-2673.011949(25)\approx-66825.30\text{ euros}$$

A −25 bp shock reverses this **first-order** sign. Use market price, not face value, in $P D_{\mathrm{mod}}$: a premium bond priced at €1,200 with modified duration 8 has DV01€1,200 ×8 ×0.0001 = **€0.96**, not €0.80 based on a €1,000 face assumption. [CME’s investor education defines basis-point value and illustrates its use for rate-risk scaling](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/courses/introduction-to-treasuries/how-can-you-measure-risk-in-treasuries).

### A finite bump is close, not identical, to the derivative

One symmetric finite-bump convention is $[P(y-0.0001)-P(y +0.0001)]/2$. For the annual example, the repriced amounts are €1,000.2673507 and €999.7327483, giving **€0.2673012026 per bp**. This closely approximates the derivative DV01€0.2673011949; the tiny difference is a finite-step effect. Neither should be confused with the signed up-bump change, €999.7327483−€1,000≈ **−€0.2672517**.

### When to use it

Use currency-per-bp risk to size a position or a hedge against a specified rate shock. State whether the figure is per bond, per face amount or for the full position, and retain signed position quantities. For a shock $\Delta b$ measured in basis points, the approximation is **−position DV01 ×Δb**. Do not multiply by 0.0001 again after converting the risk to currency per bp.

## Exact repricing reveals the approximation’s scope

Duration supplies the tangent at today’s yield. It does not replace the full pricing function for a finite shock. For the annual 3-year example:

| Annual nominal yield shock | Duration price estimate | Exact fixed-flow repricing |
|---:|---:|---:|
| −200 bp | €1,053.4602 | €1,055.5018 |
| −50 bp | €1,013.3651 | €1,013.4897 |
| +50 bp | €986.6349 | €986.7576 |
| +200 bp | €946.5398 | €948.4581 |

For +50 bp, the exact price follows directly from $60/1.065+60/1.065^2+1060/1.065^3\approx986.7576224$. The tangent is about **€0.1227 lower**. Larger shocks in this example increase the curvature gap.

For fixed positive option-free cash flows and a positive discount base, the price function is convex: its tangent lies below the curve. Within that model, the linear estimate overstates the loss for a yield rise and understates the gain for a fall. Callable bonds, changing expected cash flows and other risk measures need not have this positive-convexity pattern. Nor does a pricing model ensure the promised payments will actually arrive. The [next lesson on convexity](/fixed-income-analytics/convexity) adds a second-order term, which is still an approximation.

### Read cash-flow patterns before using a rule of thumb

At fixed yield and maturity, adding earlier positive coupon weight moves Macaulay duration toward earlier times. For a given fixed schedule, raising yield reduces the relative PV weight of later payments. A longer maturity often raises duration, but it is not a universal ordering for all coupon-bond schedules.

For an original counterexample, take annual coupon 1%, yield 10% and face €1,000. Calculate $P=10\sum_{k=1}^{T}(1.10)^{-k}+1000(1.10)^{-T}$ and the same PV-weighted time sum:

| Maturity | Price | Macaulay duration | Modified duration |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 30 years | €151.577698 | 17.049286 years | 15.499351 |
| 40 years | €119.885435 | 15.607509 years | 14.188644 |

Despite the longer maturity, the duration falls in this deep-discount example. Changing maturity changes the placement of principal and the number of coupons. Reproduce both rows with the controls instead of treating “longer means more sensitive” as an unrestricted theorem. For **zero-coupon** bonds at the same quoted yield and frequency, the relation is exact:$D_{\mathrm{mod}}=T/(1+y/m)$.

### When to use it

Use duration for small shocks and quick comparisons under stated conventions, then reprice finite shocks when accuracy matters. Quantify the approximation gap for the position rather than declaring a universal “safe” shock size. A hedge against a common yield move does not remove credit, liquidity, option or curve-shape risk.

## Aggregate a common shock with signed currency risk

For positive market values $V_i$ and a **common decimal yield shock under consistent quotes**, the first-order changes add:

$$\Delta V\approx-\sum_i V_iD_i\Delta y$$

If total value $V=\sum_iV_i$ is nonzero, the corresponding modified duration is $D_{\mathrm{port}}=\sum_i(V_i/V)D_i$. It is a value-weighted average for an ordinary all-long bond portfolio.

### Work the weights and the currency sum

€6 million with modified duration 3 contributes DV01€6,000,000 ×3 ×0.0001 = **€1,800**. €4 million with duration 9 contributes **€3,600**. The total is **€5,400 per bp**, and portfolio duration is

$$D_{\mathrm{port}}=0.6(3)+0.4(9)=5.4$$

A common +10 bp yield move gives approximate P&L −€54,000. For long and short quantities $n_i$, keep their signs in $K=\sum_i n_i\operatorname{DV01}_i$, then use $\Delta V\approx-K\Delta b$. A short position’s coefficient is negative and can offset a positive long coefficient. If net market value is zero, a duration ratio dividing by that net value is undefined, even when the signed currency risk remains perfectly calculable.

### When to use it

Add currency sensitivities only after aligning currency, price units, shock definition and signs. Own-YTM duration aggregation is not automatically an exact hedge against every benchmark-curve shift. A curve twist can move cash-flow dates differently; key-rate or curve-bucket sensitivities examine that extra structure. Duration matching also needs the appropriate asset and liability values and assumptions, it is not an unconditional hedge guarantee.
