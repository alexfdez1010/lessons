## The plumbing — collateral and positions on Polygon

**Analogy.** Think of digital chips backed by another asset. Holding a chip and holding a bank dollar are different claims: you must understand the backing and the operations needed to recover it.

**Definition.** A **stablecoin** is designed to track a reference value, not guaranteed to trade at that value forever. Checked on 6 November 2026, the international [pUSD documentation](https://docs.polymarket.com/concepts/pusd) describes pUSD on Polygon backed by USDC. The [position documentation](https://docs.polymarket.com/concepts/positions-tokens) distinguishes CTF balances from Protocol V2 PositionManager balances. The applicable ledger and contracts determine how positions and collateral are held and released; this is not one unchanging USDC-only mechanism.

**Worked example.** Assume 200 settlement units are available and each is worth one reporting dollar. Buying 100 YES shares at 0.62 uses 62 units before any additional cost, leaving 138. If a held settlement unit were later worth 0.98 reporting dollars, 100 units would be worth 98 dollars. The unit accounting remains valid, but the dollar-value assumption changes.

**Pitfall.** “Non-custodial” does not mean immune to loss, restrictions or unavailable operations. Keys, approvals, contracts, the collateral asset and the path to withdrawal still matter. pUSD backing is not a promise that every user can always obtain a bank dollar instantly; the documentation itself describes contract conditions, including pause conditions on some operations.

### When it matters

Before funding or reclaiming collateral, check the current token, network, ledger, approvals, withdrawal path and access rules. Network gas and any trading or intermediary fee are separate costs; do not infer a fixed tiny charge for every action from the network name. The [sizing lesson](/polymarket-prediction-markets/sizing-bets-and-the-real-risks) separates collateral-valued payoffs from probability, cash cost and practical access.

## Outcome tokens & the complete set — the \$1 conservation law

**Analogy.** In the full binary teaching model, a matched YES/NO pair is like two complementary tickets backed by one settlement unit. One pays the unit and the other pays zero. That describes their combined payout, not two independently executable quotes or a guarantee about the unit's bank-dollar value.

**Definition.** A **complete set** contains equal YES and NO quantities for the same market. Current documentation describes one full pair backed by one pUSD of value, with release depending on the position system. Some binary markets permit split payouts; the full zero-or-one examples here are a narrower model. In a frictionless, internally consistent valuation of one pair, let P denote values in settlement units:

$$P_{\text{YES}} + P_{\text{NO}} = 1.$$

The complete-set payout identity does not require the two actual ask prices, bid prices or last trades to sum to one. A quoted value, an executable purchase cost and an accessible collateral operation are different objects.

**Worked example.** If the theoretical YES value is 0.62 in this frictionless one-unit model, NO is valued at 1 − 0.62 = **0.38**. Buying an executable pair for 1.02 would exceed its one-unit model payout by 0.02 before costs; buying one for 0.96 would leave a gross 0.04 difference only if the pair can actually be acquired and converted as assumed. Fees, execution, collateral and operation constraints must still be checked; the subtraction alone is not risk-free cash.

**Pitfall.** "If NO is cheap, the event is unlikely, so NO is a bad bet." No — *cheapness is the whole point*. NO at 38¢ pays \$1 if the event fails: that's a profit of 62¢ on a 38¢ stake (a 163% return) when it hits. A low price means a low implied probability *and* a high payoff-per-dollar. The price already encodes the odds; there is no separate "bad bet" penalty.

### When it matters

The complete-set accounting explains splitting and merging. Keep its one-unit model distinct from the prices you can actually trade and the collateral you can actually recover.

## Minting & merging — making and unmaking the dollar

**Analogy.** A splitter and joiner exchange a settlement unit for a matched pair and back again. The picture assumes the corresponding operations work; it does not promise free processing or unlimited availability.

**Definition.** **Splitting** converts collateral into equal YES and NO positions. **Merging** converts a matched pair back into collateral; **redemption** after resolution follows the recorded payout and is a different operation. Current documentation describes these operations for pUSD-backed positions with system-specific contracts. Merging avoids finding an order-book buyer for a full pair, but still depends on the correct market, ledger, permissions and available contract operation.

This is the source of every share in the market. Sellers usually aren't "the house" — they're traders who minted a set and then sold the half they didn't want.

**Worked example — minting then selling the leg you don't want.** You're bullish on YES and the order book quotes NO buyers at 38¢. Instead of buying YES outright, you:

1. **Mint:** deposit one settlement unit → receive 1 YES + 1 NO.
2. **Sell the NO** into the order book at 38¢ → collect \$0.38.
3. **Keep the YES.** Your net cash outlay was $1.00 − $0.38 = **\$0.62**.

You now hold one YES share for a net model cost of 0.62 units before additional costs. Direct purchase and splitting-then-selling can be compared using their actual net cash flows; neither route must always be cheaper. A matched pair may be merged through its applicable operation rather than sold, subject to the stated collateral and contract conditions.

The splitter illustrates complementary theoretical values and matched-pair accounting in the fee-free one-unit model. Its NO = 1 − YES control is not a live two-book quote or a promise of permanent dollar parity.

## The CLOB — a central limit order book, not an AMM

**Analogy.** A stock exchange's bulletin board. On one side, buyers post: "I'll pay up to 60¢ for 500 YES." On the other, sellers post: "I'll sell 250 YES at 62¢." A match happens when a buyer's price meets a seller's. Nobody's bet is against "the house" — every trade is one trader meeting another on the board.

**Definition.** Polymarket matches trades through a **CLOB — a central limit order book**: for each outcome token, a live, sorted list of resting **limit orders**. **Bids** are buy orders (sorted highest-price-first); **asks** (or offers) are sell orders (sorted lowest-price-first). The **best bid** is the highest someone will pay; the **best ask** is the lowest someone will sell for. The gap between them is the **spread**, and the **mid** — halfway between best bid and best ask — is the market's cleanest read of the implied probability.

$$\text{mid} = \frac{\text{best bid} + \text{best ask}}{2} \approx \text{implied probability}.$$

**This is *not* an AMM.** An *automated market maker* (AMM) like Uniswap prices trades off a formula against a shared liquidity pool — there's no order book, just a curve. A CLOB instead has named, individual orders you can hit or join. The practical upshot: on a CLOB you can post a **limit order** and *provide* liquidity (earning the spread when someone trades against you), exactly like a market-maker, rather than only trading against a pool.

**Worked example.** Best bid for YES is 60¢ (someone wants to buy), best ask is 62¢ (someone wants to sell). The **spread** is 62 − 60 = **2¢**. The **mid** is (60 + 62)/2 = **61¢**, so the market reads the event as ~61% likely. If you place a market buy, you pay the *ask* (62¢); if you place a market sell, you hit the *bid* (60¢). To **bet NO**, you don't short anything — you simply switch to the NO token's book and **buy NO shares** there. No borrowing, no margin call, no locating shares to short: NO exposure is just owning NO tokens.

**Pitfall.** "The mid is the price I'll get." No — the mid is a *reference*, not a fill. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid; the mid is the average of the two, and you only ever transact at one side of it (worse than mid by half the spread, at least). Confusing the mid with your fill price is the first step toward being surprised by your cost.

## Spread, depth & slippage — why size costs more

**Analogy.** Buying out a farmers' market's tomatoes. The first crate is cheap, but once you've cleared the cheapest stall you have to move to the next, pricier stall, then the next. The *more* you buy at once, the *higher* the average price you end up paying — because you eat through the cheap supply and climb into the expensive stuff.

**Definition.** **Depth** is how many shares are resting at each price level. A **market order** "walks the book": it fills against the best price first, and when that level's size is exhausted, it rolls up to the next-best price, and so on. **Slippage** is the gap between the price you *hoped* for (the best ask) and the **volume-weighted average price** you actually paid after climbing several levels. Thin books (little depth) produce big slippage; deep books absorb size with barely a wobble.

**Worked example — walking the ask ladder.** Suppose the YES ask side looks like this, and you send a **market buy for 300 shares**:

| Ask level | Shares available | Shares you take | Cost of that slice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 62¢ | 150 | 150 | 150 × 0.62 = \$93.00 |
| 63¢ | 100 | 100 | 100 × 0.63 = \$63.00 |
| 65¢ | 200 | 50 | 50 × 0.65 = \$32.50 |
| **Total** | — | **300** | **\$188.50** |

Your **average fill price** is \$188.50 / 300 = **62.83¢** per share — not the 62¢ you saw quoted. The **slippage** is 62.83 − 62 = **0.83¢ per share** (about \$2.50 across the whole order) because your size forced you up into the 63¢ and 65¢ levels. Had you bought only 150 shares, you'd have filled entirely at 62¢ with zero slippage. Size *is* the slippage.

**Pitfall.** Reading the top-of-book price and assuming the whole order fills there. On a thin market, a single large order can blow through several levels and pay *cents* more per share than the quote — and on a *very* thin book it may only **partially fill** (there simply aren't enough sellers). Always check the *depth*, not just the best ask, before sizing up.

### When it matters

Spread and depth can matter even for a small order when the intended edge is thin. A passive limit order may reduce the price paid, but may not fill and can face adverse selection. Compare that execution choice with the actual fees rather than assuming a universal dominant cost.

## Fees & gas — where the cost actually hides

**Analogy.** A "free" checking account that still nickel-and-dimes you on the spread when you exchange currency. The advertised price (zero monthly fee) isn't where the money goes — the cost is baked into the *exchange rate* you're quoted. Same story here.

**Definition.** The [current fee page](https://docs.polymarket.com/trading/fees) describes market-specific taker fees and zero maker fees. Network transaction costs and intermediary charges are separate. A fill price already reflects the levels crossed; add separately paid fees without counting the same spread or slippage twice. There is no universal “all costs are tiny” assumption.

**Worked example.** The existing 300-share ladder costs 188.50 units before additional fees, giving 0.6283 per share. Relative to buying all 300 at the initial 0.62 ask, the extra trade value is 2.50 units. Any separately paid fee must be added; no network fee has been specified in this example.

**Pitfall.** Judging a market as "cheap to trade" off its advertised fees alone. A zero-fee market with a 5¢ spread and shallow depth is *expensive* to trade in size; a market with a tiny fee but a 1¢ spread and deep books is cheap. Always price the spread and expected slippage into your edge — a thin edge can be entirely eaten by crossing a wide spread.

### When it matters

Check actual execution prices, market-specific fees, any network or intermediary cost and the net quantity received, even for a small trade. A fee-free market can still have an expensive spread; a low spread does not remove other costs. The sizing lesson shows a current fee-formula example and avoids counting a spread twice after using actual fills.

Now you know what you're holding, where it lives, and how it trades. But one giant question still hangs over every market: when the event is over, **who decides who was right**? A market can quote "62% chance the bill passes" all day, yet someone — or some*thing* — must ultimately rule YES or NO so the winning shares can pay out their dollar. Next lesson we meet **resolution and the UMA optimistic oracle**: a clever escalation game where anyone can propose an outcome, anyone can dispute it by putting money on the line, and a decentralized vote settles the rare contested case. It's where a prediction market's promise either becomes a paid-out dollar — or a controversy.
