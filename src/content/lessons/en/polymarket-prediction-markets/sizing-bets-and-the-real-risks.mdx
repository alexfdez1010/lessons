Buying **500 YES shares at 0.60** costs **300 settlement units before fees**. Their full redemption notional is **500 units**. In the fully funded binary example, a zero payout loses the 300 spent; a full payout returns 500, making 200 profit. “A position of 500” leaves the most important question unanswered: 500 *what*?

We will keep quantities, costs and outcomes separate, then examine what a probability estimate can justify. All numeric scenarios are invented unless explicitly identified as an example of the current fee formula. They are calculations under stated assumptions, not allocation recommendations.

## Quantity, purchase cost, funded loss and redemption notional

 {text:'The maximum loss is 200, equal to the possible winning profit'},
]} explanation="Purchase cost is 500 × 0.60 = 300. Full payout is 500 × 1 = 500, so a win earns 200 and a zero payout loses 300. No loan or further liability is assumed." />

Your **bankroll** is the amount assigned to the trading plan, not automatically every asset you own. **Net share quantity**, $Q$, is the actual outcome-token amount received. **Purchase cost** is $Qc$, where $c$ is the average executed price per net share. **Total outlay** also includes separately paid entry costs.

For this standalone **fully funded long position**, with no borrowing or additional obligations, the capital lost if the shares pay zero is the outlay. This is not the same as **redemption notional**, which we define as $Q$ times the contractual full payout unit. “Notional” is a named payoff scale here, not cash already spent or a guarantee of dollar redemption.

| Item, without fees | Arithmetic | Amount |
|---|---|---:|
| Share quantity | Given | 500 shares |
| Purchase cost | $500(0.60)$ | 300 |
| Full redemption notional | $500(1)$ | 500 |
| Net profit at full payout | $500-300$ | +200 |
| Net profit at zero payout | $0-300$ | −300 |
| Bankroll after full payout | $1{,}200-300+500$ | 1,400 |
| Bankroll after zero payout | $1{,}200-300$ | 900 |

### When these distinctions matter

A “25% Kelly fraction” in the simple fully funded model refers to **cash put at risk as a fraction of bankroll**, not 25% of the share count or notional. Here $300/1{,}200=25\%$, while $500/1{,}200=41.6667\%$ is the redemption-notional ratio. Fees, leverage, collateral losses or other wallet exposures require additional accounting; the standalone loss bound does not describe every possible loss to an entire account.

## Compare a payout belief with an executable all-in cost

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Your payout probability is 60%. A display shows 0.59, but your order would actually cost 0.61 per net share including its entry fee. Is the model edge positive?" options={[
 {text:'No. Expected payout 0.60 is below all-in cost 0.61',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes. Always subtract the displayed 0.59'},
 {text:'Yes. A probability above one half makes every YES purchase profitable'},

Let $p$ be your predictive probability of this contract paying its **full YES amount** in the binary model. With an all-in entry cost $d$ per net share, expected profit per share is

$$
EV=p(1-d)+(1-p)(-d)=p-d.
$$

For $Q$ shares, expected profit is $Q(p-d)$. A positive model expectation is conditional on the probability, payout, cost and execution assumptions; it is not a requirement to take the trade regardless of the rest of your portfolio.

The [official price and order-book documentation](https://docs.polymarket.com/concepts/prices-orderbook) distinguishes displayed prices from bids, asks and fills. With a bid of 0.58 and ask of 0.62, the midpoint is $(0.58+0.62)/2=0.60$, but an immediate small purchase against the ask costs 0.62 before fees. A larger purchase may reach several levels. Do not subtract the midpoint and count the spread again as a separate cost if you already used the actual ask or average fill.

### A probability estimate needs its own assumptions

A **prior** is a starting probability; a **likelihood** describes how probable evidence is under each possible state; a **posterior** updates the prior after observing that evidence. In an invented forecasting model, let the prior be 0.60, and evidence probabilities be 0.75 if the relevant event occurs and 0.35 if it does not. Let E name the event and D the evidence. Bayes gives

$$
P(E\mid D)=\frac{0.75(0.60)}{0.75(0.60)+0.35(0.40)}=\frac{0.45}{0.59}\approx0.7627.
$$

Those likelihoods are assumptions, not measured election statistics. A market price can provide useful information, but treating it as another independent observation after using the same underlying evidence can double count that information. A weighted average with the price is not automatically a Bayesian update. We use **$p=0.70$ as a separate illustrative payout belief below**, not as a result mechanically implied by this Bayes calculation.

### Costs: an actual fee parameter is not a universal percentage of stake

At the review date, the [official fee page](https://docs.polymarket.com/trading/fees) describes market-specific taker fees, zero maker fees and a formula based on shares, the execution price and a market fee parameter. Writing the execution price as $c$ to avoid confusing it with our probability $p$:

$$
F=Q\tau c(1-c).
$$

For the documented parameter example $\tau=0.04$, $Q=500$ and $c=0.60$, the formula gives $500(0.04)(0.60)(0.40)=4.80$ USDC under that fee calculation. This is **1.6% of the 300 purchase value**, not a flat 4% of that value. It is an illustration of that parameter, not a tariff for every market or future date.

Assume, specifically for our worked sizing bridge, a one-for-one reporting conversion between the fee and settlement units, an additional cash payment for the fee, and receipt of all 500 net shares. Then outlay is $300+4.80=304.80$, so $d=304.80/500=0.6096$. With $p=0.70$, expected profit is $500(0.70-0.6096)=45.20$, rather than 50 before costs. If $p=0.60$, the expected profit is $500(-0.0096)=-4.80$.

Use actual paid amounts and net received shares if a fee changes quantity instead. For fills at different prices, sum the actual costs and fees; applying a price-dependent fee formula once to a quoted average need not reproduce the fill-by-fill total.

## Kelly sizes the funded cost under a payoff model

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="For p = 0.70, c = 0.60 and no costs, binary Kelly gives 25%. On a 1,200 bankroll, what does that represent?" options={[
 {text:'300 of cash at risk, buying 500 shares in the continuous model',correct:true},
 {text:'300 shares, regardless of price'},
 {text:'A guaranteed 25% return on the next outcome'},
]} explanation="The Kelly fraction is the funded stake divided by bankroll. The share quantity then follows from stake divided by the all-in cost per share." />

If fraction $f$ of bankroll buys shares at all-in cost $d$ each, the net odds per unit of funded stake are $b=(1-d)/d$. A full payout multiplies wealth by $1+bf$; a zero payout multiplies it by $1-f$. Expected log growth is

$$
g(f)=p\ln(1+bf)+(1-p)\ln(1-f).
$$

For a known binary distribution, $0<d<1$ and an unconstrained interior optimum, maximizing this function gives

$$
f^*=\frac{p-d}{1-d}.
$$

For nonnegative purchases, choose zero when the expression is nonpositive. If the all-in cost is at least the maximum payout, this model has no positive-return long allocation. Real quantities, position limits and costs may impose further constraints. The repeated-growth interpretation also needs the model's opportunity and probability assumptions; it does not guarantee growth on each outcome.

Without fees, $d=0.60$ and $p=0.70$, so $f^*=0.10/0.40=0.25$. With the separately specified per-share fee above, $d=0.6096$, so

$$
f^*=\frac{0.70-0.6096}{1-0.6096}=\frac{0.0904}{0.3904}\approx0.23156.
$$

For NO, use its **actual all-in ask** and the appropriate NO payout probability. In a full binary model $p_{NO}=1-p$, but its executable cost need not be $1-c_{YES}$. If $p=0.40$ and NO costs 0.55 without fees, $f^*_{NO}=(0.60-0.55)/0.45\approx11.11\%$, rather than the 20% obtained by assuming a 0.50 NO cost.

### Uncertain beliefs: a fractional stake is not a safety guarantee

**Fractional Kelly** scales the model optimum, for example $0.5(25\%)=12.5\%$. It reduces exposure relative to that computed optimum. It does not establish that the input probability is correct, that returns are guaranteed, or that every chosen fraction remains below the true optimum.

If the actual payout probability were 0.58 rather than 0.70 at a cost of 0.60, every positive purchase has negative expected profit. Halving its size reduces the amount exposed; it does not turn the edge positive. The new simulator lets you change the assumed payout probability while preserving the purchase's conditional outcomes.

<PredictionPositionRisk client:visible title="An estimated edge and a funded stake are separate inputs" allocation={0.254} entryFee={0.0096} showKelly />

In the original no-fee model, the exact positive zero of expected log growth is about **47.16%**, not a universal twice-Kelly rule. A 50% stake is already beyond that root in this particular model. With a stake below 100%, finitely many ideal losses leave positive wealth, but a practical drawdown floor can still be crossed. Four consecutive losses at full Kelly leave $1{,}200(0.75)^4=379.6875$; at half Kelly they leave $1{,}200(0.875)^4\approx703.41797$. Neither path is promised, and fractional stakes do not remove drawdowns.

## Resolution risk belongs in a payoff distribution

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="You assign 70% probability to a real-world event. Does that automatically mean a particular contract pays one with 70% probability?" options={[
 {text:'No. Its definition, resolution mechanism and other possible payouts matter',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes. Every event description has exactly the same contract interpretation'},
 {text:'No. Resolution risk is always completely absent from the price'},
]} explanation="A belief about the world and a belief about this contract's payout are different objects. Known resolution risks may already influence prices and your own estimate." />

The [official resolution documentation](https://docs.polymarket.com/concepts/resolution) says the rules, not just the title, define how a market resolves. It currently distinguishes UMA-resolved prediction markets from Chainlink TWAP up/down markets. The [position documentation](https://docs.polymarket.com/concepts/positions-tokens) also describes possible split payouts in some binary markets. Do not apply one resolution story to every product.

Let $E$ denote the real-world event and let $Y$ be the eventual payout per share. In a full binary contract, use the probability of $Y=1$. In an invented **one-sided failure** model, $P(Y=1\mid E)=0.95$ and $P(Y=1\mid \neg E)=0$. If $P(E)=0.70$, then

$$
P(Y=1)=0.95(0.70)+0(0.30)=0.665.
$$

That recovers the familiar 5% haircut **only under those conditional assumptions**. If instead a 5% error symmetrically flips either event outcome, $P(Y=1\mid\neg E)=0.05$ and the result is $0.95(0.70)+0.05(0.30)=0.68$. These are invented risk models, not measured oracle failure rates.

### A partial payout is not merely another success probability

Suppose a hypothetical contract has these terminal payout scenarios:

| Payout Y | Assumed probability | Contribution to expected payout |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.65 | 0.65 |
| 0.5 | 0.10 | 0.05 |
| 0 | 0.25 | 0 |
| Total | 1 | 0.70 |

Expected payout is $E[Y]=0.65(1)+0.10(0.5)=0.70$, while the probability of a **positive** payout is 0.75. At cost 0.60, expected profit is still 0.10 per share, but a binary Kelly formula with “success probability 0.75” uses the wrong payoff model. For general payout states $Y_j$ with probabilities $\pi_j$, the log objective is

$$
g(f)=\sum_j\pi_j\ln\left(1+f\left(\frac{Y_j}{d}-1\right)\right),
$$

on the domain where each possible wealth multiplier is positive.

A collateral loss can change payout **magnitude** too. If a successful share yields 0.95 reporting dollars with probability 0.70, and otherwise zero, expected payout is $0.70(0.95)=0.665$. At cost 0.60 its net odds are $(0.95-0.60)/0.60$, and its binary optimum is $(0.665-0.60)/(0.95-0.60)\approx18.57\%$. Replacing that distribution by a full one-dollar payout with probability 0.665 instead gives $(0.665-0.60)/0.40=16.25\%$. Equal expected payouts do not make the distributions or log optima identical.

### When to revise a belief, and when to stop

A price may reflect anticipated wording, collateral and oracle risks. Your payout forecast may already include them. Do not mechanically deduct the same risk again. Distinguish an uncertain payout, a separately priced cost, a restriction on trading and a joint portfolio exposure. If the contract cannot be interpreted or access is unavailable, reducing the number in a formula does not resolve that constraint.

## Size a book with joint outcomes, not a sum of separate Kelly slogans

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Two positions each have a 60% chance of full payout. Do those marginal probabilities tell you the chance that both pay zero?" options={[
 {text:'No. Their dependence must also be specified',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes. It is always 40% × 40% = 16%'},
 {text:'Yes. Different market titles guarantee independence'},
]} explanation="Multiplying failure probabilities assumes independence. Related events can share losses, and separate titles do not establish a joint distribution." />

A **marginal probability** describes one outcome alone. A **joint probability** describes outcomes together. **Correlation** summarizes a form of co-movement; it does not by itself specify every feature of a larger portfolio's joint distribution.

Take a bankroll of 1,000. Buy 200 shares in market A at 0.50 and 200 in B at 0.50, without fees: each costs 100 and total outlay is 200. Let each full-payout probability be 0.60. Expected profit per position is $200(0.60)-100=20$, so the combined expectation is 40.

| Joint payout state | Independent probabilities | Perfectly linked probabilities | Portfolio profit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A YES, B YES | 0.36 | 0.60 | $400-200=+200$ |
| A YES, B NO | 0.24 | 0 | $200-200=0$ |
| A NO, B YES | 0.24 | 0 | $200-200=0$ |
| A NO, B NO | 0.16 | 0.40 | $0-200=-200$ |

Under independence, expected profit is $0.36(200)+0.16(-200)=40$. Under perfect linkage it is $0.60(200)+0.40(-200)=40$ too. But the chance of a 200 loss increases from **16% to 40%**. The expectation alone does not describe the risk.

### Quantify dependence and dispersion in this distribution

For the two zero-or-one payout indicators, correlation is covariance divided by their standard deviations. With marginal probabilities 0.60, each indicator has variance $0.60(0.40)=0.24$. If $t$ is the probability both pay one, covariance is $t-0.60(0.60)=t-0.36$, so correlation is $(t-0.36)/0.24$. It is zero at $t=0.36$ and one at $t=0.60$. Zero correlation alone is not a general independence test for arbitrary portfolios.

**Variance** averages squared deviations from the expected profit. In the independent example, expected profit is 40 and the possible profits are 200, 0, 0 and −200. Its variance is

$$
0.36(160)^2+0.24(40)^2+0.24(40)^2+0.16(240)^2=19{,}200.
$$

The **standard deviation**, the square root of variance, is $\sqrt{19{,}200}\approx138.56$ reporting dollars. Under perfect linkage, variance is $0.60(160)^2+0.40(240)^2=38{,}400$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{38{,}400}\approx195.96$. These measure dispersion in the assumed distribution; neither is a worst-case loss or a promise about future outcomes.

### When adding funded costs is useful, and when it is insufficient

For fully funded long shares, summed outlays bound their standalone zero-payout loss. Five positions each costing 12.5% of the same initial bankroll use 62.5%; if an allowed joint state makes all pay zero, 37.5% remains before other changes. That is a scenario calculation, not proof that every correlated group equals one binary bet or exceeds a universal Kelly threshold.

For simultaneous positions sharing one bankroll, use joint terminal wealth in an objective such as $E[\ln W_{\text{terminal}}]$ and enforce funding constraints. Even independence does not justify blindly adding individual single-bet Kelly fractions. Mutually exclusive outcomes, shared resolution sources, overlapping economic drivers and capital reserved for open orders all affect the feasible book.

## Exit capacity, time and collateral are separate constraints

**Liquidity risk** concerns obtaining the required quantity at an acceptable price and time. A positive model edge does not create an exit counterparty. A limit order controls price but may be partially filled or not filled; an aggressive order can consume depth. An unfilled order is not the same as a completed position.

The specified sale above produces 278, so a 300 initial cost leaves $278-300=-22$ before any further exit cost. The following single-average-price illustration assumes the entire quantity can fill at the entered price; it does not simulate a real order book.

A delay in resolution or redemption changes how long capital is committed. Two identical quoted expected profits payable in a week and a year need not be equally attractive. Funding needs, alternative uses of cash, contract operations and the ability to reclaim collateral matter; do not call an uncertain settlement date a guaranteed maturity date.

The current [pUSD documentation](https://docs.polymarket.com/concepts/pusd) describes USDC backing and withdrawal-related contracts, not immunity to every price, contract or access failure. The official [availability documentation](https://docs.polymarket.com/api-reference/geoblock) distinguishes restriction statuses; applicable terms and permissions can change. Read the current venue and contract rules rather than treating a historical country list as permanent. Access restrictions, collateral mechanics and wallet permissions are not solved by calling every concern a probability haircut.

### A decision record that can be checked later

Record the exact contract, payout states, net quantity, actual or assumed average fill, costs, funding boundary, expected settlement timing and joint exposures. State which beliefs already incorporate which risks. Possible decisions include obtaining clarification, changing an order, retaining cash or not opening a position; a smaller stake is only one available response.
