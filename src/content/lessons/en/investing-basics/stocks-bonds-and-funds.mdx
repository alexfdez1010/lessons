The same company can raise money by selling shares or issuing a bond. A share makes its buyer an owner; a bond makes its holder a creditor. A fund can hold either instrument on behalf of many investors. Start with those relationships before comparing names, apps or recent returns.

A **share** is a unit of ownership interest in a company. The company owns its assets and owes its debts; you hold shareholder rights, which depend on the share class and law. You do not directly own a particular oven in its pizza shop.

The share price can rise or fall. Some companies distribute **dividends**, but the amounts and payment dates are not guaranteed. For fully paid ordinary shares bought with your own cash, the share value can fall to zero; leverage and other arrangements introduce additional obligations.

### Worked example

Ten shares at \$50 cost **\$500**. If they later trade at \$58, their value is 10 × \$58 = **\$580**: an \$80 price gain. A \$2-per-share dividend adds 10 × \$2 = **\$20** cash. The combined gain is \$80 + \$20 = **\$100**, or 20% of the \$500 cost, before fees and tax.

### When to use the distinction

Ownership exposes you to the business and to the price paid for that ownership. A successful company can still be a disappointing investment at too high a purchase price. Knowing the business does not remove market, concentration or valuation risk.

## Bonds: a contractual claim

A **bond** is a debt instrument issued by a government or company. Its contract specifies what the issuer promises to pay. A simple fixed-rate bond has:

- **Face value:** the amount promised at maturity. It can differ from the price you paid.
- **Coupon:** contractual interest, often stated as a percentage of face value.
- **Maturity:** the date the final contractual payment is due.

A \$1,000-face-value bond with a 5% annual coupon promises \$1,000 × 0.05 = **\$50 a year**. If it matures in three years and the issuer meets every payment:

| Year | Promised cash flow |
|---|---|
| 1 | \$50 coupon |
| 2 | \$50 coupon |
| 3 | \$50 coupon plus \$1,000 face value |

That is **\$150 of coupons plus \$1,000 repayment**. Repayment of face value is not itself an extra profit. Buying the bond at \$1,050 rather than \$1,000 changes your return even though the coupons stay \$50. Other bonds can have floating coupons, no periodic coupon or additional contract features.

**Credit risk** is the risk that the issuer fails to pay. **Interest-rate risk** is the sensitivity of the resale price to changing rates; a fixed-rate bond generally falls in price when comparable market yields rise, all else equal. Inflation and currency can affect the value of the cash received too.

## Compare the rights, not a universal risk ranking

A shareholder holds residual ownership; a bondholder has a contractual claim. In a failure of the **same company**, creditors generally rank ahead of ordinary shareholders, but they may still recover only part of what is owed. Different creditors can have different priorities.

That ranking does not make every bond safer than every share. A distressed company's bond can be riskier than a diversified equity holding. Compare the issuer, seniority, maturity, currency, market price and the rest of the portfolio.

## Funds: inspect the holdings and mandate

A **fund** pools investors' money and invests according to a **mandate**, the rules or objective that determine what it can hold. Your fund units represent an interest in that pool. A fund may hold shares, bonds or other assets; its risk comes from those holdings, their weights and any leverage or derivatives.

A broad fund can make diversification easier. A narrow fund may concentrate in one sector, country or even a single underlying exposure. Count both the holdings and their shared risks. The [SEC's explanation of mutual funds](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-funds-etfs/mutual-funds) describes pooled investing and why fund risks vary.

An **index** is a calculated measure tracking a specified group of investments, such as shares in one industry. A fund can try to follow that group without covering the whole market.

For dealing in a fund, **[net asset value per share](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/net-asset-value)** means the value of its assets minus liabilities, divided by the number of fund shares. If a fund has \$1,000 of assets, \$100 of liabilities and 100 shares, the per-share value is (\$1,000 − \$100) ÷ 100 = **\$9**. This is distinct from an ETF's market trading price.

| Term | What it identifies |
|---|---|
| Ordinary open-end mutual fund | A fund bought or redeemed at its next calculated net asset value per share, under its dealing rules |
| ETF, or exchange-traded fund | A fund whose units trade on an exchange during trading hours |
| Index fund | A strategy seeking to track a specified index; it can use either structure |

**Index** describes what the fund follows, not a promise that it owns the whole market. An index can be narrow. ETF describes trading structure, not automatic low fees or diversification.

## Fees change the result you keep

The **expense ratio** is an annual operating-cost rate charged to fund assets. At a constant \$10,000 balance, 0.05% is \$10,000 × 0.0005 = **\$5 a year**. At 1%, the corresponding amount is **\$100**. Actual charges are reflected in the fund value over time, so a changing balance changes the amount.

The expense ratio is not necessarily the entire cost. Trading spreads, commissions, taxes and other charges may also matter. Compare funds with comparable exposure and read the fee disclosure rather than assuming every index fund is cheap.

### When to use the distinction

Choose the exposure and risks before comparing costs. A cheap fund with the wrong mandate does not serve your goal; higher fees do not guarantee better management.

## Key takeaways

<Callout variant="success" title="What to remember">
- A share gives ownership rights; dividends are possible, not guaranteed.
- A bond gives a debt claim. Its face value, coupon and market price are different quantities.
- Creditor priority concerns claims on the same issuer, not a universal ranking of every bond and share.
- A fund's mandate, holdings and weights determine its exposure. ETF is a trading structure; indexing is a strategy.
- Compare the expense ratio alongside other costs and the suitability of the underlying exposure.
</Callout>
