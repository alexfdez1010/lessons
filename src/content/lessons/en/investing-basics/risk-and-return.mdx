A fund reports a 10% gain. That number does not tell you how far its value fell during the year, whether it could have lost everything or whether you could sell when needed. **Return** describes the result; **risk** concerns uncertain outcomes and their consequences for your goal.

**Expected return** is the probability-weighted average of the outcomes in a model. For the three outcomes above:

$$(0.50\times20\%)+(0.30\times0\%)+(0.20\times-30\%)=4\%$$

On \$1,000, the possible gains are \$200, \$0 and −\$300. Their weighted average is \$40: \$100 + \$0 − \$60. You do not receive \$40 simply because it is the average. The probabilities themselves are assumptions that can be wrong.

Investors generally require compensation for bearing risk, but **taking more risk does not guarantee a higher expected or realized return**. A badly priced asset can combine high risk with poor expected returns. Diversification and lower costs can sometimes improve a portfolio on both dimensions.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Investigate a guarantee before trusting it">
A high-return pitch that dismisses all risk is a warning sign. Ask who guarantees what, in which currency, for which period, and under what conditions. A registration or a smooth chart does not verify the promise. Avoid treating a particular interest-rate percentage as mathematically impossible in every currency and inflation regime.
</Callout>

## Several kinds of risk

**Volatility** measures how variable returns are. It captures the size of fluctuations, not every way an investment can disappoint. **Permanent capital loss** can occur when an asset becomes worthless or loses value that never recovers. **Liquidity risk** is difficulty selling or accessing money on acceptable terms. **Inflation risk** is a loss of purchasing power.

For a simple comparison, Fund A returns +7%, +9%, +8%, +8%; Fund B returns +35%, −20%, +28%, −11%. Both arithmetic averages are 8%: A sums to 32%, as does B. Yet \$100 compounds to about **\$136.04** in A and **\$123.03** in B. Their identical arithmetic average does not imply identical wealth; multiplying the annual growth factors matters.

### When to use it

Ask which risk measure answers your actual question. A stable quoted price does not prove an investment is liquid or its issuer solvent. A liquid share can fall sharply. Holding for longer does not make an insolvent business recover.

A steadier historical path can be useful when you may need funds along the way. It does not establish that the asset is always safer or will perform better next year. ## Asset classes are a starting point

An **asset class** groups investments with related characteristics. Cash, bonds and shares help organize the comparison, but each group contains different instruments. A long-term government bond can be highly sensitive to interest rates; a corporate bond can default; shares and crypto can lose their entire value.

The ladder is a qualitative illustration of potential gains and losses, not a measured ranking of every asset or a forecast that crypto has the highest expected return. Currency, maturity, issuer, price, diversification and leverage can change the comparison.

## Willingness, capacity and time

**Risk tolerance** is your willingness to accept uncertain losses. **Risk capacity** is your financial ability to bear them without losing essential spending or goals. Wanting a large gain does not increase either one.

For a fixed house deposit in 18 months, a large price decline near the deadline could prevent the purchase. A flexible goal in thirty years may permit more market risk, but still needs a diversified allocation and a plan for changing circumstances. Retirement itself has many spending dates, so its entire balance need not become cash on one day.

### When to use it

State the goal, deadline, flexibility and affordable loss before selecting an asset. A longer horizon increases the time available; it does not guarantee that losses reverse.

## Diversification reduces concentration

**Diversification** spreads exposure across investments that do not all depend on the same outcome. With \$1,000 entirely in one company, that company's total failure can lose the full \$1,000. In an equally weighted basket of 100 companies, its initial weight is 1%; if only it fails and all other prices stay unchanged, the direct loss is **\$10**, or 1%.

The conditions matter: holdings can fall together, weights can become concentrated, and a fund focused on one sector may share the same risk across many names. Diversification reduces some risks without guaranteeing a profit or preserving a particular expected return. [Investor.gov's diversification guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation) explains why a fund is not automatically diversified.

## Key takeaways

<Callout variant="success" title="What to remember">
- Expected return is a modelled average; more risk does not guarantee a better result.
- Volatility, permanent loss, liquidity and inflation describe different risks.
- Matching final returns do not make intermediate paths equivalent.
- Asset classes organize a comparison but do not replace inspecting the instrument.
- Use willingness, loss capacity, deadline and flexibility together; diversification reduces concentration while common risks remain.
</Callout>
