An investment gains \$100. On \$1,000 invested, that is 10%; on \$10,000, it is 1%. Both dollar amounts matter to your finances, but the percentage answers a different question: how much did each invested dollar earn? To compare investments, you also need the period, income and costs.

A **gain** is an increase in value; a negative gain is a loss. Returns can be reported as an amount or as a rate, so always check the unit. Here, **percentage return** means the gain relative to the starting investment.

For one investment with no later deposits or withdrawals, and no income or costs:

$$\text{return \%}=\frac{\text{ending value}-\text{starting value}}{\text{starting value}}\times100$$

### Worked example

Start with \$500 and finish with \$560 after one year. The gain is \$560 − \$500 = **\$60**. The percentage return is \$60 ÷ \$500 × 100 = **12%**. The \$60 amount and 12% rate describe the same result in different units.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Keep the starting amount in the denominator">
Dividing \$60 by the ending \$560 gives about 10.71%, which is the gain as a fraction of the final balance. For this investment return, the base is the \$500 committed at the start. Later deposits or withdrawals require additional treatment; they are not investment gains.
</Callout>

## Total return includes income

**Price return** measures only the price change. **Total return** also includes income received during the holding period. For one unit bought once, before costs and tax:

$$\text{total return \%}=\frac{(\text{ending price}-\text{buy price})+\text{income}}{\text{buy price}}\times100$$

Buy a share for \$100, value it a year later at \$110 and collect \$5 in dividends. The price gain is \$10, the income is \$5 and the combined gain is **\$15**. Dividing by \$100 gives **15%**. Looking only at the price would give 10% and miss the income component.

A rental property has the same two-part logic: price change plus rent. To assess the net result, also include maintenance, vacancy, transaction costs and other relevant expenses.

### When to use it

Check whether a chart shows price return or already includes reinvested distributions. Adding dividends again to a total-return series double-counts them. The simple example keeps the cash income outside the share; reinvested income belongs in the resulting holding value instead.

## Losses use the same calculation

Buy for \$100, finish at \$92 and collect \$3 income. The price change is \$92 − \$100 = **−\$8**. Add the income: −\$8 + \$3 = **−\$5**. Divide by \$100 and the total return is **−5%**. Income cushions this loss but does not erase it.

## Compare the same period and cost basis

Two investments each gain \$100 in a year. If A starts with \$1,000, its return is 10%; if B starts with \$10,000, its return is 1%. A produced ten times the percentage return in that period. It does not follow that A was ten times better: risks, liquidity and costs may differ.

**Gross return** is before specified costs; **net return** is after them. If an investment starts at \$100 and ends with \$112 of price value plus cash income, its gross gain is \$12. If a \$2 holding charge is then deducted from those proceeds, net value is \$110 and net return is **10%**. State which charges and taxes the measure includes.

A later deposit is different. Start with \$100, add \$50 and finish with \$150: the balance rose 50%, but the investment earned **zero**. Separating contributions and withdrawals from performance becomes essential for real portfolios.

## A return needs a time period

A 15% total return in one year differs from 15% over five years. **Annualizing** finds the constant yearly rate that would produce the same overall growth through compounding. For an investment with no intermediate cash flows:

$$\text{annualized rate}=(1+\text{total return})^{1/n}-1$$

Here $n$ is the number of years and the return is written as a decimal. For 15% over five years, $1.15^{1/5}-1 \approx 0.02835$: about **2.83% a year**, not exactly 3%. Check it in the other direction: $1.02835^5 \approx 1.15$.

The annualized figure is a constant-rate equivalent, not a report that every year actually earned 2.83%. More detailed ROI, compound growth and cash-flow-adjusted measures are covered in Investment Metrics.

## Key takeaways

<Callout variant="success" title="What to remember">
- State whether you mean the gain amount or the percentage return.
- For a single initial investment, divide the gain by the starting capital.
- Total return combines price change and income, without counting reinvested income twice.
- Keep losses negative, deduct the costs included in the measure and separate contributions from gains.
- Compare consistent periods; annualization finds a compound equivalent rather than a guaranteed yearly payment.
</Callout>
