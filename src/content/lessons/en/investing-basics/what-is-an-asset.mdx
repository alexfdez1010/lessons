## What counts as an asset

For your personal finances, an **asset** is a resource you own or a right you hold that has economic value. Cash, a house, a share and a bank deposit can all be assets. Being valuable today does not mean an asset will rise in price, earn income or be easy to sell.

A car worth \$10,000 remains an asset if it falls to \$9,000. The fall is **depreciation**, a reduction in value; it does not turn the car into a debt. The [Dallas Fed's guide to assets and net worth](https://www.dallasfed.org/-/media/microsites/cd/wealth/online/ch1.html) distinguishes owning something of value from owning a wealth-building investment.

<Callout variant="info" title="An asset can be a poor investment">
A coat with a resale value is an asset for that value, even if you never plan to sell it. Buying it may still be mainly a consumption expense. Keep three questions separate: what is it worth, what does it cost to hold, and why are you buying it?
</Callout>

### When it matters

Before buying an investment, identify the right you acquire, who owes you anything and how you could recover value. The app used to buy it is only the route to that asset.

## Ownership and rights to payment

A **share**, also called stock, is an ownership interest in a company. A **bond** is a claim on an issuer that promises payments under a contract. You own the bond, but you do not thereby own a slice of the issuer's business.

Imagine a small shop. Buying half its equity gives you a share in the residual value and any distributions to owners. Lending it \$50 for a promised \$55 repayment gives you a debt claim: \$50 principal plus \$5 interest. The shop may fail to pay. The promised amount differs from a guaranteed amount.

A bank deposit is also a claim: the bank owes you the recorded balance under the account terms. It does not keep a labelled stack of notes for each customer. Deposit protection, where it applies, has conditions and limits.

### When it matters

Equity owners and creditors have different contracts and priorities. Creditors often rank ahead of shareholders if the same company fails, but a risky bond can still be riskier than many shares. Read the particular claim rather than treating the category as a safety rating.

## Price changes and income

An investment's result has two main parts:

- **Price change:** buy a share at \$100 and value it at \$108 later; the increase is \$8. A rise is appreciation; a decline is a capital loss. A gain remains unrealized until you sell, but it is still a change in the value you hold.
- **Income:** cash received while holding the asset, such as rent, bond interest or a **dividend**, a distribution a company makes to its shareholders. Payments depend on the asset and its terms; dividends and rent are not guaranteed.

If the \$100 share also pays a \$3 dividend, the combined gain is \$8 + \$3 = **\$11**, before costs and tax. If it falls to \$92 instead, the same dividend leaves −\$8 + \$3 = **−\$5**. Income can offset part of a price loss without eliminating it.

| Asset | Can its market price change? | Possible income |
|---|---|---|
| A share | Yes, up or down | Dividends, if paid |
| A rental flat | Yes, up or down | Rent, less costs |
| A tradable government bond | Yes, up or down | Contractual interest, subject to payment |
| A gold bar | Yes, up or down | None from the bar itself |
| Cash held in its own currency | Face amount is unchanged | None from physical notes |

The two components form **total return**. A dividend is not a free addition unrelated to price: paying out cash reduces the company's assets, and the share price normally adjusts around the ex-dividend date, all else equal.

### When it matters

To assess an investment, combine price changes with income and deduct costs. An income payment alone does not show whether you gained overall. The later lesson on returns turns the combined amount into a percentage for a stated period.

## Assets, liabilities and expenses

An **asset** is on the ownership side of your finances. A **liability** is an amount you owe. An **expense** is a cost incurred over a period. These describe different things.

Return to the car: its current value is \$10,000 and the loan balance is \$6,000. The car contributes \$10,000 − \$6,000 = **\$4,000** to your net worth. Fuel, insurance and interest reduce your cash flow. Repaying loan principal uses cash but also reduces the debt.

$$\text{Net worth} = \text{assets} - \text{liabilities}$$

Net worth is a snapshot; **cash flow** tracks money moving over time. An asset can produce negative cash flow, and a person with valuable assets can run short of spendable cash. The distinction also works for a home and its mortgage: record the property and the debt separately.

### When it matters

For a major purchase, calculate both the effect on net worth and the continuing costs. Calling something an asset answers a classification question; it does not prove affordability or profitability.

## Liquidity

**Liquidity** is how readily you can sell or access an asset without a large price concession or cost. It is a matter of degree, and it can change when markets are stressed.

Suppose a repair costs \$5,000 this week. A readily accessible deposit can meet that bill. A \$50,000 interest in a house cannot normally be spent directly: selling takes time, and borrowing against it needs approval and creates a debt. Equal net worth does not imply equal ability to pay today.

Actively traded shares may be easy to sell during market hours, but the price can fall before sale and the proceeds may not be withdrawable until settlement. Liquidity is separate from price stability. A house is usually slower to sell and has substantial transaction costs.

### When it matters

Match access to the payment date and the loss you can afford. Money needed for an immediate bill requires both accessibility and a suitable degree of value stability. Illiquidity alone does not guarantee a higher return.

## Key takeaways

<Callout variant="success" title="What to remember">
- An asset has economic value; it can lose value or cost money to maintain.
- Ownership and payment claims give different rights. Neither category guarantees safety.
- Combine price changes and income when measuring an investment result.
- Record an asset and its associated debt separately; net worth is assets minus liabilities.
- Liquidity concerns access and sale, while price stability concerns changes in value.
</Callout>
