A trading agent buys two shares, its screen reports a gain, and the episode ends. Did it make money? We still need its purchase price, cash balance, fees, closing position and a credible sale price. A green reward chart cannot supply the missing accounting.

In the previous lesson, a **policy** chose an action from a state. Here we choose what those actions are scored on. Separate three questions: what economic outcome occurred, what outcome we prefer under uncertainty, and what extra learning signal preserves that preference. They require different calculations.

All prices and policies below are invented. Cash earns zero; there are no external deposits, withdrawals, dividends, borrowing charges or taxes unless stated. Actions use information available at that point. A useful training signal does not establish a profitable or safe live strategy.

## 1. Reconcile cash and inventory before scoring trades

<MCQ client:visible pretest

 explanation={"A purchase exchanges cash for an asset. Fill-versus-mark differences and fees can change wealth, but the purchase notional is not automatically a loss."}

Think of a warehouse: buying stock moves value from the bank account to the shelf. Paying for the stock is not, by itself, a loss of the whole purchase amount. For shares, let $C_t$ be cash, $q_t$ the number of shares and $m_t$ a reference **mark**, the price used to value each held share. Marked wealth is

$$
W_t=C_t+q_tm_t.
$$

For a signed trade $u_t$ (positive for a buy, negative for a sale), actual fill price $p_t$ and explicit fee $f_t\geq0$, the immediate cash/inventory update is

$$
q_t^+=q_t+u_t,\qquad C_t^+=C_t-u_tp_t-f_t.
$$

A buy reduces cash and increases inventory. A sale does the reverse. A spread or price concession already embedded in $p_t$ has already affected cash; subtracting it again would charge it twice.

Start with USD 1,000 and no shares. Buy two at USD 101, pay USD 1, and initially mark them at USD 100. Later the mark becomes USD 104. Finally sell both at USD 103 and pay another USD 1.

The trade-level check agrees: $2(103-101)-1-1=2$ dollars. If the base reward is the marked wealth change, $r_t=W_{t+1}-W_t$, its **undiscounted** sum is $-3+8-3=2=W_T-W_0$. That cancellation is a telescoping sum, like adjoining lengths whose internal endpoints disappear.

Marked P&L gives feedback while a position remains open. **Realised P&L** uses a convention for profits recognised on closing transactions; sparse realised rewards can postpone the loss on a position that the episode never closes. Neither accounting label proves that the mark or simulator fill is credible. With external flows, remove the contribution of those flows when measuring investment P&L; with financing and distributions, put their cash flows into the ledger.

### When to use this ledger

Use it to test one action and an entire episode by hand. The state needs cash and inventory if they affect feasibility; time, outstanding orders and other relevant variables may also be necessary. A reward optimiser cannot repair missing economic transactions.

## 2. Charge friction once and settle the final position

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"An episode ends with shares still open. Which number answers the liquidation question?"}
 options={[{"text": "Cash plus proceeds from a specified feasible exit, less costs not already charged", "correct": true}, {"text": "Only the last favourable reference mark, regardless of sale depth"}, {"text": "Only realised profits, treating the open position as nonexistent"}, {"text": "The trade bonus, because reward units are legal tender"}]}
 explanation={"Terminal ownership must be represented. Liquidation needs an executable fill/cost assumption; a mark alone is not the same quantity."}
/>

A restaurant bill has a total and its ingredients. A trading ledger likewise needs a net total and a decomposition that reconciles to it. In the same example, measuring the price move from entry mark 100 to exit mark 104 gives gross marked opportunity $2(104-100)=8$. Entry is USD 1 above its reference mark, and the sale is USD 1 below its reference mark: adverse fills cost $2+2=4$. Explicit fees cost $1+1=2$. Thus $8-4-2=2$ dollars net.

The four dollars are already in the actual fills. You may use the decomposition to explain the result; you must not subtract them again from the cash-based net P&L. A separate turnover penalty, such as $\kappa|u_t|$, is either an explicitly modelled cost **not already charged** or an additional preference. It is not automatically a second real commission.

Now end the episode **before** selling. Cash 797 plus two shares marked at 104 is USD 1,005, a marked gain of 5. If the terminal executable price is 103 and the exit fee is 1, terminal cash is only $797+2(103)-1=1,002$. A different available exit price changes that result: at 99, cash is 994 and the liquidated P&L is −6.

<RewardAccountingLab client:visible mode="terminal" title="Marked wealth and executable liquidation" caption="Invented same two-share account. Mark stays at 104; the chosen executable sale price and fee determine final cash. The two bars compare marked P&L and liquidated P&L relative to starting USD 1,000; the table also reports wealth levels. The decomposition uses reference marks, not an extra charge to subtract from net P&L." />

Three legitimate specifications answer different questions:

- **Mandatory liquidation:** execute the residual inventory under a stated terminal fill and fee model, and include the resulting cash change.
- **Continuing ownership:** retain the asset with a defined terminal value or continuation objective. An arbitrary episode boundary does not make the position disappear.
- **Hard completion requirement:** restrict feasible actions so the mandate finishes, or explicitly solve a constrained problem. Completion still needs feasible liquidity and an execution model.

A finite penalty is a trade-off, not a logical constraint. Suppose finishing costs USD 12 while leaving a share costs a terminal penalty of USD 5. A policy maximising negative cost prefers the penalty, since $-5>-12$. Raising the penalty may change this toy ranking; an arbitrary finite number does not guarantee completion across every state and possible cost.

### When to use terminal accounting

Use it whenever training truncates a position or order. Compare reward, marked wealth and liquidation wealth separately. A real fill model needs spreads, depth, partial fills, adverse selection and applicable fees; declaring costs “realistic” does not validate it.

## 3. A risk penalty chooses a preference

<MCQ client:visible pretest

 options={[{"text": "They tie at USD 10; a preference or constraint is needed to rank this risk difference", "correct": true}, {"text": "B must win because variance itself raises the mean"}, {"text": "A must win because every optimiser secretly penalises variance"}, {"text": "Neither is feasible because all P&L must be positive"}]}

An accountant can tell us both strategies have the same average profit. Choosing between their distributions takes a preference. With one USD outcome per period, suppose A pays 9 or 11 with equal probability; B pays −10 or 30 with equal probability.

</table>

This is the specified **population** distribution, so divide by two, not the sample variance denominator $n-1$. The criterion $J=\mathbb E[P]-\lambda\operatorname{Var}(P)$ has dollars as its output only if $\lambda$ has units per dollar. At $\lambda=0$, both tie; at positive $\lambda$, A wins here. No fee-free objective mathematically requires constant trading: with no predictive edge, flat exposure can also be optimal. Positive expected edge and available leverage can favour more exposure within the feasible set; the assumptions matter.

<RewardAccountingLab client:visible mode="risk" title="Equal mean, different variance preference" caption="Invented one-period population: A pays 9 or 11 USD and B pays −10 or 30 USD, each with probability one half. Variances use population weights. Lambda is per USD, making mean minus lambda times USD-squared variance a USD criterion. Bars are preference scores, not cash earned or risk forecasts." />

Several often-labelled “risk rewards” are different objectives:

- Squared P&L is not variance: $\mathbb E[P^2]=\operatorname{Var}(P)+(\mathbb E[P])^2$. For A, it is $101$, not $1$ USD². Summing squared step P&L is not generally the variance of the whole episode; cross-period covariances and the policy also matter.
- An inventory term $\eta q_t^2\Delta t$ penalises exposure-time. If $q=2$ shares, $\Delta t=1$ minute and $\eta=0.5$ USD/(share²·minute), its penalty is USD 2. It equals a particular variance contribution only under a derived price model and matching units.
- A **drawdown** uses a running peak. Wealth 100→110→105 has drawdown $(110-105)/110=4.54545\%$. A state with wealth 105 and peak 110 differs from wealth 105 and peak 120. Include the needed peak in a Markov state rather than pretending current wealth alone determines this reward.

### Differential Sharpe is a local derivative

The Sharpe ratio uses return in excess of a specified risk-free cash return divided by the standard deviation of that excess return, with matching periods. Here cash earns zero. A rolling version uses $A$ for an exponentially weighted mean and $B$ for the second moment. Given current excess return $R$ and adaptation rate $\eta$,

$$
A'=A+\eta(R-A),\quad B'=B+\eta(R^2-B),\quad S=A/\sqrt{B-A^2}.
$$

The [differential Sharpe derivation by Moody and Saffell](https://people.idsia.ch/~juergen/rnnaissance2003talks/MoodySaffellTNN01.pdf) uses a first-order expansion. Its derivative at $\eta=0$ is:

$$
D=\frac{B(R-A)-\tfrac12A(R^2-B)}{(B-A^2)^{3/2}},\qquad S'-S\approx\eta D.
$$

Take $A=0.01$, $B=0.0004$, $R=0.02$, $\eta=0.01$. Then $B-A^2=0.0003$, $R-A=0.01$, $R^2-B=0$, and $D\approx0.769800$. The predicted first-order change is about 0.007698. Recomputing the updated moments gives the actual change shown below, rather than an equality asserted by the approximation.

<RewardAccountingLab client:visible mode="sharpe" title="Local derivative versus actual moment update" caption="Illustrative rolling excess-return moments. Current return is adjustable; A=0.01, B=0.0004 and eta=0.01 stay fixed. Compare the first-order eta times D with the actual updated Sharpe difference. These dimensionless quantities are close, not identical. No annualisation or claim about future Sharpe is made." />

The moments must belong in the state when reward depends on them. A zero or tiny $B-A^2$ makes the expression undefined or unstable; a chosen initialisation, variance floor or other regularisation changes the implemented objective. Maximising a sum of these local derivatives is **not generally identical** to maximising finite-episode or out-of-sample Sharpe. Even an exact episode statistic would still be a chosen criterion, not a guarantee of future performance.

### When to use risk terms

Specify the economic objective, units, time aggregation, state and constraints before selecting a proxy. Evaluate both net P&L and the intended risk measure. Hard leverage, inventory or loss limits can also be constraints; everything does not have to be squeezed into one unconstrained scalar.

## 4. Discounting changes the objective

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"For a finite episode scored on terminal cash gain, is gamma below one automatically required?"}
 options={[{"text": "No; gamma=1 can sum wealth changes to the terminal gain when the ledger is complete", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes; every finite sum diverges with gamma=1"}, {"text": "Yes; gamma is necessarily the market interest rate"}, {"text": "No; gamma never affects policy ranking"}]}
 explanation={"A finite well-defined sum needs no discount for convergence. Choosing discounting changes the objective unless a separate derivation says otherwise."}
/>

A reinforcement-learning **return** is the reward score $G=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t$, not automatically a financial percentage return. In a finite episode, $\gamma=1$ is valid when expectations are defined. Our ledger then sums to USD 2. With $\gamma=0.9$ it becomes

$$
G=-3+0.9(8)+0.9^2(-3)=1.77.
$$

That is a discounted score; terminal cash still rose by USD 2. Ignoring that distinction makes it tempting to celebrate a reward improvement that merely moved recognition of gains and losses between dates.

In a separate choice, X pays a reward of 5 now; Y pays 10 exactly three reward steps later. The values are $5$ and $10\gamma^3$. At $\gamma=0.5$, Y is 1.25; at 0.9 it is 7.29. The ranking changes. Neither gamma is “correct” without the intended objective and timing convention.

<RewardAccountingLab client:visible mode="discount" title="A reward score with a chosen discount factor" caption="Finite invented rewards. X pays 5 now; Y pays 10 three reward steps later. Bars are 5 and 10 times gamma cubed. The table separately shows the −3,+8,−3 ledger score under the selected gamma; economic terminal P&L remains USD 2. Gamma=1 is allowed for this finite example." />

If a modeller deliberately wants deterministic financial discounting at rate $i$ per matching period, $\gamma=1/(1+i)$ connects the two conventions. In RL it can instead encode a task preference or be part of an infinite-horizon formulation. Changing decision-step duration without reconsidering discounting also changes what a distant event weighs: a consistent continuous time preference can use $\gamma(\Delta t)=e^{-\rho\Delta t}$.

### When to choose gamma

Choose it alongside the horizon, terminal treatment and units. A smaller gamma reduces the weight of a later exit cost, but does not prove the trained policy overtrades. A larger gamma changes both the optimisation problem and, often, estimation difficulty; it does not universally make a policy wiser or learning slower.

## 5. Potential shaping needs its boundary condition

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"Does writing F=gamma×next potential−current potential guarantee invariance when you stop at any terminal state?"}
 options={[{"text": "Only with the appropriate policy-independent boundary term and consistent return definition", "correct": true}, {"text": "Yes, regardless of terminal potentials and stopping time"}, {"text": "Only when the potential is a forecast of guaranteed cash profit"}, {"text": "Yes, and it also guarantees faster learning and no simulator errors"}]}
 explanation={"The finite sum is−Phi0+gamma^T PhiT. The terminal convention and same discount factor matter."}
/>

A progress indicator can guide learning without becoming extra profit. Let $\Phi_t(s_t)$ be a finite-valued potential in reward units. In a finite task, include time in the state and add

$$
F_t=\gamma\Phi_{t+1}(s_{t+1})-\Phi_t(s_t).
$$

Use the **same gamma** in the shaping term and the return. Write out the weighted sum; the internal terms cancel:

$$
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^tF_t=-\Phi_0(s_0)+\gamma^T\Phi_T(s_T).
$$

Therefore the shaped return equals the base return plus that boundary term. For the same initial state, policy rankings are preserved if the remaining boundary is policy-independent. Setting **every terminal potential to zero** is a convenient convention even when stopping time differs. A common nonzero terminal value is constant at a fixed horizon, but can cease to be constant if $T$ depends on the policy and $\gamma<1$.

Here both paths have two transitions, $\gamma=0.9$ and initial potential 3:

For A, $F_0=0.9(4)-3=0.6$ and $F_1=0-4=-4$; weighted sum $0.6+0.9(-4)=-3$. For B it is $(-2.1)+0.9(-1)=-3$. The intermediate scores differ, but A still wins.

<RewardAccountingLab client:visible mode="shaping" title="Keep the terminal term visible" caption="Two invented two-transition paths, same gamma and initial potential 3. A has intermediate potential 4 and terminal 0; B has intermediate 1 and the chosen terminal potential. Base rewards are 0,10 and 0,9. Compare discounted base, shaping and shaped scores; raising B terminal potential shows a policy-dependent boundary term, not safe progress." />

Now incorrectly assign B terminal potential 5 while keeping A at zero and stopping the episode. B gains $0.9^2(5)=4.05$ more than before: its shaped score becomes 9.15, overtaking A's 6 although its base score was lower. The formula alone did not save this terminal convention.

The [policy-invariance result of Ng, Harada and Russell](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~russell/papers/icml99-shaping.pdf) gives the general potential-based construction. For a continuing discounted task, bounded potential and $0\leq\gamma<1$ make the limiting boundary vanish under the usual well-defined-return conditions. Undiscounted continuing tasks need other assumptions; this finite proof is not a blanket theorem for them. Non-potential bonuses can preserve a particular problem's ranking by coincidence, but lack this general guarantee.

### When to use shaping

Use it to supply intermediate information while testing the cancellation on completed and truncated episodes. Preserving the mathematical optimum does not guarantee faster learning with a particular approximator, convergence, honest fills or immunity to other reward hacks.

## 6. Audit economic results alongside the written reward

<MCQ client:visible pretest
 question={"Buy at midpoint 100 and sell at midpoint 100, with no price change and no fees. What is gross P&L?"}
 options={[{"text": "Zero; any positive reward needs another specified cash flow or reward term", "correct": true}, {"text": "Positive because there were two fills"}, {"text": "Negative100 because the purchase notional is a loss"}, {"text": "A guaranteed spread capture without specifying bid/ask fills"}]}
 explanation={"100−100=0. An unrealistic fill model can still overstate achievable performance, but unchanged midpoint round trips do not generate positive arithmetic profit."}
/>

A bonus ledger can become very profitable while the cash account quietly empties. Consider a one-share round trip at an unchanged midpoint of 100. If the simulator fills both legs at 100, gross profit is **zero**, not positive. With USD 1 fee on each leg, economic P&L is −2. Add an invented reward bonus of USD 2 per trade: two trades earn bonus 4, so the written reward is $-2+4=+2$. Doing nothing earns zero. The shaped objective now prefers this useless churn.

<RewardAccountingLab client:visible mode="audit" title="A trade bonus can reward a losing round trip" caption="Invented flat-price one-share round trip: both fills at 100 and fee USD 1 per leg. Economic net is −2; add the chosen reward bonus per trade twice. A flat unchanged-mid round trip has zero gross profit, not a free gain. Bars compare economic P&L with the written reward; the bonus is not cash income." />

A genuine maker rebate belongs in cash at its actual fee basis and date. A USD 0.20 rebate on a fill followed by USD 0.70 adverse marked price movement gives −0.50 before other costs; the rebate alone is not profit. Limit orders are not guaranteed fills, and extra turnover can increase exposure to adverse selection rather than collect a free spread.

Another loophole is a manipulable mark: a tiny favourable print may value a much larger holding above what can be sold. Test the mark against depth, conservative liquidation and independent prices. A valid cash identity proves consistency of the assumed transactions; it does not prove those transactions were achievable.

### When to run the audit

For fixed policies, compare the written return with separately reconstructed cash, inventory, financing, fees and terminal liquidation. Check an unchanged-price round trip, no trade, a losing position left open and a forced exit. Preserve as-of information and action feasibility. Then test alternative fill/cost regimes out of sample; a reward term or stress test cannot certify future alignment.
