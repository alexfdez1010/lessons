Covariance can change from **8 to 12 percentage points squared** without changing correlation at all. The returns stayed the same; the calculation convention changed. Dividing the new covariance by old or mismatched standard deviations would manufacture a different answer.

This lesson keeps the paired data, units and portfolio weights together. All examples are invented matched one-year simple returns or one-year model outcomes, measured in the same currency, before costs or external flows during the period. **Volatility** means standard deviation of return here; it is a risk summary rather than a description of every possible loss.

## Covariance: center the paired returns and state the denominator

A mean is an average return. A **deviation** subtracts that return’s own mean. **Covariance** averages the product of the two deviations. In a population model with means $\mu_A$ and $\mu_B$,

$$
\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Cov}(R_A,R_B)&={}\\&\mathbb E[(R_A-\mu_A)(R_B-\mu_B)].\end{aligned}
$$

The expectation E is a probability-weighted average. When both deviations are positive, their product is positive; when both are negative, their product is also positive. Opposite-sign deviations contribute a negative product. The resulting sign describes the average cross-product, not a guarantee that both raw returns rise or fall together in every observation.

### Retain the same three pairs throughout the calculation

Use returns A = (10 %,−2 %,4 %) and B = (4 %,0 %,2 %). Their displayed means are(10 − 2 + 4)/3 = **4 %** and(4 + 0 + 2)/3 = **2 %**. The next table measures deviations in **percentage points (pp)**: a move from 4 % to 10 % is a 6 pp deviation.

The centered product sum is $S_{AB}$ = 12 + 12 + 0 = **24 pp²**. The squared-deviation sums are $S_{AA}$ = 6² + (−6)² = **72 pp²** and $S_{BB}$ = 2² + (−2)² = **8 pp²**.

If the three rows form an entire **equally likely population model**, divide by n = 3. If they are a sample from an unknown law, the conventional **sample covariance and variances** divide their centered sums by n −1 = 2. [NIST’s covariance definition uses this sample denominator](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/software/dataplot/refman2/ch2/covarian.pdf). Choosing it does not itself justify treating historical data as a forecast.

### Units carry information rather than spoil the statistic

Covariance has the product of the two measurement units. The population covariance 8 pp² becomes **8 × 0.01² = 0.0008** in decimal-return units squared. The sample covariance 12 pp² becomes 0.0012. Standard deviations scale by 0.01, whereas the covariance scales by 0.01² when both return series change units.

Covariance is meaningful in a risk calculation. Compare it using compatible units, horizons and estimation conventions; its size deliberately includes the assets’ volatility scales. Standardizing answers a different question about linear association.

### When to use it

Use covariance when aggregating return variance, and retain the same paired observations in all component moments. Center each series on its own mean. B’s 0 % observation is below its 2 % mean even though its raw return is not negative. Also, a few large products can outweigh many smaller opposite-sign products; covariance is not a count of matching signs.

## Correlation: standardize only when both deviations have a scale

**Pearson correlation** divides covariance by the product of the two standard deviations. It is defined for finite, strictly positive variances:

$$
\rho_{AB}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_A,R_B)}{\sigma_A\sigma_B}.
$$

For sample moments, write r = $s_{AB}$/($s_A$ $s_B$). With the same denominator used throughout, the common factor cancels:

$$
r=\frac{S_{AB}}{\sqrt{S_{AA}}\sqrt{S_{BB}}}.
$$

This is [NIST’s centered-sum formula for the linear correlation coefficient](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/software/dataplot/refman2/auxillar/correlat.htm). For our pairs, r = 24/[√72 × √8] = 24/24 = **1**. Equivalently, population 8/[√24 × √(8/3)] = 1 and sample 12/(6 × 2) = 1. Returning to decimal units changes neither result.

### Perfect correlation refers to deviations, not equal return levels

The original pairs satisfy A = 3 B −2 in percentage-point units:10 = 3 × 4 − 2,−2 = 3 × 0 − 2 and 4 = 3 × 2 − 2. Their deviations are exactly proportional with a positive slope. Their means and standard deviations still differ.

“Zero linear correlation” does not mean that knowing one variable tells you nothing about the other. A concrete counterexample comes later. Population correlation also requires finite moments; an infinite or undefined variance cannot be repaired by reporting a convenient zero.

### A constant series produces an undefined coefficient

Replace every A return with 4 %, leaving B = (4 %,0 %,2 %). A’s deviations, variance and covariance with B are all zero. Under the sample convention, B still has standard deviation 2 pp. The attempted ratio is 0/(0 × 2), which is **undefined**, not zero. A series can be constant in a displayed sample without establishing a constant future payoff.

### When to use it

Use correlation to summarize linear association after checking its domain and the underlying scatter. A sample coefficient±1 describes that sample; a population coefficient±1 means exact affine dependence under that model, apart from probability-zero events. Neither a finite sample nor a selected model proves causation or a future executable hedge.

## Portfolio variance: keep weights, covariance and the comparison fixed

A weight is the fraction of initial portfolio value in an asset. For one period, hold long-only weights w in A and 1−w in B, with 0≤w≤1. Simple portfolio return is $R_p$ = w$R_A$ + (1−w)$R_B$. Expanding its variance gives

$$
\begin{aligned}\sigma_p^2&=w^2\sigma_A^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_B^2\\&\quad+2w(1-w)c,\\c&=\operatorname{Cov}(R_A,R_B).\end{aligned}
$$

If both standard deviations are positive, substitute c = ρ$\sigma_A \sigma_B$. The covariance form remains valid when a series is constant; there is no need to invent a correlation for it. For a zero-variance A and a B with 20 % standard deviation, a 50/50 mix has variance 0.25 × 0 + 0.25 × 0.20² = 0.01 and **10 % volatility**, regardless of the inactive correlation input.

[CFA Institute treats covariance and portfolio moments together](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/portfolio-mathematics). The squared terms and cross term are parts of this expansion; the variance is not a simple weighted average of asset variances or volatilities.

### Work the 30 %/20 % model separately from the earlier data

Now assume different invented assets with $\sigma_A$ = 30 % and $\sigma_B$ = 20 % per year. These are model inputs, not estimates from the three pairs above. At w = 0.5, using percentage-point standard deviations 30 and 20 gives

$$
\begin{aligned}V&=0.25(30^2)+0.25(20^2)\\&\quad+2(0.5)(0.5)\rho(30)(20)\\&=325+300\rho\quad\text{pp}^2.\end{aligned}
$$

### When to use it

For fixed nonnegative weights and standard deviations, loweringρ weakly lowers variance; the effect is strict when both weights and both standard deviations are positive. At a zero weight or zero standard deviation, the cross term vanishes. The comparison is with the **same weights atρ =  + 1**, not necessarily with the safer asset alone.

Expected return is still w$\mu_A$ + (1−w)$\mu_B$. A hypothetical change inρ can leave those means fixed by assumption; selecting a different real asset need not. A volatility gap alone does not establish unchanged expected return, higher performance or an available free trade.

## Match weighted risk exposures and locate the permitted minimum

<MCQ

Withρ = −1, the variance formula becomes a square:

$$
\sigma_p^2=[w\sigma_A-(1-w)\sigma_B]^2.
$$

Zero modeled variance requires w$\sigma_A$ = (1−w)$\sigma_B$. Therefore

$$
w=\frac{\sigma_B}{\sigma_A+\sigma_B}=\frac{20}{30+20}=0.4.
$$

Put 40 % in A and 60 % in B:0.4 × 30 = 0.6 × 20 = 12 pp of weighted exposure. They cancel under this exact correlation model. Equal 50/50 capital weights instead leave 15 − 10 = **5 pp volatility**. Withρ =  + 1, the same 40/60 mix has volatility 0.4 × 30 + 0.6 × 20 = **24 %**, not zero.

### The minimum depends on correlation and constraints

Write D = $\sigma_A$² + $\sigma_B$²−2 c. When D>0, the portfolio variance is a quadratic whose unconstrained minimum occurs at

$$
\begin{aligned}w^*&=\frac{\sigma_B^2-c}{D},\\V(w)&=V(w^*)+D(w-w^*)^2.\end{aligned}
$$

The square cannot be negative. If weights must remain between zero and one, use the closest permitted weight to w*: an endpoint when w* lies outside. If D = 0, the variance is constant over weights. For example, equal standard deviations withρ =  + 1 give the same variance for every long-only mix; dividing by D would not identify a unique minimum.

At ρ = 0 in the 30 %/20 % model, c = 0 and D = 900 + 400 = 1300 pp². Thus w* = 400/1300 = **4/13≈30.769231 %**. Its variance is(900 × 400)/1300≈276.923077 pp² and volatility **16.641006 %**. Meanwhile an 80/20 mix has variance 0.8² × 900 + 0.2² × 400 = 592 pp² and volatility **24.331050 %**, which exceeds B’s 20 %.

## Zero correlation can coexist with exact dependence and joint losses

Independence means that joint event probabilities factor into their marginal probabilities. With finite moments, independence implies covariance zero; the converse fails, as [MIT’s probability notes emphasize](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/mit18_05_s22_probability.pdf). With positive standard deviations, zero covariance gives zero correlation, but still does not prove independence.

Each variance is 0.1 × 900 + 0.1 × 900 + 0.4 × 225 + 0.4 × 225 = **360 pp²**, or 0.036 in decimal-return units squared. Each standard deviation is√360≈**18.973666 %**, so correlation is 0/360 = **0**. Yet knowing A = −30 % tells you B = −30 % with certainty **within this finite model**. The dependence is nonlinear: at magnitudes 15 % the signs oppose; at 30 % they match.

### Compare the same marginals under independence

Each asset individually has a 10 % probability of a return at or below −20 %. In the four-state law, both cross that threshold with probability **10 %**. If those same marginal distributions are independent, the joint probability is 0.10 × 0.10 = **1 %**. The conditional probability of B crossing given A crosses is 10 %/10 % = 100 % in the first law, versus 1 %/10 % = 10 % under independence.

### When to use it

Use a joint distribution or explicit scenarios when a decision depends on simultaneous extreme outcomes. The control mixes these two laws while retaining their marginal moments and covariance. A changing correlation over time is a separate question. Add one invented fourth observation to the original three pairs: A = 10 %, B = 0 %. The new means are 5.5 % and 1.5 %. Centered A deviations are 4.5,−7.5,−1.5,4.5 pp; centered B deviations are 2.5,−1.5,0.5,−1.5 pp. Their sums give

$$
\begin{aligned}S_{AA}&=99\quad\text{pp}^2,\\S_{BB}&=11\quad\text{pp}^2,\\S_{AB}&=15\quad\text{pp}^2,\end{aligned}
$$

and hence r = 15/[√99 × √11] = 15/33 = **5/11≈0.454545**. Under the sample convention, covariance is 15/3 = 5 pp² and the standard deviations are√33 and√(11/3), whose product is 11.

This is a sensitivity exercise. It does not assign a probability to the fourth observation or calibrate a confidence interval. If you interpret the displayed rows as an equally likely population instead, adding a row changes that chosen model. If they are observations from an unknown process, adding information changes the descriptive estimate; it does not identify causation.

### When to use it

Align return periods, currency bases, dates and missing-data treatment before estimating moments. State the sample window and convention, examine the scatter and stress alternative joint outcomes. Correlations can change rather than universally rising toward one in every crisis. The variance identity still applies to the relevant moments; a stale or incomplete estimate is a different problem from an incorrect formula.
