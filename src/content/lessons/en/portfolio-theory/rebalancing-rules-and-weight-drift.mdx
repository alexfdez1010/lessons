You buy a 60/40 stock–bond portfolio, leave it alone and return to find it is almost 67/33. Nobody has secretly traded in your account. The prices moved, so the proportions moved too.

The earlier [portfolio risk and return lesson](/portfolio-theory/portfolio-risk-and-return) used weights as inputs. Here we maintain those weights after markets and cash flows change them. You will calculate the money that must move, choose a rule for when to act and check whether the clean result survives implementation costs. The 60/40 target and all returns below are invented teaching inputs, not an allocation recommendation or a forecast.

## Weight drift changes the mix without a trade

A **portfolio weight** is an asset's current market value divided by the current market value of the portfolio. **Weight drift** is the difference between that actual weight and the chosen target. Think of a recipe measured by the size of two expanding ingredients: leaving the bowls alone does not keep their proportions constant.

For a stock sleeve, the group of stock holdings treated as one position, write:

$$w_S'=\frac{S_0(1+R_S)}{S_0(1+R_S)+B_0(1+R_B)}$$

Here $S_0$ and $B_0$ are starting stock and bond values; $R_S$ and $R_B$ are their returns over the **same period**. Returns include distributions reinvested into their own sleeve. There are no contributions, withdrawals or costs in this first step, and all values use euros at a common valuation time.

### Work through the drift

| Holding | Starting value | Period return | Change | Ending value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Stocks | €600 | +20% | €600 × 0.20 = +€120 | €720 |
| Bonds | €400 | −10% | €400 × (−0.10) = −€40 | €360 |
| Total | €1,000 | +8% | +€120 − €40 = +€80 | €1,080 |

The portfolio's return is €80 ÷ €1,000 = **8%**, also 0.60 × 20% + 0.40 × (−10%) = 8%. But the **ending** stock weight uses the new denominator:

$$w_S'=\frac{720}{720+360}=\frac{2}{3}\approx66.6667\%$$

Bond weight is €360 ÷ €1,080 ≈ **33.3333%**. Stock drift is 66.6667% − 60% ≈ **+6.6667 percentage points**. Its relative increase is 6.6667 ÷ 60 ≈ **11.1111%**. Percentage points and a percentage change are different measurements.

### When to use it

Measure drift at the portfolio boundary you actually manage: the relevant accounts and assets, valued consistently, including any cash you intend to count. Do not divide by the original contribution. A changed weight can alter exposure without a deliberate trade, as [Investor.gov explains in its rebalancing overview](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation).

The target describes a chosen mix. It does not freeze volatility, correlations, liquidity or future losses. Even an unchanged 60/40 portfolio can become riskier when the underlying risks change.

**Rebalancing** adjusts holdings toward a chosen allocation. A cost-free, **self-financing** rebalance funds purchases entirely from sales within the portfolio. It is like moving water between two containers: the split changes while the combined amount stays fixed.

For each asset $i$, with current value $V_i$, portfolio total $T$ and target weight $w_i^*$:

$$V_i^*=w_i^*T,\qquad\Delta_i=V_i^*-V_i$$

A positive $\Delta_i$ means a purchase; a negative one means a sale. If targets sum to one and there are no costs or outside flows, $\sum_i\Delta_i=0$.

### Restore the same €1,080 portfolio

| Holding | Current | Target calculation | Target value | Signed trade |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|
| Stocks | €720 | 0.60 × €1,080 | €648 | €648 − €720 = −€72 |
| Bonds | €360 | 0.40 × €1,080 | €432 | €432 − €360 = +€72 |
| Total | €1,080 | 1.00 × €1,080 | €1,080 | €0 |

Check both sides: €648 ÷ €1,080 = 60%; €432 ÷ €1,080 = 40%. The sale is not “sell the gain”: the total has also changed, so the target stock value has risen from €600 to €648.

### Two turnover conventions

**Traded notional** adds the monetary size of the transactions. Here both legs total €72 sold + €72 bought = **€144**, or €144 ÷ €1,080 ≈ **13.3333%** of pre-trade value.

A **one-way half-turnover** measure counts half that absolute trading amount:

$$\tau=\frac{\sum_i|\Delta_i|}{2T}=\frac{144}{2(1080)}\approx6.6667\%$$

For this balanced two-leg trade, it equals purchases divided by the portfolio value and also sales divided by that value. Reports use different conventions and periods. Name the numerator, denominator and period before comparing numbers. A 6.6667% figure and a 13.3333% figure can describe exactly the same transactions.

### When to use it

Use signed trades to prepare an execution budget and verify that funding balances. Exact restoration here assumes divisible positions, immediate execution at the valuation prices and no charges. Whole units, price moves and minimum trade sizes can leave residual cash and small weight gaps.

## Cash flows offer another route

A contribution changes the size of the whole pie, not just how you slice it. Buying an underweight sleeve with outside cash can reduce the overweight sleeve's percentage without reducing its euro value. **Underweight** means below target; **overweight** means above target.

With stocks overweight at €720, solve for an addition $C$ entirely to bonds:

$$\frac{720}{1080+C}=0.60\quad\Rightarrow\quad C=\frac{720}{0.60}-1080=120$$

Bonds become €360 + €120 = **€480**; total becomes **€1,200**. The weights are €720 ÷ €1,200 = 60% and €480 ÷ €1,200 = 40%. No existing investment is sold in this model. [FINRA describes contributions and sales as alternative rebalancing methods](https://www.finra.org/investors/investing/investing-basics/asset-allocation-diversification).

### A planned withdrawal can help too

If you already need to withdraw money, taking it from the overweight stocks can move the mix back toward target. To reach 60/40 exactly with a stock-only withdrawal $W$:

$$\frac{720-W}{1080-W}=0.60$$

So €720 − $W$ = €648 − 0.60$W$; €72 = 0.40$W$; **$W$ = €180**. The remaining portfolio is stocks €540, bonds €360, total €900. Check: €540 ÷ €900 = 60%.

| Route from €720/€360 | Stock change | Bond change | Outside flow | Ending invested total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sell stocks and buy bonds | −€72 | +€72 | €0 | €1,080 |
| Add cash entirely to bonds | €0 | +€120 | +€120 | €1,200 |
| Withdraw entirely from stocks | −€180 | €0 | −€180 | €900 |

The same weights do **not** mean the same wealth outcome. The contribution uses €120 of other money. The withdrawal leaves €180 outside the portfolio, spent or held elsewhere. Neither outside flow is investment return. Comparing ending portfolio values without including those flows would mislead.

### When to use it

Consider scheduled savings, distributions held as cash and spending needs before placing extra trades. Cash flows may be too small to restore the target fully. For example, adding only €60 to bonds gives €720 ÷ €1,140 ≈ **63.1579%** stocks: closer, still above 60%.

The needed amount depends on which sleeve is overweight. If stocks were €540 and bonds €440, adding €120 to bonds would push stocks further below 60%. Check direction before recycling a worked answer.

## Choose a rule before watching the winners

<MCQ

A rule is a thermostat: decide what counts as too far and when you check, rather than changing the setting after every market headline. **Calendar rebalancing** resets on specified dates. **Threshold rebalancing** acts when a measured deviation reaches a stated limit. A **combined rule** tests the threshold only at scheduled reviews.

For our example, define absolute stock drift as:

$$d=|w_S-0.60|$$

A **5 percentage point** threshold means $d\ge0.05$, so its band is 55%–65%, with a trigger at either boundary. Some policies use strict exceedance or relative bands instead; the convention is part of the rule. These are illustrative rules, not an optimal universal interval. [Vanguard compares calendar, threshold and combined methods](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/portfolio-management/rebalancing-your-portfolio).

### Apply three policies to one observation

On 15 May, stocks weigh 66.6667%. The next annual review is 31 December. Assume the threshold policy observes weights today.

| Policy written in advance | Trigger today? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Reset each 31 December | No | Today is not the reset date |
| Act at absolute drift of at least 5 percentage points | Yes | 6.6667 is at least 5 |
| Check the same threshold only each 31 December | No | There is no scheduled check today |

At a December review with a 63% stock weight, the pure calendar policy resets, but the combined policy does not: 63% − 60% = 3 percentage points. A review is not automatically a trade.

### When to use it

A calendar rule is easy to administer but can trade for tiny deviations and leave large drift between dates. Threshold rules link action to the size of the gap but need monitoring; weights can jump across a band between observations. Combined rules reduce checks and small adjustments but allow drift between reviews. Tighter bands can create more transactions, costs and tax events.

Write down the target, portfolio boundary, review frequency, absolute or relative threshold, equality convention and destination: full target, edge of a band or partial adjustment. This lesson restores the full target when triggered. A rule to move only to the band edge requires different trade amounts.

## Costs and changing risks limit the clean answer

The target calculation is a plan for holdings at stated prices. Implementation adds **commissions** charged for executing trades, **bid–ask spreads** between buying and selling prices, possible market impact and taxes. A sale in a taxable account may realise a taxable gain; the treatment depends on jurisdiction, account and holdings. [FINRA flags both transaction charges and potential capital-gains taxes](https://www.finra.org/investors/investing/investing-basics/asset-allocation-diversification).

### Work the fee into the funding equation

Invent a **fixed €2 total fee**, paid entirely from stock-sale proceeds, with no tax, no spread and no price move. There is no new cash. If you reuse the cost-free sale of €72, only €70 reaches bonds:

- Stocks: €720 − €72 = **€648**
- Bonds: €360 + €72 − €2 = **€430**
- Total: €648 + €430 = **€1,078**
- Stock weight: €648 ÷ €1,078 ≈ **60.1113%**

To reach 60/40 **after this particular fixed fee**, use the post-fee total:

$$S^*=0.60(1080-2)=646.80,\qquad B^*=0.40(1078)=431.20$$

Sell €720 − €646.80 = **€73.20** of stocks. Pay €2, then buy **€71.20** of bonds. Funding check: €73.20 sale = €71.20 purchase + €2 fee. Holdings become €646.80/€431.20, exactly 60/40 of €1,078. The fee still reduces wealth by €2; an exact weight does not undo it. Percentage-based charges or externally funded fees require their own equations.

### Restored weights do not promise a premium

Rebalancing controls the chosen allocation. Whether it beats leaving holdings alone depends on the return path and costs. Consider **one further invented period** after the original €720/€360 drift, with no cash flows or fees:

| Next-period returns | Leave €720/€360 alone | Restore €648/€432 first |
|---|---:|---:|
| Stocks +10%, bonds 0% | €792 + €360 = €1,152 | €712.80 + €432 = €1,144.80 |
| Stocks −10%, bonds 0% | €648 + €360 = €1,008 | €583.20 + €432 = €1,015.20 |

In the first path restoration ends €7.20 behind; in the second it ends €7.20 ahead. This two-path comparison is not a forecast or an estimate of average performance. It shows why selling a recent winner cannot guarantee a future advantage.

### When to use it

Before execution, check the target still suits the goal, time horizon and ability to bear losses; then examine tax lots, trading costs, liquidity and holding constraints. Rebalancing to an old target and deliberately adopting a new target are different decisions. Asset weights alone do not cap drawdowns, remove default risk or maintain a fixed volatility level. Recalculate exposures when the underlying risks change.
