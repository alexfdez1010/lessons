Half in a risky basket and half in a one-period certain payoff gives a simple expected-risk calculation. It does not make every stock's expected return a fixed multiple of its volatility, and it does not make borrowing cost the same as lending.

The **Capital Market Line (CML)** describes a particular set of efficient portfolios under a model. To understand it, keep three questions separate: what combinations a chosen risky basket permits, which combinations are efficient, and what extra equilibrium assumptions identify that basket with the market. All numbers and frontier shapes here are invented. Returns, rates and standard deviations use the same single period and currency, with no fees or taxes unless stated.

## Specify what is certain over the investment period

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An ideal asset has exactly the same one-period return in every state. What follows about its relation to a risky return?" options={[{text:'Its variance and covariance are zero; correlation is undefined',correct:true},{text:'Its correlation must be zero because its variance is zero'},{text:'Its purchasing-power return is certain at every future horizon'},{text:'Every government bill is therefore free of every risk'}]} explanation="Correlation divides covariance by both standard deviations. A constant return has zero standard deviation, so that correlation ratio is undefined, even though covariance is zero." />

A **risk-free asset in this model** pays a known return $r_f$ over the specified period, in the chosen currency, with no uncertainty about payment. Its variance is zero. That is a precise assumption about a payoff and horizon; a government label does not establish every part of it.

For a constant return, $R_f-\mathbb E[R_f]=0$ in every state. Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(R_f)=0,\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(R_f,R_X)=\mathbb E[0(R_X-\mathbb E[R_X])]=0.
$$

But $\rho_{fX}=\operatorname{Cov}(R_f,R_X)/(\sigma_f\sigma_X)$ divides by zero. **Correlation is undefined**, rather than a measured zero correlation. It is the zero variance and covariance that simplify the portfolio calculation.

A short bill held to its matching maturity in the investor's currency can be a useful proxy under stated credit assumptions. Sell it early and its price may move; use another currency and the conversion may vary; roll it into another bill and the next rate is unknown. Even a certain nominal payoff is not a certain real payoff when inflation is uncertain. If nominal return is 3% and realised period inflation 5%, real return is $1.03/1.05-1\approx-1.904762\%$.

### When to use the assumption

Match payoff maturity, currency and return period before treating an asset as the anchor. A near-risk-free proxy with nonzero risk requires the actual variance and covariance terms; the exact zero-variance formulas then become approximations. The [CFA curriculum distinguishes the risk-free allocation construction from the market-equilibrium model](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/portfolio-risk-return-part-2).

## A fixed risky basket gives a capital allocation line

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="With 50% in a basket of volatility 16% and 50% in the ideal certain asset, is portfolio volatility 8% or approximately 11.31%?" hint="Only the risky basket contributes random variation. Take the square root after scaling its variance." text="It is 8%: variance is 0.5²× 0.16², so standard deviation is 0.5 × 0.16. The 11.31% answer would come from incorrectly taking the square root of a half-weighted variance." />

Let $m$ be the risky basket's weight measured against **initial net wealth**, and $1-m$ the weight in the certain asset. A **capital allocation line (CAL)** is the expected-return/standard-deviation set from mixing that particular basket with the risk-free asset. Any selected basket gives a CAL under these assumptions; it need not be the best basket.

For equal borrowing and lending rate $r_f$:

$$
R_p=(1-m)r_f+mR_X,
$$
$$
\mu_p=\mathbb E[R_p]=r_f+m(\mu_X-r_f),
$$
$$
\operatorname{Var}(R_p)=m^2\sigma_X^2,\qquad\sigma_p=|m|\sigma_X.
$$

Our allocation viewer restricts $m\ge0$, so its volatility is $m\sigma_X$. If shorting the risky basket were allowed, negative $m$ would still give **positive** standard deviation. For $m=-0.5$, $\sigma_X=16\%$ gives 8%, not −8%; it lies on a different branch. Do not silently extend the positive-weight CML formula to that branch.

Take risky mean 10%, volatility 16% and certain rate 3%:

### When to use a CAL

Use it to compare exposures to one fixed basket at consistent financing terms. The straightness follows from a constant financing payoff and one source of random basket returns; it does not imply every risky portfolio or individual asset lies on that line. [William Sharpe's Stanford teaching chapter derives the two-asset construction and different financing rates](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/mia/rr/mia_rr5.htm).

## Efficiency selects a tangency portfolio; CML adds a market interpretation

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Does adding a risk-free asset make the CAL through any chosen stock efficient?" options={[{text:'No; the chosen risky basket must maximise the feasible excess-mean/volatility slope',correct:true},{text:'Yes; every CAL has the same best slope as every other CAL'},{text:'Yes; every stock becomes the market portfolio automatically'},{text:'No; even the best feasible basket cannot be mixed with cash'}]} explanation="A feasible line and the efficient envelope are different objects. Selecting the steepest admissible positive-slope CAL requires the risky opportunity set and the model's constraints." />

An **efficient portfolio** offers the highest expected return at a specified standard deviation among the allowed choices. A **tangency portfolio** maximises the slope from the risk-free anchor:

$$
S_X=\frac{\mu_X-r_f}{\sigma_X}.
$$

This is an expected **Sharpe ratio** for the stated period. It compares an excess mean with total return standard deviation; the realised return can differ from that mean. Among feasible baskets with the same financing terms, a higher slope gives more expected return at the same attainable volatility.

For example, basket A has mean 9%, volatility 20%, while basket M has mean 10%, volatility 16%, and $r_f=3\%$. Their slopes are $6/20=0.30$ and $7/16=0.4375$. At volatility 8%, mixing A with cash needs weight 0.4 and gives $3+0.4(9-3)=5.4\%$. Mixing M at 0.5 gives 6.5%. **M beats A in this comparison**; that alone does not prove M beats every other feasible basket. The viewer supplies a synthetic opportunity set in which M really is the lending tangency.

The **CML** is the efficient CAL through the **market portfolio**, the value-weighted basket of all risky assets, under the corresponding equilibrium assumptions. A computed tangency portfolio over your chosen fund list is not automatically the entire market. The standard separation result assumes a one-period mean–variance framework, shared beliefs and opportunity set, frictionless accessible assets, and suitable borrowing/lending at the same known rate. Investor risk preferences then choose the position on the common efficient line. Constraints, different beliefs, taxes, costs and liabilities can change that conclusion.

Under that model, for efficient portfolios on the nonnegative-weight branch:

$$
\mu_p=r_f+\frac{\mu_M-r_f}{\sigma_M}\sigma_p.
$$

The line bounds the risky-only efficient set in the same model. It is not a return entitlement for any asset with that standard deviation. Without borrowing, the segment beyond M is not automatically available; higher-risk choices may stay on a risky-only frontier.

### When to use separation

Use it as a conditional benchmark for allocation, not an instruction that every person must hold the same real-world fund. Tangency depends on estimated means, variances, covariances and restrictions; estimation error can move it. Check whether the proposed basket, financing and investor circumstances fit the model before calling a point efficient.

## Different borrowing and lending rates can change the efficient basket

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A higher borrowing rate makes the line through a fixed M flatter beyond M. Does that prove the entire efficient frontier has just that one kink?" hint="Could another risky basket become the better choice at the borrowing rate?" text="No. Another risky basket could become preferable. The fixed-M allocation set has a kink. With a full risky opportunity set, the borrowing tangency can differ from the lending tangency, leaving a curved risky-only section between them. The frontier must be rebuilt under the actual financing constraints." />

Let lending rate $r_L=3\%$ and borrowing rate $r_B=6\%$, and keep the same basket M. For $0\le m\le1$:

$$
\mu_p=r_L+m(\mu_M-r_L).
$$

For $m>1$, the borrowing position is a liability:

$$
\mu_p=m\mu_M-(m-1)r_B
=r_B+m(\mu_M-r_B),\qquad\sigma_p=m\sigma_M.
$$

At $m=1.5$, mean $=1.5(10\%)-0.5(6\%)=12\%$, with volatility 24%. The equal-rate answer was 13.5%. The 1.5-point difference is the extra 3-point interest cost on borrowed weight 0.5. The fixed-basket borrowing slope is $(10-6)/16=0.25$, versus lending slope 0.4375. If M actually loses 20%, the same borrowing-cost arithmetic gives −33% on equity, before any margin liquidation or costs.

### Rebuild the envelope rather than relabeling a kink

The graphic uses an invented risky frontier with $\sigma$ and $\mu$ expressed as percentage numbers:

$$
\sigma^2=14.4^2+a(\mu-8.67)^2,\qquad
 a=\frac{16^2}{0.19\times7^2}=27.4973147\ldots.
$$

Its upper branch is $\mu=8.67+\sqrt{(\sigma^2-14.4^2)/a}$. At $\sigma=16$, it gives 10%, with slope $7/16=0.4375$, exactly tangent to lending at 3%. This is a teaching geometry, not a frontier estimated from actual securities.

At borrowing rate 6%, the different borrowing tangency B is:

$$
\mu_B=8.67+\frac{14.4^2}{a(8.67-6)}
=11.494382\ldots\%,\qquad\sigma_B=20.656954\ldots\%.
$$

You can verify that B satisfies the same frontier equation. At volatility 24%, the line through the 6% borrowing intercept and B gives:

$$
6+\frac{11.494382\ldots-6}{20.656954\ldots}\times24
=12.383573\ldots\%.
$$

That exceeds the **12% fixed-M mix**, under this synthetic opportunity set. Between M and B the efficient envelope follows the risky-only curve: investors need not borrow at all in that part. With equal rates B coincides with M and this intervening section disappears.

### When to model actual constraints

Use your actual feasible borrowing rate, leverage limit and available baskets. A one-period variance diagram does not model collateral calls, forced sales, changing loan rates, default, liquidity or the path of losses. If borrowing is prohibited, remove those combinations. A universal claim that the practical frontier is always two lines joined at the original M overlooks re-optimisation and constraints. [Sharpe's optimisation chapter explains how constraints can change separation](https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/mia/opt/mia_opt3.htm).

## The SML uses beta; the CML uses total portfolio volatility

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An individual stock has standard deviation 32%. Can you automatically apply 3%+(7%/16%)× 32% to assign its expected return?" options={[{text:'No; that CML expression is for efficient market/risk-free mixtures under its assumptions',correct:true},{text:'Yes; every stock lies on the efficient CML by definition'},{text:'Yes; beta and standard deviation are always the same number'},{text:'No; individual stocks never have an expected return'}]} explanation="The CML is a portfolio-efficiency relation using total standard deviation. CAPM's Security Market Line instead uses beta to model expected returns of individual assets as well as portfolios." />

The **Security Market Line (SML)** is the graph of the CAPM expected-return relation against **beta**, $\beta_i=\operatorname{Cov}(R_i,R_M)/\operatorname{Var}(R_M)$, rather than standard deviation. Beta measures market-related sensitivity under that model. The [CFA refresher distinguishes CML and SML scope](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/portfolio-risk-return-part-2).

With $r_f=3\%$ and market expected premium 7%, a stock with beta 0.5 has CAPM expected return $3\%+0.5\times7\%=6.5\%$. It could still have total volatility 32% because of additional company-specific variation. Feeding 32% into the CML gives 17%, but that is the line's efficient-portfolio reference at that risk, not the stock's assigned CAPM mean. A half-market/half-risk-free portfolio also has mean 6.5%, but volatility 8%; equal means do not imply identical risk.
