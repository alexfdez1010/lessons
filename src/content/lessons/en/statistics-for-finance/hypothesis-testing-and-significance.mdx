A report estimates a gross trading edge of 2 basis points and prints p=0.0455. That is not a 95.45% probability of a genuine opportunity. Change the dependence assumptions and the p-value can change; subtract a known 3-basis-point cost and the estimated net mean becomes negative. The number is useful only with its question, model and research process attached.

A **hypothesis test** compares observed data with what a specified statistical model would produce under a stated hypothesis. It can detect incompatibility with that model; it does not identify a unique cause, prove an effect, calculate the probability of skill or guarantee future profitability. Here one **basis point (bp)** is 0.01 percentage point of return. All examples are invented; no live strategy is evaluated.

## Specify the null, statistic and sampling model together

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Does stating H₀: mean edge=0 completely determine a p-value?" options={[{text:'No; the sampling model, statistic, tail rule and research design also matter',correct:true},{text:'Yes; every zero-mean dataset has the same normal sampling distribution'},{text:'Yes; a positive observed mean alone determines its significance'},{text:'No; a null hypothesis can never specify a numerical mean'}]} explanation="A mean restriction alone does not specify variance, dependence, distribution or selection. A p-value is computed under the null and the other assumptions of the test." />

The **null hypothesis** $H_0$ specifies the claim tested: for example mean gross excess return $\mu=0$ relative to a benchmark fixed before looking at the data. The **alternative** might be two-sided, $\mu\ne0$, or one-sided, such as $\mu>0$. A null need not be zero: testing whether a gross mean covers a known cost 3 bp asks about a different reference value. Choose the tail rule, sample/stopping plan and test family before searching for a favourable result.

For a mean estimate $\bar x$ and a null value $\mu_0$, a standardised statistic divides the estimated gap by its **standard error (SE)**, the sampling standard deviation of the estimator. Under an iid Gaussian model with **known** individual standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\qquad
Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)\quad\text{under }H_0.
$$

“Iid” means independent and identically distributed. Known $\sigma$ is a deliberate teaching assumption. With unknown variance estimated by sample $s$, **iid normal observations** instead give an exact Student statistic with $n-1$ degrees of freedom:

$$
T=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}\quad\text{under }H_0.
$$

A **degree of freedom** here reflects estimating the mean before estimating variance. The distinction changes probabilities, especially for small samples. If $n=36$, $\bar x=1\%$, $s=3\%$ and $\mu_0=0$, then SE$=3\%/6=0.5\%$ and $t=2$. Its two-sided $t_{35}$ p-value is approximately 0.053308, not the standard-normal 0.045500. The 5% two-sided critical value is about 2.030108, not 1.96. A blanket “t above 2 proves an edge” misstates both the reference distribution and the inference. [Penn State distinguishes known-variance normal and unknown-variance t procedures](https://online.stat.psu.edu/stat415/Lesson09).

### Dependence can change the denominator

For a stationary series with marginal variance $\sigma^2$ and lag correlations $\rho_k$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}
\left[1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\rho_k\right].
$$

An invented stationary Gaussian AR(1) model has correlations $\rho_k=0.75^k$: observations retain some influence from previous observations. With $n=10,000$ and marginal $\sigma=100$bp, the independent SE would be 1 bp. The bracket above is 6.9976, so the correct **known model SE** is $1\sqrt{6.9976}=2.645298$bp. For the same observed mean 2 bp and null 0, $z=2/2.645298\approx0.756059$, giving two-sided p≈0.449614.

This positive dependence increases uncertainty. Dependence need not always increase it: negative lag covariances can reduce the variance of a mean. Estimating those quantities adds uncertainty absent from this known-model example. [CFA's model-misspecification reading discusses serial correlation and inference problems](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/model-misspecification).

### When to use a reference distribution

Check distribution, finite moments, dependence, changing variance, overlapping returns and a stable parameter before choosing a procedure. A suitable central limit theorem can justify approximate normal inference under its conditions; raw fat-tailed returns do not automatically tell you the exact distribution of a studentised mean. Heteroskedasticity/autocorrelation-consistent SEs or block resampling require their own assumptions. More observations do not repair look-ahead bias, an invalid stopping rule, regime changes or a wrong model automatically.

## A p-value and a confidence interval describe conditional evidence

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A valid two-sided test reports p=0.03. Is there a 3% probability that the null is true, or a 97% probability the strategy will profit?" hint="Which hypothesis and model were assumed while computing the tail area?" text="Neither. Under the stated null and model, the probability of a statistic at least as extreme under the selected rule is 3%. It is not a posterior probability of the null, a probability that chance caused the result, or a profit forecast." />

A **p-value** is a tail probability under $H_0$ **and the sampling/selection assumptions**. For a two-sided standard-normal statistic:

$$
p=P_{H_0,\,\mathrm{model}}(|Z|\ge|z_{\mathrm{observed}}|)
=2\left[1-\Phi(|z_{\mathrm{observed}}|)\right],
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard-normal cumulative distribution. It includes outcomes **at least as extreme**, not just the exact observed data. Small p indicates a particular kind of incompatibility with the specified model; it does not prove the tested alternative or tell us which assumption failed. [The ASA's statement explains these limits and why thresholds alone do not settle a substantive conclusion](https://www.amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/P-valuestatement.pdf).

For a prechosen significance level $\alpha$, a valid procedure rejects when its rejection rule is met, often written $p\le\alpha$. “Statistically significant at 5%” describes that procedure's decision, not the size, truth, durability or economic value of the effect. Values just either side of 0.05 are not two different kinds of reality.

### Work the normal p-value and its matching interval

Return to the **independent Gaussian known-SD model**: $n=10,000$, $\sigma=100$bp, SE$=1$bp, observed gross mean 2 bp and $H_0:\mu=0$. Then:

$$
z=(2-0)/1=2,
$$
$$
p=2[1-\Phi(2)]\approx2(1-0.977249868)
=0.045500264.
$$

The matching two-sided 95% **confidence interval for the mean** is:

$$
\bar x\pm z_{0.975}\mathrm{SE}
=2\pm1.959963985(1)
=[0.040036,\ 3.959964]\text{ bp}.
$$

Zero is just outside this interval, matching rejection at 5% in this model. A mean interval is not a range containing 95% of individual trade returns. Its **coverage** refers to the procedure: across repeated samples generated under its assumptions,95% of the intervals would cover the fixed mean. After this interval is observed, frequentist coverage is not a 95% posterior probability assigned to that fixed parameter. It also does not guarantee the mean remains constant in a future regime. [NIST explains the test–interval correspondence](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section1/prc15.htm).

<HypothesisEvidenceLab client:visible title="Normal p-value and matching mean intervals" costBps={0} />

Move the observed mean while keeping the assumed true mean for power fixed. The p-value and interval change with the sample estimate; the planning power does not. Switching from 5% to 1% changes the decision threshold and widens the matching interval, but leaves the observed p-value unchanged.

### When to report them

Report the estimate, units, interval, p-value, null, model and research design together. Failing to reject does not prove zero; rejecting does not prove profitable or causal. A more relevant null can concern a specified cost or economically meaningful margin, rather than zero. A Bayesian posterior requires an explicit likelihood and prior; it cannot be obtained by reading $1-p$ as a probability of success.

## Power is a planning probability at a specified alternative

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A test fails to reject zero. Can that alone establish that any useful effect is absent?" options={[{text:'No; evaluate uncertainty and power for a specified meaningful effect',correct:true},{text:'Yes; a non-significant estimate proves the parameter is exactly zero'},{text:'Yes; Type II errors are impossible whenever p exceeds 0.05'},{text:'No; power is simply one minus the observed p-value'}]} explanation="A procedure may often miss an effect of a relevant size. Power is computed at an assumed alternative and design, not as 1−p after observing the result." />

A **Type I error** rejects a true null. A valid level-$\alpha$ test controls that probability at most $\alpha$ under its stated null/design; our continuous known-normal test has exact size $\alpha$. This is not the probability that a particular rejection is wrong. A **Type II error** fails to reject at a specified false-null parameter. Its probability is $\beta$ at that parameter; **power** is $1-\beta$.

Power depends on effect size, SE, threshold and design. There is no single power number for every nonzero effect. For known-normal two-sided testing, define $\delta=(\mu_* -\mu_0)/\mathrm{SE}$ and critical $c=z_{1-\alpha/2}$:

$$
\mathrm{Power}(\mu_*)=\Phi(-c-\delta)+1-\Phi(c-\delta).
$$

Assume a **true mean for planning** of 2 bp versus null 0, with known SE 1 bp. Thus $\delta=2$. At $\alpha=0.05$, $c=1.959964$:

$$
\mathrm{Power}=\Phi(-3.959964)+1-\Phi(-0.040036)
\approx0.516005.
$$

Only about 51.6005% of repetitions reject under that particular 2 bp alternative; about 48.3995% miss it. At $\alpha=0.01$, critical 2.575829 gives power≈28.2368% with the same effect and SE. Stricter false-alarm control reduces detection probability here. Neither number is the probability that the observed 2 bp estimate is true. [Penn State teaches power as a probability conditional on a specified alternative](https://online.stat.psu.edu/statprogram/reviews/statistical-concepts/power-analysis).

### When to plan sample size or interpret a non-rejection

Choose a meaningful effect before collecting the confirmation sample. More independent, comparable observations can reduce SE and increase power at fixed size; reducing measurement noise, using an appropriate paired design or changing the relevant test can also help. More data is not the only lever, and a longer changing-regime history need not estimate one stable current mean better. To support a sufficiently small effect rather than exactly zero, use an explicitly designed equivalence or non-inferiority procedure with stated margins; a large ordinary p-value is not that test.

## Searching many strategies changes the inference

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="Twenty independent tests each have a true null and size 5%. Is the chance of at least one false rejection 5%,100% or another value?" hint="Compute the chance that none rejects first." text="None rejects with probability 0.95²⁰. At least one rejects with probability 1−0.95²⁰≈64.1514%. The expected count is 20 × 0.05=1, which is a different quantity from that probability." />

**Multiplicity** means making several statistical comparisons. **Data snooping** includes searching rules, windows, assets or parameters and reporting a favourable result while ignoring the search. Even if each unselected test were valid, selection changes the interpretation of the winner. If the underlying test was invalid, merely counting attempts will not fix it.

For $m$ **independent, true-null tests of exact size** $\alpha$:

$$
P(\text{at least one false rejection})=1-(1-\alpha)^m.
$$

For 20 at 5%, this is $1-0.95^{20}=0.641514\ldots$. The expected false-rejection count is $m\alpha=1$; it does not mean exactly one will occur. Without independence, the product formula need not hold. The union bound gives probability at most $\min(1,m\alpha)$ if each test's true-null rejection probability is at most $\alpha$. For 20 it only gives the upper bound 1, not an exact forecast.

The **Bonferroni** rule tests each member of a specified family at $\alpha/m$. For family level 5% and 20 tests:

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{per\ test}}=0.05/20=0.0025.
$$

For valid marginal p-values and the stated family, the union bound gives overall false-rejection probability at most $20\times0.0025=0.05$ under all true nulls; more generally it controls the probability of rejecting any true null in that family. **Independence is not required for this control.** It can be conservative, and changing the family or hiding searches changes the claim. [NIST describes Bonferroni simultaneous control](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section4/prc473.htm).

### When to validate a researched strategy

Log the full search, specify the family and use appropriate corrections or a fresh confirmation plan. Keep validation genuinely unused in tuning and consider time order, leakage, survivorship and overlapping observations. An out-of-sample period stops being untouched once it repeatedly guides revisions. Neither a correction nor a holdout guarantees profitability or fixes future distribution changes. A selected p=0.04 does not itself tell you the posterior probability that the strategy is noise; that requires assumptions about alternatives, prevalence and selection.

## Economic relevance requires an estimate in net units and uncertainty

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A gross mean is statistically significant at 5%. Does that prove the strategy earns positive net returns?" options={[{text:'No; estimate costs, relevant net effect and uncertainty under a suitable design',correct:true},{text:'Yes; significance turns every trade into a positive payoff'},{text:'Yes; costs cannot exceed a statistically significant estimate'},{text:'No; a small effect can never be statistically significant'}]} explanation="Statistical incompatibility with gross zero is a different question from the size and uncertainty of a net return. Significant does not mean true, durable or profitable." />

Return to the independent known-normal example: observed gross mean 2 bp, SE 1 bp, two-sided p≈0.0455 and 95% gross interval[0.040036,3.959964]bp. Stipulate a known constant **total cost 3 bp per observation**, measured in the same units and not already included in the gross returns. Then:

$$
\widehat\mu_{\mathrm{net}}=2-3=-1\text{ bp},
$$
$$
CI_{\mathrm{net}}=[0.040036-3,\ 3.959964-3]
=[-2.959964,\ 0.959964]\text{ bp}.
$$

A known constant subtraction leaves SE unchanged. The estimated net mean is negative and the interval includes both negative and positive net means. This does not show a loss on every trade, prove the true net mean is negative, or establish that gross mean 2 bp is a fixed fact. A two-sided test of **net zero** uses $z=-1$ and p≈0.317311, a different null from gross zero. With uncertain or variable costs, their variance and covariance with gross outcomes must also enter the analysis.

### When to turn research into a decision

State gross versus net, benchmark, exposure, turnover and the cost horizon. Check spreads, commissions, slippage, market impact, capacity and funding where relevant; do not subtract a per-trade cost from an annual mean without conversion. Report uncertainty and out-of-sample evidence. The minimum effect worth implementing depends on risk and constraints; no universal p threshold substitutes for that decision. A favourable historical estimate, however precise under its model, is not a promised future return.
