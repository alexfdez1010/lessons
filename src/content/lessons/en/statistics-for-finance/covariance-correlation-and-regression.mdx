A line explains about 87 % of the variation in four observations. Freeze that exact line and apply it to four later observations: its R² score can be negative. Both calculations can be correct. They answer different questions about different rows.

We will keep one small dataset visible from covariance through the fitted residuals, then test what the line does and does not establish. All observations and financial returns are invented. Returns are simple returns over matched periods in the same currency, before costs unless stated. A **predictor** supplies the horizontal coordinate; a **response** supplies the vertical one. Calling a variable a predictor does not establish that it was known before a trade.

## 1. Center paired moments and retain their units

**Covariance** averages the product of deviations from each variable’s own mean. For a population model with finite second moments,

$$
\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)&={}\\&E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)].\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\mu_X$ and $\mu_Y$ are the population means. Think of each centered product as a signed contribution to the total. Two positive deviations contribute positively, and so do two negative deviations. Opposite signs contribute negatively. A positive total need not mean a majority of matching signs: large products can dominate small ones. It also does not say that both raw returns are positive.

### Work the same four pairs

The original observations are X = (2 %,−1 %,3 %,−4 %) and Y = (1 %,−2 %,4 %,−3 %). Both means are zero. The following calculations use **percentage points (pp)**: a return of 2 % is displayed as 2, rather than the decimal return 0.02.

Write $S_{xx}$ for the sum of squared X deviations, $S_{yy}$ for the Y version, and $S_{xy}$ for the sum of cross-products. The centered sums are $S_{xx}$ = 30 pp², $S_{yy}$ = 30 pp² and $S_{xy}$ = 28 pp². If these four rows are a complete equally likely population model, covariance is 28/4 = **7 pp²** and each variance is 30/4 = **7.5 pp²**. Under the conventional sample rule, divide every centered sum by n −1 = 3: covariance 28/3 and variances 10 pp².

**Pearson correlation** standardizes covariance by both standard deviations. It requires finite, strictly positive variances. When all moments use the same divisor,

$$
r=\frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}\sqrt{S_{yy}}}=\frac{28}{30}=\frac{14}{15}\approx0.933333.
$$

Using rounded standard deviations 2.74 in place of √7.5 gives only an approximation; 2.74² is not exactly 7.5. A constant series instead has zero covariance and zero standard deviation, so its Pearson ratio is undefined, not zero.

### When to use these moments

Covariance has useful product units. The population value 7 pp² is **0.0007** in decimal-return units squared: both columns were divided by 100, so covariance was divided by 10,000. Standardization removes those units; it does not make covariance meaningless. Use compatible paired periods, units and denominators before computing a portfolio variance or a regression slope. The [NIST centered-sum formulas](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmd/section4/pmd431.htm) will let the common divisor cancel from that slope next.

## 2. Fit a line by squared vertical residuals

Imagine placing a straight ruler through the scatter. Its tilt and height are free; the criterion determines which placement wins. **Ordinary least squares (OLS)** chooses an estimated intercept $\widehat\alpha$ and slope $\widehat\beta$ to minimize the sum of squared **vertical residuals**:

$$
\begin{aligned}\widehat y_i&=\widehat\alpha+\widehat\beta x_i,\\e_i&=y_i-\widehat y_i,\\\operatorname{SSE}&=\sum_i e_i^2.\end{aligned}
$$

SSE means sum of squared errors of this fitted rule; here those errors are its observed residuals. A hypothetical population model might be Y = α + βX + ε. The unhatted α and β denote hypothetical population coefficients. Its unknown **model error** ε is not automatically the residual from estimated coefficients. Fitting a line also does not establish that the population conditional mean is linear or that its errors are independent or normally distributed.

### Derive the minimizer rather than guessing the tilt

For any slope b, the best intercept is the average of y−bx, so a = $\bar y$−b $\bar x$. Substituting gives

$$
\begin{aligned}\operatorname{SSE}(b)&=S_{yy}-2bS_{xy}+b^2S_{xx}\\&=S_{yy}-\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}}\\&\quad+S_{xx}\left(b-\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\right)^2.\end{aligned}
$$

When $S_{xx}$>0, the final square is minimized at **$\widehat\beta$ = $S_{xy}$/$S_{xx}$**. The intercept is **$\widehat\alpha$ = $\bar y$−$\widehat\beta \bar x$**. Thus the same conventional sample covariance divided by the sample predictor variance gives the slope; population-divisor moments give the same ratio. This is an algebraic fit, requiring no Gaussian sampling assumption. [MIT derives the same least-squares construction](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/mit18_05_s22_class26-prep.pdf).

For the four pairs, $\widehat\beta$ = 28/30 = 14/15 and $\widehat\alpha$ = 0. The table retains the exact fractions before rounding.

### When to use OLS

Use it when squared response errors are the chosen fitting loss. Zero residuals are allowed, so squaring produces nonnegative terms rather than making every term strictly positive. The fit is asymmetric: reversing X and Y minimizes a different set of vertical gaps. Here both reverse and forward slopes happen to be 14/15 because the spreads match; the reverse slope is **not** the reciprocal 15/14.

A unique intercept-and-slope pair requires a varying predictor. We will work the constant-predictor exception explicitly below. A smooth extension of the fitted line beyond observed predictor values is algebra, not validated forecasting evidence.

## 3. Interpret beta and the intercept within a return model

A **slope** says how the fitted response changes when its supplied predictor changes by one unit. A slope of 1.5 maps a one-percentage-point predictor change to a 1.5-point change **in the fitted line**. The actual response still has a residual. It need not move by exactly that amount, and beta is not the ratio of the assets’ total volatilities.

In a market model, the predictor might be a benchmark return. **Benchmark** means the selected comparison portfolio or index. Its regression slope is commonly called **beta**. A fitted market-model beta is an estimate with a chosen benchmark and period; the **Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)** is an equilibrium model relating expected returns to market exposure. Interpreting this fitted beta as its parameter requires the corresponding market-portfolio and model assumptions.

## 4. Define R² on its fitting rows and handle constants

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Every response equals 1 and a free-intercept OLS fit has no residuals. What is its centered R² ratio?"}

A flat line at the observed response mean provides a comparison. Its squared error is **total centered variation**, SST = Σ($y_i$−$\bar y$)². Define the centered score

$$
R^2=1-\frac{\operatorname{SSE}}{\operatorname{SST}},\qquad\operatorname{SST}>0.
$$

For unweighted simple OLS **with an intercept**, on the **same rows used for fitting**, the fitted centered sum and residual sum split total variation. If both variables vary, this gives

$$
\begin{aligned}\operatorname{SSE}&=S_{yy}-S_{xy}^2/S_{xx},\\R^2&=\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}}=r^2.\end{aligned}
$$

For our original four rows, SST = 30, SSE = 58/15 and fitted centered sum = 392/15 pp². Their sum is 450/15 = 30. Hence R² = 196/225≈**0.871111**, or 87.111111 % of this sample’s centered variation accounted for by its fitted line. It is not an 87 % hit rate, a causal share, or the probability that a hypothesis is true. The remaining variation is **residual variation**, not automatically pure noise or stock-specific risk.

### A constant predictor gives a family; a constant response removes the denominator

Set every X to 2 pp while retaining Y = (1,−2,4,−3) pp. The best fitted response is its mean, zero. Every pair satisfying **α + 2β = 0** gives that same fitted value. For example, (α,β) = (0,0),(−2,1),(2,−1) all give zero at X = 2. The slope and intercept are not separately identified by these data. SSE = SST = 30 and centered R² = 0, while Pearson correlation is undefined.

If instead X varies as originally and every Y is 1 pp, the unique fitted coefficients are α = 1 and β = 0. All residuals vanish, but SST = 0. The ratio 1 − 0/0 is **undefined**. If both columns are constant, coefficient nonuniqueness and undefined centered R² can occur together.

### Removing the intercept changes the problem

For X = (−1,0,1), Y = (2,3,4), the free-intercept fit is y = 3 + x with no residuals. Force the intercept to zero and OLS chooses slope Σxy/Σx² = 2/2 = 1. Its residuals are 3,3,3, so SSE = 27. The centered SST is (−1)² + 0² + 1² = 2. Thus the **centered score is 1 − 27/2 = −12.5**, although the data correlation is 1.

### When to use R²

Keep its formula, intercept convention and scoring rows with it. The familiar 0–1 bounds hold for the in-sample free-intercept OLS problem with a nonconstant target. An arbitrary rule, constrained fit or held-out prediction can have a negative centered score. A high value also does not establish correct specification, and a low value does not by itself make an estimate useless for its stated purpose.

The r² identity now concerns the correlation of **Z and Y**, not the original X and Y. ### When to use the scatter and a richer model

Plot the pairs and residuals when a linear summary misses the question. A curved feature can represent a known relation, but trying many features until one fits a sample raises selection and overfitting problems. A fitted or withheld association does not prove that intervening on X would change Y. A common cause, reverse direction, chance and selection may need investigation; the coefficient alone cannot select among them. Statistical association does not require a proven causal mechanism to exist, and a plausible story does not validate one.

The residual numerators are 58,29,−29,−58 over 15. SSE = 2(58² + 29²)/225 = **1682/45≈37.377778 pp²**. The later response mean is zero and its centered SST is 4 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 10 pp². Therefore

$$
R^2_{\mathrm{later}}=1-\frac{1682/45}{10}=-\frac{616}{225}\approx-2.737778.
$$

The negative score means squared error exceeds the centered-mean comparison on these later rows. That comparison happens also to equal the earlier fitting mean of zero here. It is not a negative variance or an impossible coefficient; the fit was not selected to minimize this later block’s errors. [The official R² score documentation makes its possible negative values and constant-target domain explicit](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.metrics.r2_score.html).

### When to use held-out evaluation

Separate fitting, model selection and final evaluation chronologically when time matters, and retain the information available at each decision date. Here the later market-like X values are **observed for conditional evaluation**. They are not known at the start of those periods. A strategy trying to trade before X arrives needs predictors available then, with appropriately dated targets; a contemporaneous scatter alone cannot establish that forecast.

A positive held-out score would still be evidence about the stated evaluation block and procedure, not guaranteed future profits, stability or causation. Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests need additional sampling/model assumptions; the [following estimation lesson](/statistics-for-finance/sampling-clt-and-estimation) develops that next step. Keeping a fitted line separate from a future law is part of using it correctly.
