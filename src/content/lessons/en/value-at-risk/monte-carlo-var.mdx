A million simulated days can make a percentile look impressively stable. They cannot reveal a loss mechanism the generator has ruled out. Monte Carlo VaR needs both a well-defined calculation and a defensible model of the portfolio's horizon risks.

The method samples joint market scenarios, revalues today's positions in each and estimates a loss quantile. We will inspect each link using invented numbers whose answers can be checked without trusting the picture. Monte Carlo is a numerical method, not a distributional assumption or an automatic upgrade to the truth.

## Decide which probabilities the scenarios represent

<MCQ client:visible pretest

A **risk factor** is a market input affecting value: a stock price, exchange rate, yield curve or implied volatility surface. Choose a horizon and a joint model for those inputs. For a real-world loss-frequency question, the scenario probabilities should represent a **physical model**, often written $P$: assumptions about possible market outcomes and their frequencies. Estimating that model from data introduces uncertainty.

A **risk-neutral pricing measure**, $Q$, serves a different purpose. When that representation is valid under the chosen no-arbitrage model, Q supplies weights that reproduce traded values as discounted expected payoffs. The discounting uses a specified savings account as the reference asset. Those pricing weights need not equal physical frequencies. A scenario-based VaR engine may therefore have a physical outer simulation and a pricing model inside each scenario. [MathWorks' worked example](https://www.mathworks.com/help/fininst/simulating-electricity-prices-with-mean-reversion-and-jump-diffusion.html) explicitly separates real-world calibration from risk-neutral option valuation.

### Identical outcomes with different weights

Consider a complete two-state, one-period market: stock now 100 USD, endpoint either 80 or 120, no dividends and zero interest. Stipulate physical down probability 4% and up probability 96%. These unusually optimistic numbers are invented assumptions, not an estimate.

The no-arbitrage pricing weights satisfy $100=q(120)+(1-q)(80)$, so $q=0.5$ for up and $0.5$ for down. A **put** gives its holder the right to sell a share for its strike K; at expiry its payoff is $\max(K-S_h,0)$. The writer owes that payoff. A strike-100 put therefore pays 20 or zero, giving price $0.5(20)+0.5(0)=10$ USD per share under $Q$. Its physical expected payoff is $0.04(20)=0.8$ USD. An expected payoff under $P$ is not automatically a no-arbitrage price.

Ten shares start at 1000 USD. Their horizon losses are $1000-10(80)=200$ or $1000-10(120)=-200$ USD. Under $P$, 96% of the mass is at −200, so the inverse-CDF 95% loss quantile is **−200**. Under $Q$, only 50% is there, so the same quantile is **+200**. The state losses never changed; their weights did.

<ProbabilityMeasureComparison client:visible  />

A negative signed VaR is allowed by this quantile definition: the chosen percentile is a gain. The model still assigns 4% probability to losing 200 USD. If a report floors VaR at zero, disclose that reporting rule separately rather than changing the definition silently.

### When to use it

Before simulating, record the horizon, currency, portfolio, probability measure and joint-factor assumptions. Pricing-calibrated scenarios can answer a pricing-distribution question; do not label their tail frequencies physical forecasts without a justified conversion.

## Revalue today's positions at the horizon

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="The desk now has two short put contracts, but its old scenario file valued one. Can you reuse the old P&Ls unchanged?" text="No. Revalue the current book. In the specified expiry case at stock 80, two contracts have cash 1200 and liability 4000, so P&L is −2800 USD." />

Monte Carlo supplies scenarios. The valuation step supplies their portfolio losses. Freeze **current holdings** for a declared static-book calculation; do not replay the positions the desk happened to hold in old data. If a strategy rebalances, simulate its specified trading and financing rule instead.

Use a single accounting definition. With intermediate distributions accumulated to the horizon, one convenient nominal horizon P&L is

$$
\Pi_h=V_h+D_h-V_0,\qquad L_h=-\Pi_h.
$$

Here $V_h$ is the value of the positions still held and $D_h$ contains relevant cash flows not already in that value. Include coupons, dividends, option settlements, financing and transaction costs when they belong to the chosen book. Cash kept inside a valued cash account must not also be counted again in $D_h$. An excess-return or discounted P&L definition requires its own financing benchmark; declare it.

For an option that survives the horizon, shorten its remaining maturity and use the scenario's valuation inputs. At expiry, use the actual contractual payoff. Path-dependent instruments need sufficient path information and contractual monitoring; endpoint prices alone may not determine value. Full repricing respects the chosen pricing model's nonlinearities, not necessarily executable market prices or the true future distribution. [The RBA's comparison](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/1997/9708/value-at-risk.html) distinguishes scenario revaluation from local sensitivity approximations.

### A short put and its premium cash

This is a separate invented book, not the 10-USD put above. Sell one put contract at a stipulated current mark of 6 USD per share, strike 100 and multiplier 100; hold the 600-USD premium in cash at zero interest until expiry. Initial net wealth is cash 600 minus option liability 600 = zero. There are no new trades or other cash flows.

$$
\Pi_h=600-100\max(100-S_h,0),\qquad
L_h=100\max(100-S_h,0)-600.
$$

Two current contracts double both cash and liability: at stock 80, $1200-4000=-2800$ USD P&L. Do not simulate an old one-contract book and report it as risk for today's two-contract exposure. Net wealth initially zero also does not imply unlimited credit or no collateral needs.

A long vanilla put has negative stock delta; selling it reverses the position delta to positive. A fall in the stock therefore hurts the short position locally. A spot-only delta approximation also omits time, volatility and curvature effects. Monte Carlo can use either full repricing or an approximation; the sampling method alone does not decide that choice.

### When to use it

Check one scenario by hand before running thousands. Reconcile valuation date, current position size, cash flows and units. Compare approximations with complete repricing where finite moves or nonlinear terms matter.

## Choose the loss quantile convention explicitly

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"With M=10000 distinct losses and the inverse-ECDF convention at c=99%, which observation is selected?"}
  options={[{"text": "Ascending rank 9900, which is 101st from the worst", "correct": true}, {"text": "Ascending rank 9900, which is 100th from the worst"}, {"text": "Ascending rank 100, which is 100th from the best"}, {"text": "The maximum loss, independently of confidence"}]}
  explanation={"k=ceil(0.99×10000)=9900. Descending one-based rank is M−k+1=101. There are 100 observations strictly larger."}
/>

For each of $M$ equally weighted simulated losses, positive means losing and negative means gaining. Define the empirical cumulative distribution $\widehat F_M(\ell)$ as the fraction with $L_i\leq\ell$. We use its lower inverse:

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_c=\inf\{\ell:\widehat F_M(\ell)\ge c\}
=L_{(k)},\qquad k=\lceil cM\rceil,
$$

where $L_{(1)}\le\cdots\le L_{(M)}$. This is the inverse-ECDF convention called **type 1** in [R's primary documentation](https://www.stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-patched/library/stats/html/quantile.html). Interpolated estimates, including type 7, are different finite-sample conventions.

For a hand-authored set of 20 simulated teaching losses, the largest three are 140, 200 and 500 USD. At 95%, $k=\lceil0.95(20)\rceil=19$, so VaR is 200 USD: second from the worst. Exactly one observation, 500, is strictly greater; its empirical probability is $1/20=5\%$. At 99%, $k=20$, so the sample VaR is its maximum, 500. Neither statement makes 500 a maximum possible future loss.

For $M=10{,}000$ and $c=99\%$, this convention selects ascending rank 9900, hence **rank 101 from the worst**, with 100 observations strictly above it if all losses are distinct. Calling it the “100th-worst” silently chooses a different boundary convention. With ties, the fraction strictly exceeding VaR can be smaller than $1-c$.

The upper $c$ loss quantile and a negated lower P&L percentile need compatible conventions at discrete boundaries. For our 20 values, the lower inverse 5% P&L quantile is the worst P&L, −500; negating it gives 500, while the defined 95% loss quantile is 200. Keeping signed losses throughout avoids this off-by-one trap.

### When to use it

Record sign, confidence, sample size, scenario weights, rounding and quantile algorithm. Unequal probability weights need a weighted cumulative distribution, not the equal-weight rank formula.

## More draws reduce one kind of uncertainty

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"A new seed changes simulated VaR under unchanged inputs. What does that demonstrate?"}
  options={[{"text": "Finite sampling variability within the fixed model", "correct": true}, {"text": "A necessary change in actual market risk"}, {"text": "A proof that the pricing formula is wrong"}, {"text": "A guarantee the new seed is more accurate"}]}
  explanation={"The model distribution can be unchanged while its finite pseudo-sample changes. A particular new sample need not be closer to the model target."}
/>

Hold the model fixed and generate independent identically distributed (iid) draws. A new random seed changes the sample and may change its VaR. Reusing a seed makes the calculation reproducible; it does not validate the model. Increasing sample count can reduce the distribution of estimation error without moving every realised estimate monotonically toward its target.

Use a model with a known answer to see this. Ten fixed shares start at 100 USD each, with no cash flows. Suppose their signed total loss is uniform on $[-100,100]$ USD. Equivalently, simulate endpoint stock $S_h=100-L/10$, which lies between 90 and 110. The exact model cumulative probability is $F_L(\ell)=(\ell+100)/200$ on its support, so

$$
\mathrm{VaR}_{0.95}=-100+200(0.95)=90\text{ USD}.
$$

For $M=400$, the sample inverse-CDF estimate selects rank $\lceil0.95(400)\rceil=380$. Its value will depend on the draw. The display reports that value, the analytic 90-USD model target and their difference; the target is known only because we invented the distribution.

<MonteCarloQuantileLab client:visible  />

Change seed, sample size and confidence. A stable estimate near 90 means the numerical procedure is resolving this chosen uniform model. It does not show that market losses really have that distribution. The pseudo-random sample is deterministic for its displayed seed, and the table shows selected sorted rows rather than pretending to show every draw.

### When to use it

Use reproducible seeds for debugging and independent replications or justified uncertainty methods to study simulation precision. Separate parameter estimation uncertainty, horizon discretisation, valuation approximation and structural model error from finite sampling error.

## Quantile precision depends on the density in the tail

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question={"Which input is missing from “quantile SE equals a constant divided by √M”?"}
  options={[{"text": "The density at the model quantile, together with sampling and regularity conditions", "correct": true}, {"text": "Only the number of physical days on the desk’s trading calendar"}, {"text": "The current sample mean, which always determines tail precision"}, {"text": "An assumption that every extra draw moves the estimate closer"}]}
  explanation={"The regular iid quantile asymptotic has constant sqrt(c(1−c))/f(q). Sparse tails, atoms and dependence require care."}
/>

The familiar square-root rule has conditions. For iid draws, fixed $0<c<1$, and a continuous positive density $f_L(q_c)$ at a unique smooth model quantile, the large-sample approximation is

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\widehat q_c)\approx
\frac{\sqrt{c(1-c)/M}}{f_L(q_c)}.
$$

A useful intuition comes from counting draws below the true quantile. Their fraction has approximate standard deviation $\sqrt{c(1-c)/M}$. A small loss shift changes cumulative probability by approximately $f_L(q_c)$ times that shift. Dividing converts probability uncertainty to loss units. A sparse tail, with small density, gives larger quantile uncertainty for the same probability noise. [UW's statistical inference notes](https://courses.washington.edu/stat512/506-512Notes7.pdf), section 10.6, state the regular quantile limit and its exceptions.

### The uniform example in money units

For uniform losses on $[-100,100]$, $f_L=1/200=0.005$ **per USD**. With $c=0.95$ and $M=10{,}000$,

$$
\operatorname{SE}\approx
\frac{\sqrt{0.95(0.05)/10000}}{0.005}
=0.43588989\text{ USD}.
$$

At 40,000 draws it is 0.21794495 USD: four times the draws halves this asymptotic standard error under the same conditions. This is not a deterministic absolute-error bound or uncertainty about the next day's loss. For a different smooth model with density 0.0002 per USD at that quantile, the same 10,000 draws give **10.89724736 USD** of approximate standard error.

<QuantilePrecision client:visible  />

Do not apply this formula mechanically at a probability atom, zero density or an extreme confidence level moving toward one as $M$ grows. Dependent draws change the variance calculation; an estimated density adds estimation error. Even a valid approximate confidence interval around the **model's quantile** does not become a guarantee of market coverage.

Variance reduction also depends on the target estimator. Antithetic losses $-100,+100$ have 95% inverse-ECDF quantile +100. Replacing the pair with its average zero gives quantile zero: it changes the distribution rather than estimating the original quantile more efficiently. Tail-probability or weighted importance-sampling methods need correct estimators and uncertainty analysis; averaging scenario losses is not a generic VaR correction.

### When to use it

Use the square-root comparison when its sampling and regularity conditions are credible. For discrete or irregular tails, examine the actual quantile estimator and appropriate finite-sample or replication methods rather than inserting a nonexistent density.

## A precise answer cannot repair missing model support

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="Your loss generator has support [−100,100]. Will a billion additional draws reveal a 500 USD loss?" text="No. That model assigns the loss probability zero. You need an explicit alternative model or stress assumption, not just more draws." />

Return to the uniform loss model. It assigns probability zero to any loss above 100 USD. No extra draws from it can reveal a 500-USD scenario. Now explicitly change the model: with probability 98%, draw a uniform loss on $[-100,100]$; with probability 2%, set loss to 500. This is an invented alternative physical model, not evidence of an actual crash frequency.

Below 100, its cumulative probability is $0.98(\ell+100)/200$. Thus its 95% quantile solves $0.98(\ell+100)/200=0.95$:

$$
q_{0.95}=-100+\frac{200(0.95)}{0.98}
=93.87755102\text{ USD}.
$$

Its cumulative probability reaches only 0.98 at 100 and jumps to one at 500, so **99% VaR is 500 USD**. Under the original uniform model, 99% VaR was only $-100+200(0.99)=98$ USD. More accurate simulation of the original model cannot close that structural gap.

<MonteCarloQuantileLab client:visible model="crash" confidence={0.99} />

A chosen joint distribution matters just as much as each factor's separate distribution. A correlation matrix summarises second moments; it does not by itself determine joint tail dependence or changing crisis behaviour. A heavy-tailed marginal distribution also needs calibration and a joint model. Simulating full paths can accommodate contractual monitoring, but only under the stated path dynamics and resolution.

### When to use it

Compare plausible alternative models, parameter settings and stresses; test realised coverage under an appropriate backtesting design. Keep stress severity separate from probability. Historical simulation assumes a past shock sample is relevant to the current book; delta-normal assumes a normal linear approximation; Monte Carlo uses a chosen joint generator and valuation scheme. Each can be useful within its scope. None acquires correct tails merely from its method name.
