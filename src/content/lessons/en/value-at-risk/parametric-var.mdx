Two analysts model the same portfolio. One works with percentage weights; the other uses dollar exposures. Their VaRs should agree. If one multiplies by the portfolio value twice, the spreadsheet can look precise while its units have gone completely astray.

This lesson uses **normal parametric VaR**, also called the variance–covariance method: choose a normal distribution for linear portfolio P&L, estimate its mean and covariance inputs, then calculate a loss quantile. Other parametric models can use other distributions; normality is the particular choice examined here.

## A normal model supplies a quantile template

A bell-curve template is like a standard-size cutting pattern: location and scale let you place it, but the template does not acquire a different shape just because the data need one. In the normal model, the mean $\mu$ sets the centre and the standard deviation $\sigma$ sets the spread.

For a normal **return** $R$, its quantile at cumulative probability $p$ is:

$$q_p(R)=\mu+\sigma z_p,\qquad z_p=\Phi^{-1}(p).$$

Here $\Phi$ is the cumulative standard-normal distribution. The quantile $q_p$ leaves probability $p$ below it. Keep that probability separate from the VaR **confidence** $c$: a high-confidence loss quantile uses the low return quantile at $p=1-c$.

### Locate a return threshold

For an invented one-day normal return with mean zero and standard deviation 2%, the return's fifth percentile is:

$$q_{0.05}(R)=0+0.02(-1.645)=-0.0329.$$

That is a return of **−3.29%**. Under this model, 5% of returns are lower and 95% are higher. It is a percentile of the specified distribution, not a maximum possible loss.

The chart uses a separate normal example with **0.05% daily mean** and **2% daily standard deviation**. Move confidence to 95%: its loss VaR is about 1.645 × 2% − 0.05% = **3.24%**, slightly below the zero-mean example's 3.29%. The shaded tail contains outcomes beyond the return threshold. **Expected shortfall** is their average loss, not the shaded probability area itself.

### When to use the template

It gives a quick, interpretable calculation when a normal approximation to the relevant P&L is adequate. Estimate and refresh the inputs from suitable data; accepting a distribution does not remove estimation error or the need to check later observations. Covariances describe joint dispersion, and the mean is also needed unless you explicitly set it to zero.

## Turn a return quantile into a loss quantile

Let $V>0$ be the initial portfolio value. Dollar P&L is $VR$ and dollar **loss** is $L=-VR$. For a continuous normal return and confidence $c>0.5$:

$$\mathrm{VaR}_c(L)=-Vq_{1-c}(R)=-V\left(\mu+\sigma z_{1-c}\right).$$

Since $1-c<0.5$, the quantile $z_{1-c}$ is negative. The same expression can be written:

$$\mathrm{VaR}_c(L)=\left(|z_{1-c}|\sigma-\mu\right)V.$$

| Confidence $c$ | Return-tail probability $1-c$ | Lower-tail $z_{1-c}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 90% | 10% | −1.282 |
| 95% | 5% | −1.645 |
| 97.5% | 2.5% | −1.960 |
| 99% | 1% | −2.326 |
| 99.5% | 0.5% | −2.576 |

### Work through a dollar example

For an invented \$10M portfolio, daily return standard deviation is 2% and the mean is set to zero:

- At 95%, $\mathrm{VaR}=1.645(0.02)(10000000)=329000$, or **\$329,000**.
- At 99%, $\mathrm{VaR}=2.326(0.02)(10000000)=465200$, or **\$465,200**.

At 95%, the model assigns 5% probability to a loss exceeding \$329,000. It says nothing here about how large those exceedances become. Increasing confidence moves farther into the loss tail of this fixed distribution.

Setting the mean to zero is an approximation when expected P&L is small relative to the tail term. It is not a rule that every one-day model has zero mean.

### A loss quantile can be negative

Suppose a different illustrative one-period model has mean return 2%, standard deviation 1%, and portfolio value \$1M. Its 95% loss quantile is:

$$\left(1.645(0.01)-0.02\right)(1000000)=-3550.$$

A **−\$3,550 loss** means a \$3,550 gain. This model assigns 95% probability to a gain of at least that amount; the remaining outcomes can include losses. It does not guarantee a profit. A report may choose to show $\max(0,\mathrm{VaR})$, but that is an explicit reporting floor, different from the unfloored loss quantile.

### When to use the sign convention

State whether you report a return threshold, a dollar P&L threshold or a loss threshold. Keep that convention consistent across models and documents. With zero mean, the usual positive VaR is the absolute normal multiplier times dollar volatility.

## Scaling across time with stated assumptions

Adding independent daily shocks is like adding random steps: variance adds, while standard deviation grows with the square root of the number of steps. This is a rule for the dispersion of a **sum**, not a universal rule for any loss percentile.

Let each day's dollar P&L have mean $m$ and standard deviation $s$. Assume independent, identically distributed **normal additive P&Ls**, unchanged exposures and no trading. For $h$ days, the mean is $hm$ and the standard deviation is $s\sqrt{h}$. Therefore:

$$\mathrm{VaR}_c(h)=|z_{1-c}|s\sqrt{h}-hm.$$

If $m=0$, this reduces to $\mathrm{VaR}_c(h)=\sqrt{h}\mathrm{VaR}_c(1)$. A one-day 95% VaR of \$329,000 becomes approximately $329000\sqrt{10}$, or **\$1.040M**, over ten days under those assumptions.

### What changes when the mean is nonzero

Suppose daily expected P&L is **\$1,000** and daily standard deviation is **\$10,000**. At 95%, one-day VaR is $1.645(10000)-1000=15450$, or **\$15,450**. Over four days it is $1.645(10000)\sqrt{4}-4(1000)=28900$, or **\$28,900**. Doubling the one-day VaR would give \$30,900 and miss the mean's different scaling.

### When to use it

Square-root scaling is a model shortcut for suitable additive P&L. Independent finite-variance shocks have additive variances even without normality, but their quantiles need not scale with the same square root. Compounded simple returns, changing positions, volatility clustering and jumps need further modelling.

For stationary additive P&L with lag-$k$ autocovariance $\gamma_k$:

$$\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{h}X_t\right)=hs^2+2\sum_{k=1}^{h-1}(h-k)\gamma_k.$$

Positive covariance terms increase the variance of the sum relative to $hs^2$; negative terms reduce it. This explains a variance change, while the VaR conclusion also depends on the tail distribution. Labels such as “trending” or “mean reverting” alone do not determine a loss quantile.

## Portfolio VaR with consistent units

Two positions of \$5M each are not weights of five million: each is **50% of a \$10M portfolio**. You can calculate risk using either amounts or fractions, but the output unit changes with the input. Think of kilometres and metres: both measure the trip, and converting twice sends you somewhere else entirely.

For the VaR calculations below, assume jointly normal asset returns, fixed linear exposures, zero mean and a confidence level above 50%. The covariance volatility calculation itself only needs finite second moments; its conversion to a normal quantile needs the distributional assumption.

Let $x_i$ denote the dollar exposure to asset $i$, and let $w_i=x_i/V$ denote its fraction of the portfolio value $V$. The asset volatilities $\sigma_i$ are daily returns expressed as decimals. Correlation $\rho$ measures their linear co-movement.

### Route one uses fractional weights

The daily **return volatility** is dimensionless:

$$
\sigma_{R,p}=\sqrt{w_1^2\sigma_1^2+w_2^2\sigma_2^2+2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}.
$$

With mean return set to zero, dollar VaR is:

$$
\mathrm{VaR}_c=|z_{1-c}|\,\sigma_{R,p}\,V.
$$

For two equal long positions, $w_1=w_2=0.5$, daily volatilities 2% and 3%, and correlation 0.3:

$$
\sigma_{R,p}^2=0.5^2(0.02)^2+0.5^2(0.03)^2+2(0.5)(0.5)(0.3)(0.02)(0.03)=0.000415.
$$

Take the square root: $\sigma_{R,p}\approx0.0203715$, or **2.03715% per day**. At 95% confidence, multiply by 1.645 and the \$10M value: the result is approximately **\$335,112**.

### Route two uses dollar exposures

Alternatively, calculate the **P&L volatility** in dollars directly:

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{PnL}}=\sqrt{x_1^2\sigma_1^2+x_2^2\sigma_2^2+2x_1x_2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2},\qquad
\mathrm{VaR}_c=|z_{1-c}|\,\sigma_{\mathrm{PnL}}.
$$

Work in thousands of dollars. The individual P&L volatilities are $5000(0.02)=100$ and $5000(0.03)=150$. Portfolio variance is $100^2+150^2+2(0.3)(100)(150)=41500$ in squared thousands. Its square root is **203.715 thousand dollars**. Multiply by 1.645 to obtain the same **\$335,112** VaR. Multiplying by the portfolio value again would produce squared-dollar units, exposing the mistake.

| Input representation | Volatility output | Convert to dollar VaR, zero mean |
|---|---|---|
| Fractional weights | Return fraction, about 0.0203715 | Multiply by 1.645 and \$10M |
| Dollar exposures | Dollar P&L, about \$203,715 | Multiply by 1.645 only |

### When to use it

Use weights when comparing portfolios independently of size; use exposures when aggregating positions in one reporting currency. The covariance matrix must use compatible return units and the same horizon. For these two positive long positions, reducing correlation below one lowers VaR relative to the sum of standalone normal VaRs: \$164,500 + \$246,750 = **\$411,250**. With short positions, the signs of the exposures also enter the covariance term.

The normal formula is useful because it is quick to calculate and easy to inspect. You can see how the estimate responds to a different exposure, mean, volatility or correlation. The required estimates still need adequate data, and a compact formula does not make their uncertainty disappear.

### Check tails separately from payoff curvature

**Distributional risk:** a normal model can assign too little probability to large losses when the actual P&L has heavier loss tails. The error depends on the chosen quantile, parameters and actual distribution; normal VaR is not necessarily an underestimate at every confidence level.

**Valuation risk:** delta-normal VaR approximates an option portfolio's P&L by a linear response to risk-factor changes. **Delta** measures the first-order response; **gamma** describes how that response changes. A portfolio with material gamma can depart substantially from its linear approximation on a large move.

For an illustrative local expansion, hold time and other factors fixed. Suppose delta is 10 shares, gamma is 4 shares per dollar, and the underlying moves by \$2. The delta-only estimate is $10(2)=20$ dollars. The quadratic correction is $\tfrac12(4)(2^2)=8$ dollars, making the local second-order estimate **\$28**. Full repricing can differ further; this example measures approximation error, not a VaR.

### When to use another calculation

Use full repricing for material nonlinear exposures, and examine distribution and dependence assumptions when tails matter. Historical simulation is limited by its scenario set. Monte Carlo can incorporate jumps or changing volatility **if those dynamics are included and calibrated**; generating more scenarios from the wrong model does not repair it. Backtesting and stress scenarios help examine different weaknesses, while VaR itself still does not measure loss severity beyond its threshold.
