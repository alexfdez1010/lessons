A risk report says “average loss beyond VaR”. Before accepting its Expected Shortfall number, ask how much probability it averaged. At a discrete loss boundary, “all losses at least VaR” can include twice the intended tail.

We keep one convention throughout: **L is signed loss**, positive for a loss and negative for a gain; larger L is worse. All worked scenarios are invented, use one-day losses in the same currency and describe a fixed portfolio. Probabilities belong to the stated distribution, rather than guaranteeing tomorrow’s frequency.

Let $F_L(x)=\Pr(L\le x)$ be the loss distribution function and let $0<c<1$ be the confidence level. Define its **lower inverse quantile** and VaR by

$$q_u=\inf\{x:F_L(x)\ge u\},\qquad\operatorname{VaR}_c=q_c$$

The probability **strictly** beyond this boundary is at most the intended tail probability:

$$\Pr(L>q_c)\le 1-c\le\Pr(L\ge q_c)$$

Equality need not hold when the distribution has an **atom**, a positive probability at one exact loss value. A finite scenario distribution always has atoms. A continuously distributed loss has no atom at its quantile; with the usual continuous quantile calibration, its upper-tail probability is exactly 1−c.

**Expected Shortfall (ES)**, also called CVaR here, averages exactly the worst 1−c probability. For an integrable loss distribution,

$$\operatorname{ES}_c=\frac 1{1-c}\int_c^1 q_u\,du$$

Think of a queue sorted from largest loss to smallest. ES takes exactly the required fraction of the queue. If the last group is too large, it takes only the needed fraction of that group, rather than silently enlarging the queue. [Acerbi and Tasche’s original treatment distinguishes the robust definition from conditional-tail variants at discontinuities](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0104295).

### The same VaR can hide different severity

Take probabilities 90%, 8%, 2% at losses 0, 50, 100. At 95%, VaR is 50: cumulative probability at 0 is 90%, and at 50 is 98%. The strict breach probability is 2%, not 5%. Changing only the loss in the final 2% from 100 to 200 leaves VaR at 50 but raises ES from 70 to 110. We derive those averages next.

### When to use it

Always state confidence, horizon, loss sign, currency and distribution. ES≥VaR at the **same confidence** and for the same law; neither is a worst-case ceiling. ES is an average of specified tail outcomes, not the maximum loss and not a probability that each future day will satisfy.

## At an atom, include exactly the missing boundary mass

Write q for VaR and $p_>=\Pr(L>q)$. The required probability taken at q is $b=1-c-p_>$. The quantile inequalities ensure $0\le b\le\Pr(L=q)$. An exact equivalent formula is

$$\operatorname{ES}_c=\frac{E[L\mathbf 1_{\{L>q\}}]+q\bigl(1-c-\Pr(L>q)\bigr)}{1-c}$$

The indicator $\mathbf 1$ selects strict-breach outcomes. The second term fills the remaining probability using the boundary value. It does not pretend the boundary loss has become a different loss.

For our 0/50/100 law at c=95%, q=50, strict mass is 0.02 and required tail mass is 0.05. Therefore b=0.03. Only 3 percentage points of the 8% mass at 50 enter ES:

$$\operatorname{ES}_{0.95}=\frac{0.02(100)+0.03(50)}{0.05}=\frac{3.5}{0.05}=70$$

### When to use it

Use the correction for default/no-default payoffs, finite simulations, rounded data and any other discrete distribution. If q is the largest loss, strict-breach probability can be zero: the strict conditional mean is undefined, but ES still exists for this finite law and takes some of the maximum-loss atom.

For N equally weighted scenarios, each has probability 1/N. Keep the convention from [historical simulation](/value-at-risk/historical-simulation-var): ascending VaR rank $k=\lceil cN\rceil$, so its descending rank is $N-k+1$. A type 7 interpolated quantile is a different estimator; do not insert it into this inverse-ECDF calculation as though it were the same boundary.

Sort losses $x_1\ge x_2\ge\cdots\ge x_N$. Set $m=N(1-c)$, $j=\lfloor m\rfloor$ and $f=m-j$. The exact empirical tail mean is

$$\widehat{\operatorname{ES}}_c=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{j}x_i+f x_{j+1}}m$$

When f=0, omit the last term. When $m<1$, use a fraction of the worst scenario and divide by that same fraction; ES equals the observed maximum. Ties can share boundary allocation without changing the value.

### An integer tail does not make VaR its last included observation

In 100 equally weighted scenarios, suppose the six largest losses on a EUR 10 million portfolio are 600,000; 520,000; 480,000; 410,000; 390,000; 250,000 EUR, and the other 94 are zero. At 95%, k=95 ascending and the descending rank is **six**. Thus VaR is EUR 250,000. The upper 5% contains the five strictly larger losses:

$$\widehat{\operatorname{ES}}_{0.95}=\frac{600000+520000+480000+410000+390000}{5}=480000\ \mathrm{EUR}$$

The fifth-worst EUR 390,000 is not this convention’s VaR. The visual groups the 94 identical zeros into one 94% atom without removing those scenarios.

### A fractional tail needs fractional arithmetic

For 99 equally weighted scenarios at 97%, m=99(0.03)=2.97. If the largest losses are 7%, 5%, 3%, then

$$\widehat{\operatorname{ES}}_{0.97}=\frac{7+5+0.97(3)}{2.97}\%=\frac{14.91}{2.97}\%\approx 5.020202\%$$

On EUR 10 million, that is about EUR 502,020.20. Averaging three complete scenarios gives 5%, an approximation to a different 3/99 probability, not the exact 3% target. Here k=97, descending rank three, so the inverse-ECDF VaR is 3% while only part of that boundary scenario enters ES.

### When to use it

Audit probability weights as well as amounts. Finite empirical ES cannot exceed the observed maximum unless you change or extend the distribution. More simulated paths can improve numerical resolution within a model; they cannot create evidence that its missing tail assumptions are correct. Floating-point implementations need a documented boundary policy; the historical-simulation viewer retains its documented numerical policy without changing the mathematical definition.

## Coherence compares the combined book with the sum of its parts

On a common probability space and with finite first moments, quantile-integral ES is coherent. In signed-loss notation its four properties are:

- **Monotonicity:** if $L_A\le L_B$ in every scenario, $\rho(L_A)\le\rho(L_B)$.
- **Subadditivity:** $\rho(L_A+L_B)\le\rho(L_A)+\rho(L_B)$.
- **Positive homogeneity:** $\rho(aL)=a\rho(L)$ for $a\ge 0$.
- **Translation:** $\rho(L+a)=\rho(L)+a$ for a deterministic loss a. A cash benefit of a instead changes losses to L−a and lowers the measure by a.

These are properties of the risk functional, not guarantees that every real-world merger, hedge or investment improves outcomes. Subadditivity bounds the combined risk by the **sum** of standalone risks; it does not say the combined ES is no larger than either individual book.

### Enumerate two independent default positions

Each position loses 100 with probability 4% and zero otherwise; ignore coupons and other cash flows. Independence specifies the joint law:

Each standalone VaR at 95% is zero, but ES averages its 4% mass at 100 and an additional 1% at zero: $100(0.04)/0.05=80$. For the combined book, cumulative probability at zero is 92.16%, so VaR is 100. Its 5% ES uses 0.16% at loss 200 and 4.84% of the 7.68% mass at loss 100:

$$\operatorname{ES}_{0.95}(L_A+L_B)=\frac{0.0016(200)+0.0484(100)}{0.05}=103.2$$

VaR violates subadditivity here: $100>0+0$. ES satisfies it: $103.2\le 80+80=160$, yet 103.2 is greater than 80. At half the size of each position, homogeneity gives ES 51.6, below the average standalone ES 80; that comparison holds total nominal position size fixed. Dependence changes the numerical joint distribution, although ES subadditivity does not require independence.

A finite loss law has a finite ES, bounded between its VaR and largest loss. If all scenarios are gains, these signed risk numbers can be negative. For losses −30, −20, −10 with probabilities 20%, 30%, 50%, VaR at 80% and ES at 80% are both −10. The least favorable modeled outcomes still earn 10; zero-flooring would change the measure.

A finite observed maximum is not a proven bound on future loss. For example, a Pareto model with survival $\Pr(L>x)=1/x$ for $x\ge 1$ has 95% VaR 20, while its unbounded ES is infinite because $\int_{20}^{\infty}x^{-1}\,dx$ diverges. Every finite sample from that model still has finite empirical ES. The [advanced tail lesson](/extreme-value-and-tails/expected-shortfall-beyond-var) derives the moment-existence boundary and distinguishes actual contract caps from truncating an estimate.

With 1,000 scenarios and 99% confidence, the empirical tail has ten scenario equivalents. That is not automatically ten independent pieces of tail information: historical dependence, unequal weights and shared simulated factors can matter. For a continuous IID model, the actual number above a fixed calibrated 99% threshold is random with expected count ten; that expectation is not a guarantee of ten observed breaches. Heavy tails and parameter uncertainty can make severity estimates especially unstable.

### When to use it

Report distributional support, scenario weights, calibration uncertainty and stress cases with the ES number. Test sensitivity to a severe scenario and explain whether it was observed, imposed or extrapolated. Compare the same confidence and horizon. Changing the confidence level can change the ordering between different measures.

The Basel market-risk internal models approach includes a one-tailed **97.5% ES** calculation with stress calibration and prescribed liquidity-horizon treatment. It also contains additional capital components and supervisory conditions. This is narrower than saying that all market-risk capital everywhere is simply one empirical ES number. [BIS MAR 33 specifies the internal-model calculation](https://www.bis.org/committees/bcbs/basel-framework/standard/mar/33/inforce/2023-01-01/published/2020-06-05). Applicable implementation dates and local rules require their own jurisdiction-specific check.

For a standard normal loss, $\operatorname{VaR}_{0.99}\approx 2.326348$ and $\operatorname{ES}_{0.975}=\phi(1.959964)/0.025\approx 2.337803$, where φ is the standard normal density. The two standalone standardized statistics are numerically close. This does not prove that two regulatory frameworks produce identical capital for every portfolio.

Nor does same-confidence ES≥VaR order **different** confidence levels. A law with probability 98% at loss zero and 2% at loss 100 has VaR99=100 but ES97.5=$0.02(100)/0.025=80$. Under our selected convention, the remaining 0.5% tail mass comes from loss zero. Do not assume a cross-confidence inequality from an informal label such as “fat-tailed”.

A VaR exceedance indicator only records whether $L>q$. If the model has an atom at q, its correctly specified strict-breach probability can be less than 1−c. A continuous-model exception-count benchmark should not be transferred to a discrete law without this adjustment.

ES validation is not impossible, and a breach count alone does not validate severity. [Fissler, Ziegel and Gneiting explain how joint VaR–ES evaluation supports forecast comparison](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.00244). Formal tests need their own sampling and dependence assumptions; a simple worked diagnostic is not a complete pass/fail procedure.

One exact population identity makes severity visible. Define $(L-q)_+=\max(L-q,0)$, the excess above q, with zero for smaller losses. The boundary-corrected formula implies

$$E[(L-q)_+]=(1-c)(\operatorname{ES}_c-q)$$

For our original q=50, ES95=70 model, the expected excess is $0.05(70-50)=1$. In a 100-outcome tally containing 90 zeros, eight losses of 50 and two losses of 100, the mean excess is $[2(100-50)]/100=1$. Now let those two severe losses be 200 instead: the breach count is still two, but mean excess is $[2(200-50)]/100=3$. The original severity forecast no longer matches that tally. This finite comparison illustrates a signal; it is not by itself a statistically decisive model rejection.

### When to use it

Keep forecasts made before the outcome, use matching realized loss definitions, and assess both occurrence and severity. Separate model comparison, formal calibration tests and stress analysis. A noisy short window can hide a bad model or produce unusual losses under a sound one; neither a quiet breach count nor a coherent formula certifies the future.
