Ten historical scenarios produce ten losses for today’s portfolio. Sorting them is easy. Choosing what “the 95th percentile” means takes one more decision: which quantile convention are we using? With a small sample, different answers can be tens of thousands of euros apart.

We will use **one-day hypothetical profit and loss (P&L)** for a fixed current portfolio, equally weighted scenarios and an explicitly defined inverse empirical distribution. These are model choices. Historical simulation avoids fitting a normal curve; it still needs relevant history, a scenario-construction rule and a valuation model. Every numerical dataset below is invented.

A **scenario** is a specified set of market moves. In historical simulation, each past date supplies a joint move across the risk factors relevant to today’s holdings: share prices, exchange rates, yields and others. Use an appropriate change definition for each factor; not every factor should be shocked as a percentage of its own level.

The process is like trying today’s boat in recorded weather: keep the boat fixed, then replay each day’s wind and waves together. Revalue the current positions after each scenario. For options and other nonlinear instruments, a return-times-value shortcut may be inadequate; the valuation model has to reflect the payoff and relevant risk factors.

### A current-position calculation

Suppose today’s simplified portfolio holds €600 of shares and €400 of bonds. One historical scenario has shares −10% and bonds +2%, with linear total-return exposures:

$$\operatorname{P\&L}=600(-0.10)+400(0.02)=-60+8=-52$$

That is a **€52 loss**. An old all-share €1,000 account would have lost €100 under the same moves, but that is not today’s allocation. Applying the share shock from one date and bond shock from another would create a different joint scenario.

| Item | Current exposure | Replayed return | Hypothetical P&L |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Shares | €600 | −10% | −€60 |
| Bonds | €400 | +2% | +€8 |
| Portfolio | €1,000 | −5.2% | **−€52** |

### When to use it

Keep joint dates aligned and record how missing factors, corporate actions, stale prices and instrument valuation are handled. Joint replay preserves the co-movements **observed in the chosen window**; it does not establish tomorrow’s dependence structure. The [CFA Institute’s current market-risk curriculum describes historical simulation using current holdings and allocation](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/measuring-managing-market-risk).

## State the loss sign and empirical quantile rule

Define **signed loss** as $L=-\operatorname{P\&L}$. A positive number is a loss; a negative number is a gain. We retain that sign rather than automatically flooring every risk estimate at zero.

For $N$ equally weighted scenarios, each has probability $1/N$. The **empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF)** $F_N(\ell)$ is the fraction of signed losses at or below $\ell$. Our VaR convention at confidence $0<c<1$ is

$$q_c=\inf\{\ell:F_N(\ell)\geq c\}$$

Sort losses **ascending**, from smallest loss or largest gain to largest loss, and use the **one-based** index

$$k=\lceil cN\rceil,\qquad\operatorname{VaR}_c=L_{(k)}$$

The ceiling means the smallest integer at least as large as its input. If instead the table runs **worst first**, the rank is

$$j=N-k+1$$

This is the inverse-ECDF convention called **type 1** in the [R statistical documentation, which also describes several other quantile algorithms](https://www.stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-patched/library/stats/html/quantile.html). It uses an observed value and does not interpolate.

### Resolve the integer boundary carefully

| N | Confidence | Ascending k = ceiling(cN) | Rank from worst N−k+1 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 | 95% | 19 | **2** |
| 100 | 95% | 95 | **6** |
| 100 | 99% | 99 | **2** |
| 250 | 99% | 248 | **3** |
| 1,000 | 99% | 990 | **11** |

For 20 distinct losses at 95%, the second-worst loss has 19/20 = **95%** of the sample at or below it, and one observation, **5%**, strictly above it. The worst loss is the maximum and would put 100% at or below it. “Worst 5% contains one scenario” does not make that worst observation the 95% inverse-ECDF boundary.

More generally, this discrete convention satisfies $P_N(L>q_c)\leq1-c$ and $P_N(L\geq q_c)\geq1-c$. The strict exceedance probability need not equal $1-c$, especially with ties. These are statements about the empirical model, not promises about future days.

### When to use it

Write the sign, confidence, horizon, weights, one-based rank and quantile method beside the estimate. A reported percentile is not reproducible until these choices are clear. In code, protect mathematically integer rank products against floating-point representation noise; do not let a value such as 0.9 choose a different boundary merely because its binary representation is approximate.

Use the original €10 million book and these ten one-day scenario outcomes. The rows are ordered by **descending signed loss**, with gains at the bottom.

|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | −3.8% | −€380,000 | €380,000 |
| 2 | −2.5% | −€250,000 | €250,000 |
| 3 | −1.7% | −€170,000 | €170,000 |
| 4 | −0.9% | −€90,000 | €90,000 |

### A different estimator can give a materially different number

A common interpolated estimator, **R type 7**, uses the one-based position $h=1+(N-1)c$. With $a=\lfloor h\rfloor$ and $t=h-a$, its estimate is $(1-t)L_{(a)}+tL_{(a+1)}$, using ascending losses.

For the same ten scenarios at 95%, $h=1+9(0.95)=9.55$. The ninth ascending loss is €250,000 and the tenth is €380,000, so the type 7 estimate is

$$250{,}000+0.55(380{,}000-250{,}000)=321{,}500$$

The difference from inverse-ECDF VaR is **€58,500**. Neither calculation follows from the word “percentile” alone. The type 7 estimate is a separate interpolated sample-quantile estimator; it is not the inverse of this step ECDF. Do not silently mix its boundary with the empirical ES definition below.

### When to use it

Use the method required by the risk specification, and document changes when reconciling systems. Interpolation adds a calculation rule, not new observed tail events. A small sample can make conventions matter substantially; the effect depends on the gaps between observations and the portfolio scale, not a universal “few pennies.”

**Expected Shortfall (ES)** is the mean of the worst $1-c$ probability, with only as much boundary mass as needed. For this empirical distribution:

$$\operatorname{ES}_c=\frac{1}{1-c}\int_c^1 q_u\, du$$

Let the descending losses be $d_1\geq\cdots\geq d_N$, and write $m=N(1-c)=a+f$, where $a=\lfloor m\rfloor$ and $0\leq f<1$. Then

$$\operatorname{ES}_c=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{a}d_i+f\, d_{a+1}}{m}$$

An empty sum is zero. If $f=0$, the boundary term contributes nothing. The fraction is a **probability weight**, not part of an actual historical trading day. The discrete-boundary distinction is developed by [Acerbi and Tasche in their original Expected Shortfall analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0104295).

### Fill a 15% tail

For the ten-scenario table at 85%, inverse-ECDF VaR is the ninth ascending observation, **€250,000**, and $m=10(0.15)=1.5$. The worst scenario contributes 10% probability and half the next scenario contributes 5%:

$$\operatorname{ES}_{0.85}=\frac{0.10(380{,}000)+0.05(250{,}000)}{0.15}\approx336{,}666.6667$$

Equivalently, $(380{,}000+0.5(250{,}000))/1.5$ gives **€336,666.67**. Averaging all losses **strictly greater** than VaR would give €380,000; averaging all losses **at least** VaR would give €315,000. Neither supplies the exact 15% empirical tail.

### Ties still need an explicit boundary allocation

Suppose the third-worst loss is also €250,000, replacing €170,000. At 85%, losses strictly above VaR still carry 10% probability, while the VaR value itself carries 20%. ES needs just **5% from that 20% boundary mass**. Its value remains €336,666.67. Taking every loss at least VaR would instead average €380,000, €250,000 and €250,000: **€293,333.33**. Which tied row supplies the fraction does not change ES, because their loss amounts match.

### When to use it

Use this exact mass allocation when a finite or tied empirical distribution makes “average beyond VaR” ambiguous. With consistent signed losses, empirical ES is at least its inverse-ECDF VaR and at most the sample maximum. **Historical ES does not invent a loss larger than the scenarios either.** It describes observed tail severity; it does not repair missing catastrophe scenarios. The dedicated [ES lesson](/value-at-risk/expected-shortfall-cvar) develops the broader risk-measure properties.

## A small sample has coarse probability resolution

Each of ten equal-weight scenarios carries **10%** probability. At 95% and 99%, the inverse empirical quantile is the same maximum, €380,000. Their ES values also equal that maximum: each requested tail fits inside the worst observation’s mass. Matching numbers at two confidence levels do not establish that the true 5% and 1% tails are identical.

| Scenario count N | Mass per scenario | Upper-tail equivalents at 99% |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 | 10% | 0.1 |
| 100 | 1% | 1 |
| 250 | 0.4% | 2.5 |
| 1,000 | 0.1% | 10 |

These equivalents are bookkeeping for empirical tail probability, not a count of independent catastrophes. Increasing confidence does not create information outside the sample.

### The iid assumption does not arrive with the spreadsheet

**Independent and identically distributed (iid)** means observations are independent draws from the same probability law. Sorting a series does not make it iid. Historical scenarios can have serial dependence, volatility clustering, overlapping horizons and changing regimes. Equal empirical weights describe a chosen model; they do not prove that observations have equal relevance or independent information.

As a separate **hypothetical iid illustration**, suppose a known continuous model has true exceedance probability 1% per day. In 100 independent days, the probability of no exceedances is $0.99^{100}\approx0.3660$, or **36.60%**. A window with no such events is therefore possible even under this idealised model. This calculation is not a confidence interval for a dependent market dataset.

### When to use it

Report window size and tail mass alongside the risk amount. Treat a tiny tail as fragile, review scenario relevance and use uncertainty methods whose dependence assumptions fit the data. Ordinary iid resampling does not automatically handle clustered or overlapping market observations. No finite sample guarantees future coverage merely because its own ECDF has the stated percentile.

## Window changes, weighting and stress scenarios answer different needs

A rolling window replaces an old scenario with a new one. Holding the portfolio and confidence fixed, this changes the empirical distribution. At 90% in the ten-scenario example, replace the €380,000 loss with a zero-loss scenario:

| Fixed-size window | Three largest losses | Inverse-ECDF 90% VaR | Empirical 90% ES |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Before replacement | €380,000; €250,000; €170,000 | €250,000 | €380,000 |
| After replacement | €250,000; €170,000; €90,000 | €170,000 | €250,000 |

VaR falls by **€80,000** and ES by **€130,000**, solely because a scenario leaves the specified sample. This is a concrete **ghost or echo effect**. Not every extreme scenario’s exit changes every quantile; its effect depends on the rank and confidence.

### Choose a window and weights with their trade-offs

A shorter window can respond more quickly but supplies fewer tail observations. A longer one adds data while potentially mixing older regimes; more rows do not guarantee more relevant independent information. Neither change has a universal direction for VaR.

Recency weights give different probabilities to different scenarios. They can reduce the abruptness of a particular window exit, but they are a new weighting model, can concentrate effective information in few dates, and need weighted quantile and ES calculations. They do not ensure correct future probabilities or automatically add unobserved shocks. The equal-$1/N$ formulas and visual in this lesson do **not** implement those variants.

### When to use it

Historical simulation is useful when you can defend the scenario history, current-position mapping and valuation assumptions. Pair its quantiles and tail averages with sensitivity checks and explicit stress scenarios, including severe joint moves outside the selected window. A parametric or Monte Carlo model can explore other outcomes only through its own assumptions; switching engines is not proof that missing risks have been captured.
