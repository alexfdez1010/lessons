Two funds can track the same index while holding different securities and signing different contracts. The index name tells you the target. To understand the route, look at the holdings, replication method and any lending arrangements.

You already know [the distinction between an ETF, a mutual fund and an index strategy](/stock-markets-and-funds/funds-etfs-and-index-funds). Keep those categories separate: **ETF** describes an exchange-traded fund vehicle; **active or index-tracking** describes its strategy; **physical or synthetic replication** describes how a tracker seeks its exposure. An ETF can be actively managed. Neither the wrapper nor the replication label guarantees a particular cost or outcome. [Investor.gov explicitly distinguishes fund vehicles from active and passive strategies](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/characteristics-mutual-funds-exchange-traded-funds).

All amounts below are invented dollars. Returns cover the same stated period and currency and include distributions. Examples isolate the named mechanism; real funds also incur trading costs, expenses, taxes and operational effects. No collateral percentage or fee split here is a universal legal requirement.

## Physical replication means building a portfolio

A recipe lists ingredients and quantities. **Full physical replication** tries to buy all the index constituents in their index weights. **Sampling** holds a selected portfolio intended to represent the index's important characteristics, such as sectors, size, geography or bond interest-rate sensitivity. Sampling is still a physical approach; selecting representative holdings does not automatically make the fund's objective active outperformance.

Here a **weight** is the share of portfolio value allocated to an investment. Over one period with no intermediate flows or rebalancing, a weighted portfolio return is:

$$
R=\sum_j w_jR_j,\qquad \sum_jw_j=1.
$$

Suppose a three-stock index starts with weights 50%, 30% and 20%. Its constituents return +10%, 0% and −5% over the period. Ignore all implementation frictions for this calculation.

Full replication starts \$100 as \$50, \$30 and \$20. The end values are \$55, \$30 and \$19: \$104 in total. Equivalently:

$$
R_{\mathrm{index}}=0.50(10\%)+0.30(0\%)+0.20(-5\%)=4\%.
$$

## Sampling can reduce trades while introducing approximation risk

An intentionally simple sample holds A at 60% and B at 40%, omitting C. It ends with $60(1.10)+40=106$, a 6% return. Its same-period **tracking difference**, fund minus index, is $6\%-4\%=+2$ percentage points. A positive gap in one period does not establish a better replication method; the omitted exposure can help or hurt.

If the next hypothetical period instead has A flat, B flat and C up 20%, the original 50/30/20 target would return $0.20(20\%)=4\%$. A sample beginning that separate period at 60/40/0 would return zero. Its gap is −4 percentage points. These are separate starting-weight scenarios, not a continuous two-period portfolio simulation.

Real sampling is usually more systematic than dropping a stock by eye. It can avoid expensive or illiquid trades in a large index, but the selected exposures can diverge from those omitted. Full replication also has frictions: index changes, trade timing, small positions, cash awaiting investment and dividend treatment can prevent an exact match after costs. Owning the full list does not promise zero tracking difference or tracking error.

### When to use the distinction

Read the replication policy alongside current holdings and the actual total-return history. Check whether the benchmark is price return, gross total return or a specified net total return, and match currency and dates. One-period tracking difference measures a gap; **tracking error** needs a series of comparable gaps to measure variation. Sampling risk cannot be inferred from the number of holdings alone.

## Synthetic exposure uses a contract as well as assets

A **derivative** is a contract whose value depends on something else, such as an index. A **swap** is an agreement to exchange specified cash flows. The **counterparties** are the parties that owe those contractual payments; in this example they are a fund and a bank. The **notional** is the calculation base used for the payments. It is not automatically money lent to the bank or the amount currently owed.

We will model one **unfunded swap** structure: the fund buys and retains an owned **substitute basket** of securities, which can differ from the target index, and contracts to exchange that basket's total return for the index total return. The notional itself is not paid to the bank in this example. This is one model, not a description of every synthetic fund; funded structures and contractual terms can differ. [Invesco's replication explanation shows an owned basket alongside a return-exchange contract](https://www.invesco.com/content/dam/invesco/emea/en/pdf/why-structure-matters-when-choosing-an-ETF-retail.pdf).

### Follow the assets and the cash flows separately

The fund starts with \$100, owns a \$100 basket and enters a zero-initial-value swap on a \$100 notional. Over one settlement period, the basket returns 3% and the specified total-return index returns 8%. The fund owes a separate fixed swap fee of \$0.20 for this invented period. Assume the contract performs, no other cash flows or expenses and no discounting within this end-period illustration.

The return delivered by the simplified arrangement is $107.80/100-1=7.8\%$. It is the index's 8% less a 0.2-percentage-point period fee. We have **not** added the whole index gain to the already-grown basket without paying away the basket return.

For notional $N$, basket return $R_B$, index return $R_I$ and a separate fee $F$:

$$
V_{\mathrm{swap}}=N(R_I-R_B),\qquad
 V_{\mathrm{after}}=N(1+R_B)+V_{\mathrm{swap}}-F=N(1+R_I)-F.
$$

<SwapReturnLedger client:visible title="Owned basket plus the signed swap settlement"
  notional={100} indexReturn={0.08} basketReturn={0.03} fee={0.2} collateral={0} />

Keep the index return fixed and move the basket return. In this frictionless contract-performance model, a larger basket gain is offset by a smaller swap receipt or a swap payment. If the index falls 10% and the basket falls 4%, the swap payment is $100(-0.10+0.04)=-6$: the fund pays the bank \$6. The owned basket is \$96, so the ending value is $96-6-0.20=89.80$. Synthetic exposure still bears the target market's downside.

## Positive claim and notional are different amounts

**Mark-to-market** means valuing an existing position using current market conditions. At the end of our first period, immediately before settlement, the simplified swap has a positive value of \$5 to the fund, with the \$0.20 fee treated as a separate obligation. The fund's current positive swap claim is \$5, not the \$100 notional. Earlier in the period, an actual valuation would also use remaining contractual cash flows, rates and other terms; subtracting realized return legs is our settlement-date simplification.

**Counterparty risk** is the risk that the party owing a payment fails to perform. **Collateral** is cash or securities made available under an agreement to help cover obligations if that happens. It is not earned fee revenue. Suppose \$3 of collateral can legally be realized in full against this particular \$5 claim, with no other recovery, costs, delay or additional margin payment:

$$
E^+=\max(V_{\mathrm{swap}},0),\qquad
 U=\max(E^+-C_{\mathrm{realizable}},0)=\max(5-3,0)=2.
$$

<SwapReturnLedger client:visible title="A current positive claim with partial assumed collateral recovery"
  notional={100} indexReturn={0.08} basketReturn={0.03} fee={0.2} collateral={3} />

The \$2 is an uncovered **current claim in this scenario**, not an estimate of every future possible loss. If the swap value is negative to the fund, its positive receivable on this single contract is zero; the fund may owe money instead. Different netting agreements, future price moves, liquidity needs, collateral valuation and default costs can change exposure. The basket's own market risk remains, too. Do not replace “the notional is not the current claim” with “the structure has little risk.”

### When to use the distinction

Read the actual swap policy, counterparties, reset frequency, fee terms and collateral or other risk protections. A **reset** settles or restarts accrued exposure according to contract terms; it cannot prevent every price jump or settlement failure. Check what assets the fund owns and what return the contract promises separately. Physical replication can also involve lending counterparties, and synthetic arrangements have varied protections; the label alone cannot rank all risks or predict tracking quality.

## Securities lending earns a fee and creates a return obligation

<ThinkReveal client:visible
  prompt="A fund lends securities worth 50 and receives collateral worth 51. Has it earned 1 before a lending fee is paid?"
  hint="What has to happen to the collateral when the borrower returns equivalent securities?"
  text="No. The collateral supports the borrower's obligation; it is not a free gain. On a normal close, equivalent securities come back and collateral is released according to the agreement. Fee income must be calculated separately."
/>

**Securities lending** transfers securities to a borrower under an agreement to return equivalent securities. The borrower can need them for settlement or other transactions, including short selling. The fund remains economically exposed to the securities' price through its right to their return, even while those securities are out on loan. Its rights to substitute distributions, voting and recall depend on the arrangement; lending is not a sale that locks in the original market price.

A **lending agent** arranges and administers loans. The borrower provides collateral and pays the agreed compensation; the agent may receive part of the gross revenue for its services. With noncash collateral, that collateral can be shares or bonds rather than a cash deposit. With cash collateral, reinvestment can add its own investment and liquidity risks, and compensation may include a rebate paid to the borrower. We use a simple noncash-collateral fee arrangement below.

### Trace the loan, collateral and fee on separate lines

The normal process is: the fund delivers securities; the borrower posts collateral; values are monitored and additional collateral can be requested; the borrower pays compensation; equivalent securities are returned and collateral is released. A **margin top-up** is additional collateral requested after values change. It protects only to the extent it is delivered, available and realizable in time.

Our invented fund has a fixed \$10,000 NAV for the fee calculation and lends \$5,000 of securities for 90 days. Assume a 2% annual simple gross lending fee, a 90/360 day fraction, and an agent share of 20% of that gross fee. These are stipulated contract assumptions, not industry-wide rates.

$$
G=5{,}000(0.02)\frac{90}{360}=25,\qquad
 A=25(0.20)=5,\qquad L=25-5=20.
$$

The net fee contribution relative to the assumed fund NAV is $20/10{,}000=0.20\%$, or **20 basis points**. One basis point is 0.01 percentage point. We used the fund NAV as the denominator, not the amount lent and not the collateral balance. This is a fee contribution for 90 days, not a guaranteed annual return. On-loan amounts, demand and fees can change.

The \$5,100 collateral is not added to the \$20 fee. ESMA's [UCITS fee-sharing Q&A](https://www.esma.europa.eu/publications-data/questions-answers/1108) allows normal agent compensation to be deducted from gross revenue and points to annual-report disclosure of revenue and costs. That is the cited UCITS context, not a universal split for all funds. Inspect the actual gross/net figures and agent arrangement.

## Stress the replacement cost, not yesterday's coverage

Now isolate a different, smaller **invented default scenario**, with no fees included: securities initially worth \$50 are lent against \$51 of noncash collateral. Initial coverage is $51/50=102\%$. Then the securities rise 10% and the collateral falls 20%. The borrower defaults before any margin top-up. Assume immediate full realization of the stated collateral value, no other recovery, no indemnity and no transaction or legal costs.

The initial extra \$1 did not fix later coverage. The replacement obligation became more expensive while its protection lost value. The \$14.20 shortfall is measured against the **\$55 equivalent-securities obligation at default**, not against the original \$50 investment. It is not, by itself, the total fund return or a percentage loss on the whole fund.

Set both price changes to zero in the visual. Collateral exceeds the replacement cost by \$1 and the modeled shortfall is zero; that excess still is not lending income. Increase the collateral loss or the loaned security's price rise and inspect the current coverage. This is a conditional stress, not a forecast of default probability. [The issuer's lending explanation identifies this joint movement and borrower default as a loss mechanism](https://www.ishares.com/uk/individual/en/products/297843/ishares-digital-security-ucits-etf-fund).

### When to use the distinction

Read how much is on loan, who borrows, what collateral is accepted, its currency, liquidity, concentration and valuation haircuts, and how recalls and top-ups work. A **haircut** discounts collateral's quoted market value for protection purposes; for example a stipulated 10% haircut on market value 60 recognizes only 54. A market quote is not necessarily immediately realizable cash. A contractual indemnity is compensation promised for specified losses by another party; read its scope and ability to pay rather than assuming complete protection.

ESMA's [ETF and UCITS guidelines](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/2015/11/esma-2014-0011-01-00_en_0.pdf) address collateral liquidity, valuation, issuer credit quality, correlation, diversification and haircut policies in that framework. Our 102% starting ratio is only an example. A stronger starting ratio, daily monitoring or an indemnity can mitigate particular risks; none replaces analysis of what can be recovered after a gap or default.

## Connect implementation to the tracking numbers

Replication choices, trading costs, cash holdings, contract fees and net lending revenue can all affect the fund-minus-index return gap. Avoid counting the same fee twice if it is already included in NAV performance. A small gap does not establish low counterparty risk, and a large collateral balance does not establish high income.

From the earlier fund lesson, annual gaps of −1, +1 and −3 percentage points have mean −1 point and sample tracking error 2 points. Constant annual gaps of −1, −1 and −1 have the same mean but zero sample tracking error. Net lending income might offset part of a cost drag; it does not make every implementation difference constant or remove market and default risk. Compare total returns on matching periods, currency and benchmark conventions before attributing a gap to one mechanism.
