A company offers a new share for 6 when its existing shares trade at 10. That difference is not a guaranteed 4 profit. New shares bring cash into the company and change how many shares divide its value; the subscription right also has value under the model. Follow shares, rights and cash together.

A **rights issue** offers eligible holders rights to subscribe for newly issued securities under stated terms. **Subscribe** means buy the new issue from the company, rather than buy an existing share from another investor. This lesson extends the IPO lesson's share-count dilution to the rights and funding that an existing holder must consider. All figures below are invented euros, with no fees, tax, interest or currency changes. They illustrate mechanics, not whether to participate in any real offer.

## The offer defines the right, the ratio and the deadlines

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An invented offer gives one right per old share and requires four rights plus 6 euros for one new share. What do four old shares entitle their eligible holder to do?" options={[{text:'Use four rights and pay 6 for one new share',correct:true},{text:'Use one right and pay 6 for four new shares'},{text:'Keep four rights and receive one new share free'},{text:'Pay 24 and receive four new shares automatically'}]} explanation="The terms here assign one right per old share and require four for each new share. A right permits subscription; it does not remove the cash payment or create an automatic purchase." />

A **subscription right** is the entitlement defined by the offer to buy specified new shares on its terms. Holding a right is not the same as already holding a new share or having paid for it. The **subscription price** is the amount due per new share. The offer's ratio tells you how many new shares the rights allow you to buy.

Our case explicitly assumes one new share for every four old shares, one right per old share, and four rights needed for one new share. The company has 400 old shares and offers 100 new ones at 6 each. An investor with four old shares receives four rights; full exercise needs 6 cash and would produce one additional share. Another offer might define each right differently, so do not transplant the four-right rule without reading it.

A **transferable** right can be transferred under the offer's rules. In markets using the term, **renounceable** commonly describes rights that can be sold; **non-renounceable** or non-transferable offers restrict that route. Transferability does not guarantee an exchange listing, an active buyer or a sale at the theoretical price. [ASX's glossary distinguishes these rights categories](https://www.asx.com.au/investors/investment-tools-and-resources/glossary). CNMV describes subscription rights as a mechanism that can let existing holders maintain their proportion, rather than a guarantee that every new issue grants every holder such rights. [Read CNMV's definition](https://www.cnmv.es/portal/inversor/glosario?id=0&idlang=1&lang=es&term=Derecho+de+suscripci%C3%B3n+preferente).

### Identify what each date actually determines

The **record date** is a date used in the offer's eligibility process. The **ex-rights date** concerns trading the old shares without the attached entitlement under that market's arrangements. An **exercise deadline** is when valid instructions and payment must reach the appropriate party. Broker processing deadlines can be earlier. Settlement, due-bill arrangements, eligibility restrictions, fractional entitlements and the offer document can alter how these dates work; simply owning shares on one named date is not a universal entitlement rule.

For example, a [2026 issuer prospectus filed with the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1468327/000095010326014832/dp254079_424b3.htm) describes transferable rights and due-bill procedures affecting entitlement, while a [different SEC-filed offer](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1803914/000110465923002094/tm232684d1_424b5.htm) specifies non-transferable rights. These are examples of differing historical offer terms, not current invitations to buy them.

### When to read the terms before calculating

Check the legal issuer, class, ratio, rights allocation, subscription price, eligibility, trading/transfer restrictions, deadlines, payment method and treatment of unused rights. A broker might sell rights automatically under an applicable arrangement; another offer can provide a different treatment or none. Our later **expiry case specifically assumes no automatic sale, compensation or other proceeds**. Do not assume that outcome for every real account.

## Derive TERP from company value plus the cash raised

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="If 400 shares at 10 become 500 shares after the company receives 600, should the model keep the price at 10 and invent another 400 of value?" hint="Divide the old equity value plus new cash by the final share count." text="No. Under a no-other-change model, value becomes 4,000 + 600 = 4,600, divided among 500 shares: 9.2 each. A fixed 10 would imply value 5,000 without identifying the extra 400." />

The **theoretical ex-rights price (TERP)** is a value-conservation benchmark for the share after the right separates, under stated issue assumptions. It is not a forecast or a price an exchange must enforce. Here the issue is fully subscribed, new and old shares have identical economic rights, and the company's value changes **only by the new subscription cash**. We exclude issue costs, tax, other news and any value effect of how the company will use that cash.

Let old share count be $N$, new share count $M$, pre-rights share price $P_0$ and subscription price $S$:

$$
\mathrm{TERP}=\frac{NP_0+MS}{N+M}.
$$

For our full company:

$$
N=400,\quad M=100,\quad NP_0=400\times10=4000,
$$
$$
MS=100\times6=600,\qquad
\mathrm{TERP}=\frac{4000+600}{500}=9.2.
$$

For a one-new-for-four-old block, the same arithmetic is $(4\times10+6)/5=9.2$. The cash raised is an asset of the company in this model, not a second personal dividend for the investor who paid it.

### Calculate one right's theoretical value

Our terms give **one right per old share**. The old cum-rights package at 10 can be viewed, under these same assumptions, as an ex-rights share at 9.2 plus its separated right:

$$
r=P_0-\mathrm{TERP}=10-9.2=0.8.
$$

Four rights plus the subscription payment obtain one new share:

$$
4r+S=4(0.8)+6=9.2.
$$

### When to use a theoretical price

Use TERP to reconcile a specified transaction and understand mechanical effects, then separate those effects from actual prices. A market can price different prospects, costs, risk or incomplete subscription. A right's traded price can differ from this model; if the share becomes unattractive at the subscription price, exercise is not automatically profitable. A transferable label does not guarantee execution at 0.8. The model assumes those trades happen solely to teach the accounting.

## Compare wealth and ownership using the same starting cash

Exercise and sale conserve the same initial budget in this model. Exercise turns the cash into another share; sale leaves more cash. Expiry gives up the model's $4\times0.8=3.2$ rights value. That expiry result depends on our express no-sale/no-compensation assumptions, not an assertion about every broker's default handling.

### Reconcile percentage dilution separately

Initially the holder has $4/400=1\%$ of the company. Exercising gives $5/500=1\%$, maintaining that fraction by contributing the required cash. Selling rights or letting them expire leaves $4/500=0.8\%$.

The decline is **0.2 percentage point**, or a **20% relative reduction** in the original stake: $(1\%-0.8\%)/1\%=20\%$. It is not a 20-percentage-point decline. Meanwhile the company's share count rises 25%, from 400 to 500. Share-count growth and relative ownership dilution use different denominators.

The sale case has lower percentage ownership but unchanged modeled combined wealth. The expiry case has that same lower percentage and lower wealth. A percentage change alone cannot tell you whether the holder was compensated or what happened to their cash budget.

### When to compare choices

Put shares, rights and cash on the same valuation basis, then state funding, costs and the desired exposure. Keeping a percentage stake requires subscribing in the stated proportion or acquiring the necessary equivalent exposure; it does not establish that committing more money is desirable. Choosing a lower stake with cash is a different portfolio position. Market moves, taxes and trading costs can break the equal-value result.

## Funding a partial subscription requires both rights and cash

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="An investor holds 40 old shares and receives 40 rights, but has no spare cash. Can those rights alone pay the 60 needed to subscribe for all ten new shares?" hint="A right permits subscription but is not the subscription payment. Selling some rights sacrifices the corresponding entitlement." text="No. Full exercise requires 40 rights and 60 cash. Under our transferable-rights model the investor can sell some rights to fund a smaller subscription, but cannot sell and use the same rights twice." />

Consider the same company and terms, now with **40 old shares worth 400 and starting cash 0**. Full exercise allows ten new shares but requires $10\times6=60$ cash. The viewer marks that full-exercise plan as **unfunded**; it does not invent a loan or show an unfinanced portfolio as completed.

If $k$ whole new shares are bought, they use $4k$ rights. Selling all remaining $40-4k$ at 0.8 brings cash; funding requires:

$$
0.8(40-4k)\ge6k,
$$
$$
32\ge9.2k,\qquad k\le3.4782608\ldots.
$$

With whole shares and no fractional subscription, the largest feasible $k$ is 3:

The investor ends with 43 shares, not 50, and 4.4 cash. Percentage ownership falls from $40/400=10\%$ to $43/500=8.6\%$, even though theoretical combined wealth stays 400. Self-financing preserves the budget in this particular model; it does not preserve the original percentage or guarantee a return.

### When the funding arithmetic stops being available

This route needs transferable rights, buyers, timely execution and enough net proceeds before subscription payment is due. Non-transferable rights cannot be sold through this assumed route. Fees, tax, rounding, market prices, payment delays and broker rules can reduce what is feasible; the theoretical maximum is not a trading instruction. Real issue proceeds and new-share rights may also differ from our identical-share/full-subscription model. Read whether unused rights expire, generate some proceeds, are sold by an intermediary or follow another process in that offer.
