A buy limit of USD 100.10 can coexist with a cash cost of USD 100.20 per filled share. The execution price respected the limit; a separate commission changed the cash needed. Order tickets, fills and account cash deserve their own columns.

An **order** is an instruction sent through an intermediary, such as a broker, to buy or sell a specified quantity. Its type, eligible session, price conditions, fees and remaining lifetime affect what happens. The worked cases below are invented, use whole shares and USD per share, and exclude taxes, currency conversion and financing unless stated. Each case declares its own book and fee rule. References to US or Spanish investor education support the stated mechanics; availability and contract details belong to the actual broker and venue.

The **best bid** is the highest displayed buying price in the quoted market or feed; the **best ask**, or offer, is its lowest selling price. A quote also has a size and time. The **spread** is ask minus bid. A **last price** records the most recent trade. It is historical information, even when fresh, and may differ from the next execution price.

The shelf label might show a price for three items while you want five. Price and available quantity are separate facts. A displayed quote can change before your order arrives, represent only one accessible venue, or be missing on one side. A halted stock need not have two currently executable quotes.

A percentage also needs its denominator. In a separate bid 4/ask 4.40 example, buying 100 costs 440 and selling returns 400. The loss 40 divided by the **purchase outlay 440** is **9.090909 %**, not 10 %. The latter is 0.40/4, a different quoted-price denominator.

### When to use the quote

Use spread and depth together when judging the cost of trading a quantity. A narrower spread alone does not establish greater available depth or that a large order will finish. A fill can also improve on the displayed quote: for example, a buy at 100.02 is better than an ask 100.05. The [SEC explains price improvement and why routing and elapsed time matter](https://www.sec.gov/investor/pubs/tradexec.htm). Our fixed-book examples deliberately exclude that improvement; they do not claim that every buy must execute at or above the displayed ask.

If the last trade was 20 while the current quote is 19.90/20.10, a one-share fill at 20.10 is consistent with taking the displayed ask. Compare the actual fill and contemporaneous terms; the last print alone neither fixes the fill nor settles its quality.

A **venue** is a market or execution system where orders can be matched. A **market order** requests execution at available prices without a stated security-price limit. It generally seeks prompt execution. Full or immediate execution still depends on acceptance, eligible trading, available counterparties and operating systems. A pause or halt can prevent execution; [FINRA’s trade lifecycle describes partial or absent fills](https://syndication.finra.org/content/how-online-stock-trading-works-understanding-trade-lifecycle). Removing a price limit does not make liquidity or an operating market appear.

A market buy of 500 shares takes 300 at 100.10 and 200 at 100.20:

$$
\begin{aligned}V&=300(100.10)+200(100.20)\\&=30{,}030+20{,}040=50{,}070,\\\bar P&=50{,}070/500=100.14.\end{aligned}
$$

Here V is the security principal,  $q_f$ the filled quantity and $\bar P$ the quantity-weighted average execution price. When no shares fill, there is no average execution price to report. The average is not the midpoint of touched prices. **Slippage** compares execution with a **named reference**. Against the initial best ask 100.10, this case costs 500 × (100.14 − 100.10) = **20 USD**. Against the initial midpoint 100, it costs 70 USD. These are two comparisons of the same fill, not costs to add together. Fees remain separate.

A **limit order** specifies a highest acceptable security execution price for a buy, or a lowest acceptable price for a sell. It can execute at that limit or better. It may fill fully, partially or not at all. The total cash C adds the separately charged fee to the buy principal V. A **marketable limit** can trade against the currently quoted opposing side: a buy limit at or above the ask, or a sell limit at or below the bid. That description concerns the current quote; it does not promise enough quantity at an allowed price when the instruction is processed.

### Retain the cap while allowing a partial fill

With the same book, buy 500 with limit 100.15. Only 300 shares at 100.10 meet the limit; the next ask 100.20 does not. The immediate principal is 300 × 100.10 = **30,030 USD**, with 200 requested shares still unfilled. The [CNMV guide distinguishes this price condition from available counterparty quantity](https://www.cnmv.es/docportal/publicaciones/guias/g05_ordenes_de_valores.pdf). Handling the remaining 200 needs a further instruction, which the next section compares.

A limit also need not be displayed as the first order in a queue. Existing orders, routing, venue priority and other eligibility rules affect where a resting instruction participates. A trade touching its price does not establish that this particular order received a fill.

### Work the fee-inclusive cash rather than renaming it the fill price

Use a **separate**100-share buy, limit 100.10, with 100 shares filled at 100.10 and an invented flat fee 10 charged once if anything fills. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}V&=100(100.10)=10{,}010,\\C&=10{,}010+10=10{,}020,\\C/q_f&=10{,}020/100=100.20.\end{aligned}
$$

The security fills respected 100.10. The all-in cash measure exceeded it because that fee is separate. A sell floor likewise does not establish net proceeds per share after a separate fee. In our model the fee is charged once only when a snapshot has a fill; actual commissions, minimum charges and partial-fill fee bases follow the provider’s terms.

### When to use a price constraint

Use a limit when a worst security price is part of the intended instruction, while accepting its possible nonexecution. Budget fees and other cash requirements separately under their actual definitions. Raising a buy cap can expose more depth; it also permits higher security prices. There is no universal retail-order default that guarantees both acceptable total cash and completion.

A **partial fill** executes only part of the requested quantity. Completed fills are trades; a later cancellation of the remainder does not undo them. What can happen next depends on priority and **time-in-force**, the instruction governing eligible duration and remaining quantity.

### Work a separate same-price queue

Assume a best bid 100.05 with 200 shares already ahead, ask 100.10, and your resting buy 400 at 100.05. In this declared **FIFO** model, first in comes first served at the same price. There are no better competing bids, cancellations or new orders ahead.

If 150 eligible sell shares arrive, all 150 go to earlier orders. Yours fills **zero**, leaving 400 requested shares. In a fresh comparison with 350 incoming sell shares, the first 200 serve the earlier queue and the next 150 fill yours. You obtain 150 and retain 250 unfilled. Knowing that a trade occurred at 100.05 does not by itself identify your fill.

**GTC**, good-til-cancelled, can continue beyond one day, but broker expiry limits, eligible sessions and handling rules differ. Availability of a named combination also depends on the account, product and provider.

### When to inspect the remaining order

Use the confirmed filled quantity, average price, cash and remaining status rather than the original requested amount. Check the actual expiry and eligible session. A cancellation request can race an execution already in progress; confirm its outcome before treating the position as absent. The [CNMV guide describes modification and cancellation timing](https://www.cnmv.es/docportal/publicaciones/guias/g05_ordenes_de_valores.pdf); our FIFO arithmetic is a chosen model, not a universal allocation algorithm.

A **stop-market** commonly becomes a market instruction when its accepted stop condition is met. A **stop-limit** instead becomes a limit instruction with its own price. A sell trigger is usually set below the preceding market level for an existing long position; a buy trigger above it can be used to close an existing short. A long position owns shares. A **short position** owes shares that were sold after borrowing them; buying them back closes that share obligation.

The trigger is a monitoring condition, not an execution price or maximum-loss amount. Some providers use last-sale prices and others quotations; their session and acceptance rules can differ. The [SEC’s order bulletin states those reference differences](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-14), and [FINRA explains the risks of stop conversion in volatile markets](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/stop-orders-factors-consider-during-volatile-markets).

### Compare two monitoring references before any gap

An earlier accepted sell stop has trigger 95. At this update, last sale is 96, bid 94 and ask 94.20. A last-sale rule does not newly activate it, since 96>95. A bid-based rule does, since 94≤95, assuming that monitoring rule is eligible. These are different specified contracts applied to the same observed snapshot.

### Work the gap result under an executable book

Start with 100 shares bought at 100, no fees. The accepted last-sale sell trigger is 95; its monitoring is eligible. The next observed last sale is 88 and an eligible bid offers enough capacity at 88. A stop-market converts and sells 100 there. Proceeds are 8,800; closing P&L is 8,800 − 10,000 = **−1,200 USD**. That is a result of this stated executable book, not an assurance that every gap fills at its opening price.

A stop-limit with trigger 95 and sell limit 94 also activates under this rule. But all shown bids are below 94, so it executes **zero shares**. The 100-share position remains open. At the declared last-sale mark 88 its marked change is −1,200, while liquidation proceeds remain unreceived. No fill has not removed the exposure.

### When to use a conditional instruction

Specify trigger source, monitored session, resulting order type, limit if any, validity and remaining-position risk. Short-lived reference changes can activate a stop; a later recovery does not undo completed fills. A gap, unavailable matching or an unmarketable limit can prevent the intended exit. The discipline supplied by a predeclared instruction is useful only alongside its actual processing terms and position constraints.

### Work a decision with a cash calculation

Suppose the intended instruction is to buy **at most 500 shares**, pay no more than 100.15 per share for the security, accept a partial fill, and leave no order waiting after immediate processing. In our stated menu, a limit IOC fits those conditions. With the invented flat fee 10, the displayed book fills 300 at 100.10:

- Filled principal:300 × 100.10 = 30,030 USD.
- One fee:10 USD; cash paid **30,040 USD**.
- Filled 300, cancelled 200, resting 0. The actual position is 300 shares.

If instead all 500 were essential **at that immediate processing event**, FOK would produce no fill here because only 300 qualify. It charges no fee in our invented no-fill/no-fee schedule. Neither choice creates missing liquidity.

A full-size price-plus-fee cash bound in this example is 500 × 100.15 + 10 = **50,085 USD**, conditional on all 500 filling and exactly that fee rule. It is a funding bound, not a promise that the order will fill. Actual partial-fill cash 30,040 and the resulting 300-share exposure answer different questions from the original maximum budget.

### When to use the comparison

Use the final confirmed fills, fees and remaining instruction to reconcile the account. Keep reference time, available size and session scope with the price comparison. An order type can express a preference or constraint; the resulting trade still depends on market conditions and the provider’s terms. The next lessons use those trades to explain indices, funds and investment costs.
