“Expiry” sounds like one event. A futures contract can instead stop trading, receive its final price, move its last variation payment and complete delivery at different times. Confusing those steps can produce either a spreadsheet gain that was counted twice or a real delivery obligation that was never planned.

We build on the course's margin, carry and basis lessons. Every example below is an **invented contract**, with prices in one currency per underlying unit. A **long** benefits from increases in its quoted futures price; a **short** has the opposite variation. One example contract represents **10 units**, so a one-unit quote change produces 10 of cash. We assume the stated trades and payments perform, and explicitly identify omitted funding or costs. These examples are not a venue's current product specifications.

## Read the contract's calendar before the price chart

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An invented physical contract can allocate delivery from day 2 but remains tradable through day 3. Does selling on day 3 necessarily guarantee that an existing long has avoided delivery?" options={[
 {text:'No; a delivery obligation may already have been allocated',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; trading and delivery eligibility must always end together'},
 {text:'Yes; physical delivery is impossible while a contract is listed'},
]} explanation="The relevant contract and broker deadlines can differ. A later offset cannot simply be assumed to reverse an already allocated delivery obligation." />

A **contract specification** identifies the underlying or settlement reference, quote currency, multiplier, eligible grade and location, delivery instrument, price-fixing method and relevant dates. “Wheat next month” is not a complete description of what someone owes.

Separate the calendar entries:

- **Last trading time:** when ordinary trading in that expiry stops.
- **Final price determination:** the time or observation window that fixes the final settlement reference.
- **First position/notice or delivery-allocation eligibility:** when the applicable delivery process can begin. The names and sequence depend on the contract.
- **Payment and delivery dates:** when cash and the eligible asset or title actually move.
- **Broker deadline:** an account-level cutoff or instruction requirement, which can be earlier than the exchange's dates.

For our invented physical example, entry is day 0; the broker's deadline is the **end of day 1**; delivery allocation may begin on day 2; trading ends at the end of day 3; delivery is day 5. Planning an exit on day 3 because it is “before delivery” ignores two earlier events. Planning on day 1 is inside this example's window, but an unfilled order is still not a closed position. Real calendars also require times, time zones, holidays and account permissions, not just day numbers. [CME's final-settlement overview](https://www.cmegroup.com/market-data/final-settlements.html) distinguishes marking to the final price from the contract-dependent later settlement date.

**Pitfall:** treating a futures delivery allocation as an option that the long can politely decline. The obligation comes from the contract and delivery process. Another common mistake is assuming one product's notice/trading order applies to every other product.

### When the calendar matters

Before entering a maturity that could reach its delivery cycle, including when the intention is only to hedge or speculate. Check the specific contract and account instructions. A price-risk hedge does not organize transport, warehouse eligibility, funding or a broker's delivery permission.

## Make the final variation the last increment, not a second payoff

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A 10-unit long future starts at 100, settles at 96, then 103, then finally 108. Is its last settlement payment 80?" hint="The move from 100 to 103 has already been settled before the final reference arrives." text="The final increment is 10 × (108 − 103) = 50. Earlier payments were −40 and +70. Their cumulative total is 80; paying another 80 at expiry would count earlier changes twice." buttonLabel="Reconcile the three payments" />

A **daily settlement price** supplies the mark for that day's settlement variation. It need not equal the last screen trade: the product has a settlement method. A **final settlement price** supplies the last required mark for the expiring position. It is a price, not an extra full profit cheque.

With constant signed quoted units Q, no intervening position changes and compatible quote/cash units,

$$
VM_i=Q(F_i-F_{i-1}),
\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}VM_i=Q(F_n-F_0).
$$

Q is positive for a long and negative for a short. Our long has Q = 10 and path 100 → 96 → 103 → 108:

$$
VM_1=10(-4)=-40,\quad VM_2=10(7)=70,\quad VM_3=10(5)=50,
\quad \sum VM=80.
$$

The sum telescopes because intermediate quotes cancel. It is a **nominal sum**, not a return percentage or a present value. A changing position needs its actual trades and quantities. Actual exchanges can also round contract money values according to their rules; these invented whole-unit examples avoid that rounding complication. [CME's money-calculation guide](https://www.cmegroup.com/clearing/files/CME-Money-Calculations-Futures-and-Options.pdf) sets out cash variation from successive marks and trades.

Now retain all variation in an invented margin account: initial cash 300, maintenance threshold 200, no internal interest. Before the final close, a balance below 200 requires enough new cash to restore 300; we assume it is supplied. The base path gives balances **260, 330, 380**, so no extra cash is needed. Closing returns 380. Initial deposit −300 plus closing cash +380 is already **+80**. Do not add the variation total again.

The cash calendar matters even when the nominal sum is correct. Suppose an explicitly invented external funding/opportunity factor is **1.02 per settlement interval**, with three intervals and no interest credited inside the account. Value all external margin cash at the final date:

$$
380-300(1.02)^3=61.6376.
$$

The difference from 80 is the 18.3624 final-date financing/opportunity cost of the deposited cash in this scenario. This chosen 2% is a teaching input, not an observed daily rate or an annual quote. If the first quote instead falls to 60, the first balance is −100 and the assumed top-up is **400**, restoring 300. The final account then holds 780; nominal net cash remains 780 − 300 − 400 = 80, but its funded final value is

$$
780-300(1.02)^3-400(1.02)^2=45.4776.
$$

Same endpoints, different cash demand and financing outcome. Top-ups are contributions, not trading gains. If the final closing balance is negative, it is cash **due** at closure; the model does not invent a free loan. Nor does daily settlement cap a gap loss or guarantee that funding will be available.

When reading a broker statement, comparing futures with a forward or checking a hedge's cash needs. Here the external cash also includes margin posted and returned; interest, withdrawal rules and actual financing terms must match the account being modeled.

## Physical delivery requires the full invoice and the eligible asset

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="The same 10-unit long has gained 80 through variation and initially posted 300 margin. Its invented physical contract invoices 108 per unit at delivery. Is the invoice reduced to 780 because 300 was already a down payment?" options={[
 {text:'No; margin was security, and the full invoice is 1,080 under these terms',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; margin always prepays part of the asset purchase'},
 {text:'No invoice is due because the future already made a profit'},
]} explanation="The invoice and margin account are different cash obligations. Reconcile the initial margin, its eventual signed return and the full delivery invoice, together with the asset/title received." />

**Physical settlement** exchanges the specified deliverable asset or eligible title against the contract's invoice. Our toy contract explicitly uses the final settlement quote plus a stated per-unit invoice adjustment a, with payment and final margin closure both at T:

$$
\text{Invoice}=Q(F_n+a).
$$

For this invoice, Q is the positive physical quantity: the long pays the amount and the short receives it. This is the invented rule, not a universal invoice formula. Other products can have different pricing dates, grade/location adjustments, conversion factors or accrued amounts. Inspect the specification rather than infer an invoice from a ticker.

At Q = 10, Fₙ = 108 and a = 0, the full invoice is **1,080**. The long pays it and receives 10 eligible units or the specified delivery title. With zero funding cost in the base cash example:

$$
\underbrace{300}_{\text{initial margin paid}}+
\underbrace{1080}_{\text{invoice paid}}-
\underbrace{380}_{\text{margin cash returned}}=1000.
$$

Equivalently, 1,080 invoice − 80 cumulative variation = 1,000 nominal procurement cash. The quote increase has not made the purchase free: the higher invoice and prior variation must both appear. The short has the mirrored variation and receives the invoice in exchange for delivery.

An invented contractual adjustment a = +2 gives invoice 10 × 110 = **1,100** and nominal cost **1,020**. That extra 20 belongs to the specified delivery adjustment. It is not a second futures loss. Additional storage, transport, load-out, tax or execution charges would need their own rows and dates.

At the external factor 1.02 from the preceding section, final-date funded margin cash is 61.6376. The base final-date procurement cost is therefore **1,080 − 61.6376 = 1,018.3624**, not 1,000. Nominal cancellation is not a present-value equivalence once financing and collateral terms differ. A forward comparison must include that forward's own collateral, credit, payment and funding terms too.

“Physical” also does not promise a truck at your door on the last trading day. For the grain contracts in [CME's delivery explanation](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/courses/introduction-to-grains-and-oilseeds/understanding-the-grain-delivery-process), payment can transfer a warehouse receipt or shipping certificate; subsequent storage, transfer or load-out is another step. Its [receipt/certificate FAQ](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/articles-and-reports/warehouse-receipts-vs-shipping-certificates-frequently-asked-questions) distinguishes ownership of grain in an approved warehouse from a facility's commitment to deliver on request. Delivery and warehouse performance still matter; financial safeguards do not guarantee that every failure will be remedied with the physical grain itself.

### When to use the full delivery budget

When someone actually needs or supplies the asset. Compare the required quantity, grade, location, instrument, invoice and available cash. A contract can hedge a benchmark price while being unsuitable for the firm's own logistics or account permissions.

## Cash settlement follows its reference and delivers no goods

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A cash-settled contract uses the equal average of three spot observations, 80, 100 and 120. Must its final settlement reference equal the last spot price of 120?" options={[
 {text:'No; the specified average is 100',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; every final futures price must equal every last spot print'},
 {text:'No; cash settlement means that no final price is needed'},
]} explanation="The contract specifies which reference is paid. An average of observations at different times is a different object from the final instantaneous spot quote." />

**Cash settlement** completes the derivative's payment using a specified reference J. It does not deliver the physical asset. For an unchanged 10-unit long entered at 95, the cumulative nominal variation ends at

$$
10(J-95).
$$

This total includes the last mark from the previous settlement to J. It is not a second payment added after all daily variation. Our separate invented contract uses equal-weight fixings 80, 100 and 120:

$$
J=(80+100+120)/3=100,
\qquad \text{cumulative variation}=10(100-95)=50.
$$

The last spot is 120; the average is 100. Both can be correct. CME's [cash-versus-physical comparison](https://www.cmegroup.com/articles/2025/cash-settlement-vs-physical-delivery.html) gives examples of contract-specific assessment windows and averaging. Our three numbers and weights are invented, not the methodology of a named lumber contract.

A buyer still needs the goods. Suppose the separate local purchase occurs at the last observation time, at 125: the last spot 120 plus a local premium of 5. Buying 10 units costs **1,250**. Subtract the 50 derivative gain once:

$$
\text{nominal net cost}=1250-50=1200
=10\bigl(95+(125-100)\bigr).
$$

The residual difference is **25 per unit**, not zero: 20 from comparing the final spot with the reference average, plus 5 from the local premium. Matching the last calendar date has not changed the reference into the buyer's local transaction. This calculation excludes financing, fees and any additional gap between payment dates; use the dated cash ledger to compare values at a common date.

**Pitfall:** treating a cash-settled derivative as a purchase order. Another is comparing a cash final reference with an unrelated screen quote and calling any difference an arbitrage. Grade, location, currency, price window and fixing rules identify what is actually paid.

### When the reference deserves its own check

Whenever the business buys a physical product but the hedge settles an index, assessment or average. Calculate the residual using the firm's actual transaction price and date. A customized agreement might reduce particular mismatches, while retaining its own counterparty and delivery risks.

## Convergence belongs to a matched claim, not every price on the screen

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="In a correctly matched frictionless terminal model, the basis finishes at zero. Does that make it a countdown that gets smaller every day?" hint="An endpoint restriction does not determine the path taken to reach it." text="No. The reference-minus-future basis can change sign and widen before ending at zero. A local quote can also retain a premium relative to the contract's own deliverable reference." buttonLabel="Separate the endpoint from the path" />

The course defines **basis = cash price minus the selected futures price**. The cash price must be identified. In a model with identical deliverable asset/title, location, quote units, immediate delivery and feasible frictionless transactions, the terminal delivery-reference comparison supports

$$
F_T=S_T^{\text{matched reference}}.
$$

It does not set a different local grade, warehouse certificate, shipment date or transaction price equal to that number. Delivery costs and options can also matter: a right to obtain goods later is not necessarily an immediately available local pile of grain.

For a worked path, reference spot is **95, 101, 100, 108** and futures is **100, 96, 103, 108**. Reference-minus-future basis is **−5, +5, −3, 0**. It changes sign twice and does not shrink monotonically. Add a local premium of 5 to each reference spot; local basis becomes **0, +10, +2, +5**. The model's matched terminal reference converges while the local terminal basis remains +5. This is the distinction developed in the [basis-risk lesson](/futures-and-forwards/basis-risk-and-hedge-ratios), with no promise that a fitted hedge ratio removes it.

<SettlementDeliveryLab client:visible view="convergence"
  title="Separate reference basis from local basis"

For a cash-settled contract, its final mark is the prescribed reference **by the settlement rule**. That reference can have a different observation clock from a familiar spot display. For example, [CME's equity final-settlement procedure](https://www.cmegroup.com/trading/equity-index/settlement.html) uses a special opening quotation for specified quarterly index products, assembled from component opening prices. Those component openings are not necessarily simultaneous, so the reference need not equal the index screen's first or last print. This is an example of why one must read the methodology, not a universal expiry procedure for all index futures.

### When a convergence claim needs qualification

Ask: convergence to **which claim**, at **which location and time**, under **which feasible delivery or settlement mechanism**? A nonzero difference may indicate basis or reference mismatch, transaction costs, delivery constraints or a problem worth investigating. It is not automatically a freely executable profit.

## Close, roll and settle as different operations

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="You close an old future at 104 and open the next maturity at 112, each for 10 units. Does the quoted gap automatically require paying 80 of purchase principal?" hint="A futures quote is not a full notional asset purchase price paid when opening the contract." text="No. The roll changes the contract held. Real cash includes execution fees, settlement variation and margin/funding effects. The 80 quote-notional difference is not by itself another cash payment or trading loss." buttonLabel="Follow the positions actually held" />

An **offset** takes the opposite position in the same contract and expiry, under the applicable clearing/account rules. Verify the resulting position and whether any delivery obligation has already been allocated. Selling a different expiry leaves two maturity positions; it does not necessarily close the old one. A private forward offset with another counterparty also does not by itself cancel the original legal obligation.

A **roll** combines closing one maturity with opening another. Our separate example assumes all stated executions succeed before the relevant deadlines. One 10-unit old contract is held from 100 to 104; the new contract is then held from 112 to 110:

$$
\text{old settlement}=10(104-100)=40,
\qquad \text{new settlement}=10(110-112)=-20.
$$

Combined nominal settlement is **20**. Charge an invented 2 for **each of four executions**: old entry, old exit, new entry and new exit. Total fees are 8 and net nominal result is **12**, before margin interest and financing. The roll quote gap 10(112 − 104) = 80 is not another principal debit. Nor is the old position's cumulative gain paid again on top of variation already received. The [curve/carry lessons](/futures-and-forwards/contango-and-backwardation) retain these same distinctions between a maturity snapshot, the actual held paths and the funded cash result.

  title="Add the held paths and execution fees"

**Pitfall:** reading an expiring position as a harmless chart symbol that will disappear. A roll changes the delivery month and subsequent price risk. It does not purchase the required goods or guarantee that the new reference fits the business's payment date. Orders can remain unfilled, trading can become difficult and account restrictions can precede the exchange deadline.

### When to reconcile the position and the business requirement

After an intended offset or roll, and before the next relevant contractual event. Check the actual cleared/account position, outstanding allocations, remaining maturity, goods still needed and the cash calendar. No single universal “close X days before expiry” rule replaces that contract-specific work.
