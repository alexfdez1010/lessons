A forward looks expensive compared with spot. Is that a trade, or have we forgotten the warehouse bill? The useful answer comes from listing the trades, their dates and the exact asset delivered. A gap between two numbers is the start of the investigation.

**Cost of carry** means the costs and benefits of holding an asset until a later delivery date. For a tradable financial asset, a complete, feasible replica can fix a forward price. For a commodity used in production, storage access and the benefit of keeping inventory can prevent the reverse trade. We will work through both, then follow the extra cash dates created by futures settlement.

## Match the delivery promise and the growth convention

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Two one-year calculations both enter a rate of 6%. One uses continuous compounding and the other an effective annual rate. Must they give the same forward price?" options={[
 {text:'No; the same numeral has different growth factors under those conventions',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; a percentage already contains its compounding convention'},
 {text:'Yes; forward delivery is unaffected by financing'},
]} explanation="Continuous annual r gives exp(rT). Effective annual i gives (1+i)^T under its stated fractional-year convention. Match growth factors before comparing prices." />

A **spot price** is the price of the specified asset for current settlement. A **forward delivery price** F₀ is the price agreed today for an exchange at a later date T that makes a new forward worth zero under the stated pricing assumptions. An existing contract keeps its original delivery price **K**; its current value need not remain zero. Neither F₀ nor K is an upfront purchase of the underlying.

Start with one non-income financial asset. Assume it can be bought, held and delivered intact, and borrowed for a short sale. Borrowing and lending use the same known rate; there are no fees, taxes, credit losses or delivery options. A **short sale** borrows an asset, sells it now and owes the asset back later. Under these ideal conditions,

$$
F_0=S_0e^{rT}=\frac{S_0}{P(0,T)},
$$

where r is an **annual continuously compounded financing rate**, T uses years, and P(0,T) is the price today of one certain currency unit delivered at T. A known discount factor can replace the constant-rate assumption.

For spot 100 and one year, **6% effective** gives $100(1.06)=106$. **6% continuous** instead gives $100e^{0.06}=106.18365465$. Converting the effective rate gives $r=\ln(1.06)=0.0582689081$, so $100e^r=106$. The formulas agree when their economic assumptions agree. Entering the same 6% into both is not that conversion.

The distinction between price and value is equally concrete. If the fair new delivery price is 106 but an existing long forward promises to pay K = 100, that old promise is valuable. In the same non-income setup its value today is $(106-100)/1.06=5.66037736$, rather than zero. It pays the six-unit difference at delivery, discounted back to today. [CFA Institute separates forward delivery prices from contract values](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/pricing-valuation-forward-contracts-underlying-varying-maturities).

**Pitfall:** mixing an advertised annual loan rate, an effective deposit yield, a quoted bond yield and a continuous rate without conversion. Also match currency, delivery date, grade and location. A different deliverable is a different payoff, however similar the names look.

### When to use this relationship

To benchmark a new forward or value a matched existing contract. The formula needs a feasible delivery replica and a financing convention. A positive rate raises the non-income forward above spot in this model; a negative rate can put it below spot without a convenience service.

## Close both carry budgets before calling a gap an arbitrage

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A one-year financial forward sells for 110 while the frictionless replication cost at delivery is 106. Which pair of positions closes the asset obligation?" options={[
 {text:'Buy and finance one deliverable unit, then sell that unit forward',correct:true},
 {text:'Sell both the asset and the forward, leaving two delivery obligations'},
 {text:'Buy the forward and ignore the source of the delivery payment'},
]} explanation="An arbitrage budget must close its cash and asset obligations. Financing the purchased unit and delivering it against the short forward fixes the terminal sale receipt in the stated ideal model." />

Return to spot 100 and **6% effective over the year**. At a forward sale price 110, **cash-and-carry** means borrowing 100, buying one unit and selling that unit forward. There is no initial net cash. At delivery, the unit is handed over for 110 and the loan repayment is 106. The terminal net is $110-106=4$, with no asset left or owed.

The reverse direction uses a different set of permissions. At a forward purchase price 103, borrow and short-sell one asset for 100, invest the proceeds to receive 106, and buy the unit through the forward for 103. Return that unit to the stock lender. Terminal cash is $106-103=3$ and the asset obligation is closed. This assumes the sale proceeds can be invested, the asset loan lasts until delivery, and there is no unbudgeted stock-borrow charge or replacement payment.

Those exact equalities need both directions to be available. Real **bid/ask quotes** are executable selling/buying prices, respectively. Funding rates and costs can give a **band**, not one executable midpoint. Consider this fully hypothetical one-year setup:

- Spot bid 99, spot ask 101; buying costs 101 and short-selling receives 99.
- Continuous annual borrowing rate 6%, lending rate 4%.
- Each strategy has a known trading cost of 1 paid at delivery. A short asset also owes a known borrowing charge of 2 at delivery.
- The asset, financing and delivery are available on those exact terms; dividends are absent.

The cash-and-carry upper bound is

$$
U=101e^{0.06}+1=108.24549120.
$$

The reverse-carry lower bound is

$$
L=99e^{0.04}-2-1=100.04026665.
$$

No positive carry surplus requires **forward bid ≤ U** and **forward ask ≥ L** under these assumptions. At forward bid 110, the financed purchase and trading cost leave $110-108.24549120=1.75450880$ at delivery. At forward ask 111, the reverse budget gives $100.04026665-111=-10.95973335$; that direction is unattractive. At bid 104/ask 105, both modeled surpluses are negative.

These budgets exclude credit/default, taxes and any costs not explicitly modeled." />

**Pitfall:** selling a rich midpoint that nobody bids, investing restricted short-sale proceeds, or omitting collateral, recalls, taxes and financing capacity. An asset-price hedge does not fund every intermediate cash requirement. A theoretical violation is an executable arbitrage only if the complete required trades actually exist. [Ritchken's university teaching notes](https://faculty.weatherhead.case.edu/phr/textbook/powerpoint/notes2.pdf) derive the two carry directions and the role of their trading assumptions.

### When the band is useful

When comparing an actual forward quote with financing and asset-loan offers. Check the side of each trade, date each charge and verify borrowing access. A quote inside a band does not need to equal a frictionless midpoint; supply, demand and other constraints can matter there.

## Date a cash dividend; reinvest a proportional yield

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A share will pay a known cash dividend of 6 halfway through a year. Is it always correct to subtract exactly 6 from the no-dividend delivery price at year-end?" options={[
 {text:'No; the dividend’s funding benefit runs from its payment date to delivery',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; dates never change the future value of cash'},
 {text:'Yes; 6 currency units and a continuous yield of 6% are identical inputs'},
]} explanation="Money received before delivery can reduce a loan or be invested over the remaining interval. A dated monetary dividend and a proportional yield require different replicas." />

A **cash dividend** is a monetary payment per share. If its amount D and payment time t are known, and the spot buyer receives it before delivery, its present value is $De^{-rt}$. For several dated dividends Dᵢ,

$$
F_0=\left(S_0-\sum_iD_ie^{-rt_i}\right)e^{rT}
=S_0e^{rT}-\sum_iD_ie^{r(T-t_i)}.
$$

Use S₀ = 200, r = 5% **continuous**, T = 1 and a known D = 6 paid at t = 0.5. Then

$$
\operatorname{PV}(D)=6e^{-0.025}=5.85185947,
$$

$$
F_0=200e^{0.05}-6e^{0.025}=210.25421928-6.15189072=204.10232855.
$$

Follow the loan to see why. Borrow 200 and buy one share. At half a year the loan has grown to $200e^{0.025}=205.06302410$. Apply the dividend 6 to it, leaving **199.06302410**. That amount grows for another half-year to **204.10232855**. Delivery receipts at that price repay the loan. A later dividend leaves less time to reduce financing; an earlier dividend reduces more of the terminal debt when r is positive. A payment at the delivery date is included only if the spot holder receives it **before** handing over the share.

A **continuous proportional dividend yield q** describes a different model: shares pay a constant proportion of their current value continuously, with dividends reinvested. Buy $e^{-qT}$ shares initially and reinvest until the position grows to one share. Financing this starting quantity gives

$$
F_0=S_0e^{(r-q)T}.
$$

At the same S₀ = 200, r = 5%, T = 1 but **q = 3% continuous**, the starting quantity is $e^{-0.03}=0.9704455335$ shares. Their purchase costs **194.08910671**, whose financed delivery cost is $200e^{0.02}=204.04026801$. This is close to the dated six-unit example, but it is a different cash/share process and a different price. [CME's equity-index basis explanation](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/courses/introduction-to-equity-index-products/what-is-equity-index-basis) identifies dividend income and financing as separate ownership-versus-futures terms.

<CarryReplicationLab client:visible view="dividends" ledgerHeaders={["Loan stage", "Time, years", "Cash applied to loan", "Loan outstanding"]} tableLabel="Dated loan balances after applying the known cash dividend" chartLabel="Forward prices under distinct dated-cash and proportional-income replicas" title="Compare a dated dividend with a separate proportional-yield model" unitsLabel="Spot 200, one year and continuous annual financing 5%. The known cash dividend is applied to the loan at its selected time. The separate q model reinvests proportional dividends to finish with one share. Endpoint cash payments occur just after acquisition or just before delivery, while the spot holder is entitled to them." caption="Initial cash dividend 6 at half a year gives forward 204.102329. Separate continuous q = 3% gives 204.040268 and initial reinvesting quantity 0.970446 shares. The table traces the cash-dividend loan before its final repayment; the q readout belongs to the distinct share-reinvestment replica. Do not collect the same dividend in cash and reinvest it twice." />

**Pitfall:** treating uncertain future dividends as a guaranteed known-cash hedge. A dividend estimate creates a conditional benchmark, with dividend risk left over. Nor does an announced discrete payment automatically equal a constant q. Short asset positions must compensate the lender according to the actual dividend and stock-loan terms.

### When to choose each representation

Use dated cash flows when the assumed monetary payments and entitlement dates are explicit. Use proportional q for that stated continuous reinvestment model. Keep the amount, timing, currency and uncertainty visible; use the same dividend assumptions on both sides of a comparison.

## Finance storage invoices from their actual dates

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A storage invoice of 3 is paid halfway through a year. With positive financing rates, which invoice has the higher delivery-date financing cost: the same 3 paid today or halfway through the year?" options={[
 {text:'Paid today; that cash must be financed for longer',correct:true},
 {text:'Paid halfway through; later payments always compound more'},
 {text:'They are identical for every rate and date'},
]} explanation="Future-value each invoice from its payment time. With positive rates, earlier outflows create more financing cost; a zero rate removes that timing difference." />

**Storage** includes the costs required to keep the specified deliverable asset available: warehousing, insurance and any agreed handling or preservation. These are real charges, with cash dates. If the known invoice Cⱼ is due at tⱼ and a known dividend Dᵢ is received at tᵢ, the complete cash-flow model is

$$
F_0=\left(S_0+\sum_jC_je^{-rt_j}-\sum_iD_ie^{-rt_i}\right)e^{rT}.
$$

The storage costs enter with a plus sign; income enters with a minus sign. This equality requires the purchased unit, costs and eventual delivery to be fixed and feasible. It does not turn an uncertain future transport bill into a known one.

For spot 80, r = 5% continuous, T = 1, no income and a **known storage invoice of 3 at half a year**,

$$
F_0=80e^{0.05}+3e^{0.025}=84.10168771+3.07594536=87.17763307.
$$

The spot loan starts at date 0. A separate three-unit borrowing starts when the invoice is paid at date 0.5. Both debts are repaid at delivery. Equivalently, the invoice present value is $3e^{-0.025}=2.92592974$ and the full prepaid cost is **82.92592974**. At zero financing the result is simply **83**, regardless of that invoice's date. With negative financing the familiar positive-rate timing order reverses; the stated convention still controls the calculation.

Earlier payment raises the delivery cost at the selected positive rate. This replica assumes the same intact deliverable unit, secured storage and no omitted transport, insurance or loss charges." />

**Pitfall:** adding annual percentages to a price without naming the base, payment dates and compounding. An invoice of 3, a quoted annual storage rate of 3% and a service fee retained in commodity units are three different contracts. A continuous formula and its first-order simple approximation need not give the same cents.

### When the dated model helps

When comparing warehouse or financing offers, or checking whether a cash-and-carry route actually delivers the contract's grade and location. Quality changes, spoilage, capacity and delivery options can change the payoff. Owning a barrel somewhere is not automatically the same as owning the contract's deliverable barrel at the right place and date.

## Keep a consumption service out of the cash account

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A refinery values having fuel available during a shortage. Can an outside trader automatically record that convenience benefit as cash to repay a borrowing?" options={[
 {text:'No; an operational service is not a cash dividend payable to every holder',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; every convenience-yield percentage arrives in the trader’s bank account'},
 {text:'Yes; physical inventory can always be borrowed without affecting anyone'},
]} explanation="Convenience yield represents the service of access to inventory, such as avoiding a production stoppage. Its economic value need not be a transferable monetary receipt, and shorting inventory may remove that service." />

A **consumption asset** is held for use, such as fuel for a refinery. Its **convenience yield** is the economic benefit of immediate access to inventory. The factory can continue operating during a shortage. A forward promising fuel later does not supply fuel now. [CME explains convenience through this physical availability benefit](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/courses/introduction-to-base-metals/what-is-contango-and-backwardation).

We can parameterize a positive commodity price relationship as

$$
F_0=S_0e^{(r+u-y)T},\qquad
 y=r+u-\frac{\ln(F_0/S_0)}{T},
$$

for positive S₀, F₀ and T and an explicitly stated continuous storage convention u. Here **y is an implied residual or modeled service yield**, not a measured cash dividend. Inferred y can also absorb a mismatched grade, funding, fees or other model errors. Reading it back from the same F₀ is not independent evidence that all those assumptions are correct.

To make a full-carry **upper bound** concrete, specify an invented storage offer: a custodian retains commodity units continuously at annual rate u = 3%, leaving the remaining units intact. Buy $e^{0.03}=1.030454534$ units at spot 80, for **82.43636272**. After the storage-unit charge, exactly one unit remains at year 1. Borrow the purchase cost at continuous r = 5%; the certain delivery-date debt is

$$
U=80e^{0.03}e^{0.05}=80e^{0.08}=86.66296541.
$$

If that financing, custody, insurance and exact delivery are secured, selling the unit forward above U produces a positive cash-and-carry surplus. This bound uses the **cash cost of securing delivery**, with no convenience receipt subtracted from the loan. The fee-in-units offer is a specified model, not a claim that every warehouse charges this way; the previous section handles an ordinary currency invoice.

Now suppose the matched forward is **76**, below spot 80 and below U. The parameterization implies

$$
y=0.05+0.03-\ln(76/80)=0.1312932944,
$$

or about **13.129329% continuous per year**. We have not earned that percentage in cash. The reverse trade may be unavailable: an inventory owner may refuse to lend essential fuel, or require compensation for its lost service. Thus a cheap commodity forward does not automatically recreate the financial-asset reverse arbitrage. [The university notes distinguish the commodity upper bound from the problematic reverse direction](https://faculty.weatherhead.case.edu/phr/textbook/powerpoint/notes2.pdf).

For comparison, inserting an **assumed** y = 4% into the same positive-price parameterization gives $80e^{0.04}=83.26486194$. The simple expression $80(1+0.04)=83.20$ is a different approximation. With y = 10%, the exponential model gives $80e^{-0.02}=78.41589386$. Neither result establishes a guaranteed trading return. This positive-price model also does not rule out negative prices under other commodity settlement and storage conditions.

### When to interpret convenience

When studying storage, scarcity and differences between present availability and later delivery. A quote below spot is often called **backwardation**; one above spot, **contango**. An entire maturity curve can have seasonal humps, so one spot-versus-forward comparison does not establish every adjacent slope. Expectations can influence current spot prices, inventory decisions and service values; a forward is not generally an unbiased physical expected future spot price. Saying it contains no information about expectations would go too far.

## Separate delivery-date forwards from intermediate futures cash

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="One long futures unit settles at quotes 100 → 110 → 100. Does its zero nominal sum of settlement payments prove its financed terminal cash is zero?" options={[
 {text:'No; the first receipt can earn interest before the final payment',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; the timing of positive and negative cash is irrelevant'},
 {text:'No; a second payoff of 100 must be added after the last settlement'},
]} explanation="The payments are +10 and −10. They telescope to zero before financing, but a receipt and a later payment are not the same dated cash flow. Do not add a second terminal payoff already included in settlement." />

A conventional unmargined **forward** settles its promised exchange or net payoff at maturity. **Futures** transfer gains and losses through variation margin at intermediate settlement times. Actual OTC collateral terms can also create intermediate cash; the contract's terms matter more than its label. [CFA's futures curriculum explains the valuation relevance of settlement timing and interest-rate dependence](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/pricing-valuation-futures-contracts).

Use a stylized two-settlement example, at half a year and one year. It is not a venue calendar. A forward has K = 100 and terminal reference 100, giving payoff **0**. One fixed long futures unit has quotes 100 → 110 → 100. Its settlement payments are **+10 at 0.5** and **−10 at 1**, nominal total 0. If cash grows at a known **2.5% effective over the remaining half-year**, the first receipt becomes 10.25. Terminal cash is

$$
10(1.025)-10=0.25.
$$

For the path 100 → 90 → 100, the first payment is −10, financed to −10.25, then +10 is received. Terminal cash is **−0.25**. The same terminal reference does not determine the financing result of a fixed one-contract futures path. These examples assume equal borrowing/lending over that interval and exclude trading costs and collateral funding.

Under ideal **deterministic interest rates**, matched forward and futures delivery quotations can be equal. That result does not say that a fixed one-unit cash path has the same funded payoff. The exact replication adjusts the futures quantity, often called **tailing**. In this two-interval example, hold $1/1.025=0.9756097561$ futures units for the first interval, then one for the last. On the upward path the first receipt is **9.75609756**, grows to 10 and offsets the last −10. The tailed terminal result is 0, matching the forward. This uses a known remaining growth factor, divisible contracts and frictionless rebalancing. [The primary teaching derivation states the deterministic-rate condition](https://book.derivative-securities.org/Chapter_MertonMargrabeBlack.html).

With uncertain rates, the interaction between settlement cash and rates can affect the fair futures-versus-forward quotation; a single realized path does not estimate that pricing adjustment. Funding constraints can matter even when a final hedge works. We will return to contract-specific delivery and settlement references later.

**A curve shape is not a realized profit.** Suppose a long futures position starts at 105 and closes at 101: its one-unit loss is 4. A new forward-like futures position is then opened at 106 and later closes at 102: another loss of 4. Ignoring financing and fees, the accumulated settlement result is **−8**. The five-unit difference between 101 and 106 at the roll is a change of contract quote, not an additional five-unit asset purchase fee. Different future quote paths could produce gains even if the current curve has the same shape. To evaluate a strategy, record contracts, quantities, settlement paths, funding, costs and collateral returns.

### When to keep the cash calendar

When comparing forwards with futures, checking a roll strategy, or assessing liquidity before a physical sale/purchase. A maturity payoff hedge can coexist with an intermediate cash shortage. A current contango or backwardation description does not settle the distribution of later trading returns.
