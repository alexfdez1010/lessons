A futures curve compares simultaneous quotations for different delivery dates. Contango and backwardation describe that snapshot. To calculate a holding or rolling strategy’s result, we also need the quotes of the contracts actually held as time passes. Closing one contract near 80 and opening another at 82 does not by itself debit 2 per unit; the following examples show where the actual settlement gains and losses arise.

## The basis: spot minus futures

Start with the gap between today's spot and a futures price.

**Definition.** The **basis** is the difference between the spot price and the futures price: $\text{basis} = S_0 - F_0$ (conventions vary; we'll use spot minus futures). It's just the cost-of-carry wedge from last lesson, viewed as a single number. If carrying the asset is expensive (futures above spot), the basis is **negative**; if holding it is beneficial (futures below spot), the basis is **positive**.

**The iron law — convergence.** Whatever the basis is today, it must shrink to **zero at expiry**. On delivery day, "delivery now via the future" and "delivery now in the spot market" are the *same transaction*, so the futures price and the spot price must meet: $F_T = S_T$. The basis is a wedge that closes as expiry approaches — slowly at first, then snapping shut at the end. This convergence is *guaranteed* by arbitrage (if they didn't converge, you'd buy the cheap one, sell the dear one, and pocket the gap at delivery), and it's the engine behind everything else in this lesson.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why convergence is non-negotiable">
Imagine at expiry the future is \$85 but spot is \$80. Buy spot at \$80, immediately deliver it against the future for \$85, pocket \$5 risk-free — instantly. Everyone does this until the prices meet. The same logic runs in reverse if the future is below spot. At the delivery moment there's no carry left to pay (no time to wait), so the only arbitrage-free outcome is $F = S$. The basis *has* to be zero at the end.
</Callout>

## Contango: the upward-sloping curve

**Analogy.** Think of a row of price labels for different delivery dates. Reading from one label to the next changes the promised date. It does not show what happens to a single held contract tomorrow. The contango snapshot can be relevant to a strategy’s later return, but it does not charge the slope as an immediate fee.

**Definition.** A market is in **contango** when futures prices are *higher* than spot, and farther-dated futures are higher still — an **upward-sloping** curve. This is the normal state for assets with positive net carry (financing + storage outweighing any convenience yield): gold, most equity indices, and well-supplied commodities. The curve slopes up because each extra month of delivery means another month of carry costs baked into the price.

## Backwardation: the downward-sloping curve

**Analogy.** A lower price label for later delivery is still a quotation for a different promise. Entering that later contract does not immediately deposit the gap in your account. A later rise in the quote of a contract you actually hold can produce a gain, as the stated path below demonstrates.

**Definition.** A market is in **backwardation** when futures prices are *lower* than spot, and farther-dated futures lower still — a **downward-sloping** curve. It signals a high convenience yield: the market will pay a premium for the physical asset *right now*, typically because supply is tight (an oil shock, a harvest failure). The curve slopes down because the benefit of holding the asset today outweighs the cost of carrying it.

Toggle the chart to **Backwardation** to watch the curve fall away below spot — distant delivery is the bargain.

## Roll yield: keep the contracts and their price paths

A holder **rolls** by closing one futures contract and opening another maturity. The new future has zero value immediately after entry in the ideal settlement setup; the quoted delivery price is not an upfront purchase of its underlying notional. Closing the old position realizes the gains or losses already generated by its own price path. Margin adjustments and trading fees are separate dated cash items.

**Roll yield** is used in return decompositions associated with moving through a maturity curve. The convention needs a specified strategy, reference and return denominator. Here we report **currency P&L for a fixed number of units**, without relabeling a quote gap as a fee or inventing a percentage return on an unspecified capital base.

- A contango example can lose as each held contract’s own quote falls toward a lower delivery reference. That requires the stated subsequent path; the current slope alone does not establish it.
- A backwardation example can gain as each held quote rises toward a higher reference. Opening the cheaper next contract does not itself pay that gain.

### Worked example — a specified losing path in contango

Assume 12 successive one-month holding periods with matched delivery reference 80 at each expiry. In each period, enter one invented 1,000-barrel contract at 82 and hold until its final settlement at 80. Its cumulative variation payments sum to (80−82)×1,000=**−2,000**. With 12 such explicitly repeated paths, the nominal total is **−24,000**, before financing, fees and collateral income. No percentage return is stated because no capital base has been defined.

At each renewal, opening the next contract at 82 rather than the settled quote 80 adds **no extra 2,000 notional purchase fee**. The next period’s loss occurs only if that newly held contract subsequently settles at 80 as assumed. A different later quote path could give a different result even with the same initial curve.

### Worked example — a specified gaining path in backwardation

Keep the same 12 periods and 1,000-barrel contract size, but enter each successive contract at 78 and settle it at 80. Each held contract’s price gain produces (80−78)×1,000=**+2,000**, totaling **+24,000** before financing, fees and collateral income. The difference is the specified 78→80 holding path; the act of replacing one contract with a cheaper one is not an immediate cash receipt.

The [cost-of-carry lesson](/futures-and-forwards/cost-of-carry-pricing) also separates nominal settlement sums from their financed terminal cash. These examples hold contract units fixed. A fund targeting a fixed notional or a collateral-based exposure would need its actual quantity and return convention too.

## Basis risk: when your hedge doesn't quite fit

One more consequence of the basis, this time for hedgers. Last lessons assumed a hedge perfectly offsets your exposure. In reality, the futures you use rarely match your actual risk *exactly* — different grade, different location, different date — so the basis between *your* asset and the *contract* can wander.

**Definition.** **Basis risk** is the risk that the basis (the gap between the price of the asset you're hedging and the futures contract you're hedging with) changes unpredictably before you close out. A hedge kills your exposure to the *level* of prices but leaves you exposed to the *basis*. The hedge is only as good as the correlation between your asset and the contract.

### Worked example — the imperfect jet-fuel hedge

An airline planning to **buy jet fuel** is exposed to a rise in purchase costs. It normally uses a **long** position in a suitable fuel hedge. If it cross-hedges with **crude-oil futures**, it buys those futures rather than selling them, and must convert the quote units, quantities and currency appropriately. Jet fuel and crude need not move together in the same amounts. If the fuel cost rises more than the selected long hedge gains, the combined purchase cost still rises. This is product basis risk, rather than a guarantee that the local fuel price converges to the crude contract's reference. The [basis and hedge-ratio lesson](/futures-and-forwards/basis-risk-and-hedge-ratios) calculates both the direction and quantity explicitly.

## Putting it together

The curve is a snapshot across delivery dates; a strategy result follows the contracts held through time. The explicit 82→80 and 78→80 paths above produce losses and gains, respectively, before funding and costs. Their roll quote gaps are not additional principal payments. Basis and delivery references still matter, while a different local asset or contract leaves the basis risk illustrated by the airline example. Keep those distinctions with the recap:

## Key Takeaways

<Callout variant="success" title="What to remember">
- **Basis = spot − futures**, the carry wedge as a single number — and arbitrage forces it to **converge to zero at expiry**, since $F = S$ at delivery.
- **Contango is an upward curve** (futures above spot), the normal positive-carry state. **Backwardation is a downward curve** (futures below spot), signalling a high convenience yield / tight supply.
- **A roll changes the contract held.** Calculate P&L from each held contract’s dated quote changes and multiplier. A new future’s quoted notional is not an upfront purchase fee; do not subtract the roll gap again.
- **Strategy and spot returns can differ.** Held contracts, quantities, settlement paths, collateral income, funding and fees must be inspected before attributing the difference. A current curve alone guarantees no later return.
- **Basis risk** is the residual wobble when your hedging contract doesn't perfectly match your real exposure (jet fuel hedged with crude) — a key reason a bespoke forward can beat a standardised future.
</Callout>
