A producer sells a futures contract at €200 per tonne. At harvest, the local crop sells for €180 while the futures contract closes at €185. The short futures gain €15 per tonne, bringing effective revenue to **€195**, not €200.

The €5 difference comes from a local basis of −€5 per tonne. The hedge can transfer a major price exposure while leaving differences in product, place, date and quantity. We will calculate those differences before asking how much futures exposure actually reduces variation in the outcome. Prices, contracts and probability scenarios below are invented.

## Define the actual exposure before naming the basis

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Local cash grain is €180 per tonne and the selected futures quote is €185 per tonne. Under the convention cash minus futures, what is the basis?" options={[{text:'−€5 per tonne',correct:true},{text:'+€5 per tonne'},{text:'€365 per tonne'},{text:'Zero because every local price must equal futures at any date'}]} explanation="Basis = 180 − 185 = −5. The quote, units, location, quality and futures maturity must be identified before that subtraction is meaningful." />

An **exposure** is the quantity and price change that affects the business. A producer planning to sell a crop is hurt by a lower selling price. A buyer planning to purchase fuel is hurt by a higher purchase price. Owning an asset and needing to buy it later are different exposures even when both involve the same commodity.

Write down the physical product, grade, location, price currency, quantity, transaction date and chosen contract. Use comparable quote units. Subtracting euros per tonne from dollars per barrel without conversion does not produce a meaningful basis.

Here, define local **basis** at time $t$ as

$$
b_t=S_t-F_t,
$$

where $S_t$ is the cash price of the physical exposure and $F_t$ is the quote of the specified futures contract. This sign convention matches [CME's grain-basis explanation](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/courses/introduction-to-grains-and-oilseeds/learn-about-basis-grains). Other markets and documents can use the opposite convention, so read the definition.

**Basis risk** is uncertainty about that relationship when the hedge is closed. A **cross hedge** uses a contract on a different product from the physical exposure. Local freight, quality, processing margins or supply can change even when the contract tracks its own reference accurately. For example, if futures remain €185 but local grain falls from €180 to €175, basis changes from −€5 to −€10 per tonne.

### Convergence has a reference

Expiry links a futures contract to its defined delivery terms or settlement reference. It does not make every grade, delivery location and local customer quote equal to that reference. Closing a July contract against a June physical sale is also a different date from holding that contract to its own expiry. The local basis in our hedge can therefore remain nonzero at the physical transaction date.

### When this definition helps

Use it when selecting a contract and interpreting a statement that prices “converge”. A customised forward can match some terms more closely, but does not remove every delivery, credit, funding or quantity risk. A forecast of the local basis is still a forecast.

## A producer's short hedge leaves the closing basis

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A producer sells futures at €200 per tonne and closes them at €185 while selling physical grain at €180. Ignoring fees and funding, what is effective revenue per matched tonne?" options={[{text:'€195: 180 + (200 − 185)',correct:true},{text:'€200, regardless of the local sale price'},{text:'€165, by subtracting the short futures gain'},{text:'€365, by adding the closing futures quote'}]} explanation="Short futures gain €15 when their quote falls from 200 to 185. Adding that gain to the €180 physical receipt gives 195." />

A **short hedge** sells futures against a planned physical sale. Let $F_0$ be the initial futures price and $F_H$ the closing quote at the physical transaction date $H$. For one futures unit per physical unit, cumulative short futures profit is $F_0-F_H$. Revenue per unit after combining the sale and the hedge is

$$
S_H+(F_0-F_H)=F_0+(S_H-F_H)=F_0+b_H.
$$

The algebra removes the closing futures level from the expression, but leaves the **closing local basis**, $b_H$. It does not replace that unknown basis with zero.

Our invented producer expects to sell **1,000 tonnes**. Each invented contract covers **100 tonnes**, so ten short contracts match the planned units. Initial futures are €200 per tonne. At close, futures are €185 and local basis is −€5:

| Cash or position | Calculation | Amount |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Local selling price | 185 − 5 | €180 per tonne |
| Physical revenue | 1,000 × 180 | €180,000 |
| Short futures profit | 10 × 100 × (200 − 185) | +€15,000 |
| Combined revenue | 180,000 + 15,000 | €195,000 |
| Effective selling price | 195,000/1,000 | €195 per tonne |

<BasisHedgeExplorer client:visible title="A matched producer hedge still depends on local basis" />

Keep the closing basis at −€5 while moving the closing futures quote: the matched effective price remains €195. Now keep futures fixed and change basis to −€10: the effective price becomes $200-10=190$. If basis instead strengthens to +€5, the effective price becomes €205. **A stronger basis raises this short hedger's selling price.**

The example adds cumulative futures profit to the physical receipt. It does not pretend all futures cash arrives on the sale date. Daily variation margin has its own funding timeline, which we revisit below.

Its effective price is {{initial futures plus closing basis|closing futures plus initial basis|always initial futures alone}}. With initial futures €200 and closing basis −€10, that price is {{€190|€200|€210}} per tonne." explanation="Use the closing basis for the physical date. The futures gain is included once; the contract notional is not an extra cash receipt." />

### When a short hedge is useful

Use it to reduce exposure to a fall in a planned selling price, with contract choice and size matched to the business. A loss on the futures leg can coexist with useful protection: it must be evaluated alongside the physical sale, costs and remaining basis risk.

## A buyer uses the opposite futures direction

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="An airline expects to buy fuel later and is worried about rising fuel costs. Which direction is normally consistent with that exposure?" options={[{text:'A long position in a suitable fuel hedge; a crude cross hedge still has basis risk',correct:true},{text:'Short futures, which also lose when the fuel benchmark rises'},{text:'No direction matters because every hedge guarantees the same cost'},{text:'Sell the physical fuel that has not been purchased as the only possible strategy'}]} explanation="A buyer's long futures position gains when the selected futures quote rises, potentially offsetting a higher physical purchase cost. Using crude for jet fuel still leaves product, unit, timing and other mismatches." />

A **long hedge** buys futures against a planned purchase. Its cumulative profit is $F_H-F_0$ per futures unit. Deduct that profit from the physical purchase cost:

$$
S_H-(F_H-F_0)=F_0+b_H.
$$

This is the same net unit-price expression as the producer's, but it is a **cost to minimise**, whereas the producer's is revenue to protect. Consequently, a stronger basis hurts the buyer and helps the seller under this matched-unit model. [CME's long-hedge example](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/lessons/buying-futures-for-protection-against-rising-prices) uses futures purchases to cover planned commodity purchases and explicitly retains local basis risk.

Suppose our buyer needs 1,000 tonnes, buys ten 100-tonne futures at €200 and closes at €220. Local basis is +€5, making cash price €225. The physical purchase costs €225,000. Long futures profit is $1{,}000(220-200)=20{,}000$, so net cost is $225{,}000-20{,}000=205{,}000$, or **€205 per tonne**. The stronger closing basis has left a higher cost than the €195 target associated with an expected −€5 basis.

<BasisHedgeExplorer client:visible side="buyer" closeFutures={220} closeBasis={5} title="A buyer deducts long futures profit from the purchase cost" />

If both closing quotes fall to €185 futures and €180 physical, long futures lose €15,000. Net cost is $180{,}000-(-15{,}000)=195{,}000$. The cheaper physical purchase and futures loss belong in the same calculation.

<Reveal client:visible buttonLabel="Check the wrong direction" hideLabel="Hide the calculation">
If the buyer had shorted 1,000 futures units at 200 and closed at 220, the futures loss would be 20,000. Adding that loss to the 225,000 physical purchase cost gives 245,000, or 245 per unit. The position has amplified the adverse move rather than offsetting it. This calculation uses the same quantities and quote units; it is not a promise that every real fuel cross hedge moves by this amount.
</Reveal>

### When to check the business side first

Use the planned cash transaction, rather than the word “commodity”, to choose the direction. An airline planning to purchase fuel normally hedges rising purchase costs with a long position in the appropriate contract. A crude-oil cross hedge must also address the different product, quote unit, currency, quantity and dates; correlation alone does not set its size.

## A unit hedge ratio is not a value hedge ratio

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A producer has 1,000 tonnes of exposure and wants 0.8 futures units per physical unit. Each invented contract represents 100 tonnes. How many contracts is the continuous target?" options={[{text:'8 short contracts',correct:true},{text:'800 contracts'},{text:'10 contracts simply because the physical quantity is 1,000'},{text:'0.8 contracts regardless of contract size'}]} explanation="Futures units are 0.8 ×1,000 = 800. Divide by 100 units per contract to get 8 contracts. The producer uses a short position for this positive ratio." />

Define the **unit hedge ratio**, $h$, as futures units divided by physical exposure units, with direction stated separately. If physical quantity is $Q$ and contract quantity is $q$,

$$
N=hQ/q.
$$

A ratio of one matches quantities, not necessarily money values. With $Q=1{,}000$, $q=100$ and $h=0.8$, $N=8$. At an initial futures quote of €200, the futures notional is $8(100)(200)= 160{,}000$. If the physical cash quote is €195, its initial value is $1{,}000(195)=195{,}000$. Thus the **value ratio** is $160{,}000/195{,}000\approx82.05\%$, despite an 80% unit ratio.

An estimated ratio from **percentage returns** relates value exposures and needs the relevant price/notional conversion. A ratio from **price changes in comparable money per unit** can be used in the quantity formula above. Name the convention rather than swapping the two.

For a producer with a positive unit ratio $h$, effective revenue per physical unit is

$$
S_H+h(F_0-F_H).
$$

With eight contracts in our original 1,000-tonne case, $h=0.8$. At closing futures 185 and local price 180, revenue per unit is $180+0.8(200-185)=192$. If futures instead 220 with the same −5 basis, local price 215 gives $215+0.8(200-220)=199$. A partial hedge leaves sensitivity to the overall move as well as the basis.

### Whole contracts leave a sizing choice

For a 1,050-tonne exposure with 100-tonne contracts and a target ratio 0.8, the continuous count is $0.8(1{,}050)/100=8.4$. Eight actual contracts give $h= 800/1{,}050\approx0.7619$; nine give $900/1{,}050\approx0.8571$. There is no tradable 0.4 of this invented contract.

### When to compare both neighbours

Use the chosen objective, fees, position limits and funding capacity to compare nearby whole counts. More contracts are not automatically a better hedge. If actual production or demand changes, the same position represents a different ratio and needs reviewing.

## Derive a minimum-variance ratio from explicit scenarios

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Does covering exactly 100% of physical units always minimize the variation in a cross-hedged outcome?" options={[{text:'No; the relevant price changes can have different scales and imperfect co-movement',correct:true},{text:'Yes; one-for-one units eliminate every mismatch'},{text:'Yes; correlation alone always equals the correct quantity ratio'},{text:'No; every hedge must use more contracts than the exposure'}]} explanation="The ratio depends on how the actual and futures prices change together over the chosen horizon, in the units being modelled. One-for-one is a particular choice, not a general minimum." />

**Variance** is the average squared distance from a variable's mean. It measures spread, in squared units. **Standard deviation** is its square root, returning to the original units. **Covariance** averages the products of the two variables' distances from their respective means, measuring how their changes move together.

Use four **equally likely invented scenarios** over one common hedge horizon. They are a complete probability model for this example, not four observations that establish a reliable market forecast. All changes are euros per tonne:

| Scenario, probability 1/4 each | Cash-price change ΔS | Futures-price change ΔF | Product ΔS × ΔF |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A | −12 | −10 | 120 |
| B | −4 | −10 | 40 |
| C | +4 | +10 | 40 |
| D | +12 | +10 | 120 |

Both means are zero. Using all four scenarios in the denominator,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\Delta F)=\frac{100+100+100+100}{4}=100,
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\Delta S)=\frac{144+16+16+144}{4}=80,\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S,\Delta F)=\frac{120+40+40+120}{4}=80.
$$

The net price change per physical unit is $X=\Delta S-h\Delta F$: a producer combines its sale-price change with short futures; a buyer combines its purchase-price change with long futures. Expanding its variance gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\operatorname{Var}(\Delta S)+h^2\operatorname{Var}(\Delta F)
-2h\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S,\Delta F).
$$

Here that is

$$
80+ 100h^2-160h= 16+100(h-0.8)^2.
$$

A square cannot be negative, so the minimum is **16 at h = 0.8**. No calculus is required. At h = 1, variance is $80+100-160=20$; unhedged at h = 0 it is 80. The minimum removes $(80-16)/80=80\%$ of the model's variance, not all risk. Standard deviation falls from $\sqrt{80}\approx8.9443$ to 4 euros per tonne; an 80% variance reduction is not an 80% standard-deviation reduction. For a fixed 1,000-tonne quantity, total cash standard deviation is 1,000 × 4 = €4,000.

<MinimumVarianceHedge client:visible title="The minimum is 0.8 for this stated distribution" varianceLabel="Variance of net price change, (euros per tonne)²" unhedgedLabel="Unhedged variance, (euros per tonne)²" standardDeviationLabel="Net standard deviation, euros per tonne" totalStandardDeviationLabel="Total cash standard deviation, euros" />

For example, scenario A at h = 0.8 leaves $-12-0.8(-10)=-4$. The four residuals are −4,+4,−4,+4, whose mean is zero and variance is 16. At h = 1 they are −2,+6,−6,+2, giving variance $(4+36+36+4)/4=20$.

### General formula and its limits

When futures-price-change variance is positive, completing the same square yields

$$
h^*=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S,\Delta F)}{\operatorname{Var}(\Delta F)}
=\rho\frac{\sigma_{\Delta S}}{\sigma_{\Delta F}}.
$$

The covariance form needs positive futures variance; the correlation form additionally needs positive spot variance. If spot variance is zero while futures variance is positive, the covariance form gives h = 0.

Here **correlation** $\rho$ is covariance divided by the two standard deviations; it is a rescaled measure of linear co-movement. Our example gives $80/(\sqrt{80}\,10)\approx0.8944$, and $0.8944(\sqrt{80}/10)\approx0.8$. [Wolfram's formula reference](https://resources.wolframcloud.com/FormulaRepository/resources/Minimum-Variance-Hedge-Ratio) states the correlation-and-standard-deviation form. Our sign convention uses positive h for the offsetting units; a signed portfolio convention can write the futures position with the opposite sign.

Perfect correlation does not force h to equal one. If $\Delta S=2\Delta F$ in every state, correlation is one but a two-unit futures hedge cancels each price change. If futures changes have zero variance, the ratio above is not identified by division; there is no unique variance-minimising ratio from that expression. A negative covariance changes the optimal sign, rather than becoming positive because we prefer a familiar direction.

For the 1,050-tonne example, compare whole counts under this same variance model. Eight contracts give variance $16+100(800/1{,}050-0.8)^2\approx16.1451$. Nine give about 16.3265. Eight is closer to this continuous minimum, but transaction costs or a different objective can change the practical choice.

<Reveal client:visible buttonLabel="Check the denominator and horizon" hideLabel="Hide the answer">
These calculations average over four equally likely states, so the divisor is 4. If covariance and variance are both rescaled by the same factor, their ratio stays unchanged; mixing their conventions can change it. More important economically, daily changes, one-month changes and changes over the full exposure horizon need not give the same ratio. A formula does not remove the need to identify the data horizon.
</Reveal>

### When minimum variance is a useful objective

Use it as a transparent benchmark for a specified linear exposure, horizon and distribution. It minimises squared dispersion, not expected cost, worst loss, insolvency probability or funding needs. A fitted historical ratio and its risk reduction remain estimates; test them on relevant scenarios and periods beyond the data used to fit them.

## A final-price hedge still needs a cash and stress plan

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A producer's short hedge will offset a later physical sale, but futures rise before that sale. What can still happen now?" options={[{text:'Variation margin requires cash before the physical revenue arrives',correct:true},{text:'No cash can be required because the final hedge is useful'},{text:'The futures exchange must lend against all unrealised inventory gains'},{text:'Basis and quantity uncertainty automatically disappear'}]} explanation="The timing of futures settlement can differ from the physical cash receipt. A lower terminal price variance does not guarantee funding for intermediate cash outflows." />

At ten short 100-tonne contracts, a futures rise from 200 to 220 produces a cumulative **€20,000 futures loss**. Variation margin can require funding that loss while the physical crop's higher value is still unsold. [CME describes futures settlement variation](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/articles-and-reports/money-calculations-for-futures-and-options) as a daily cash transfer. The simple terminal calculations above omit that timing, collateral interest and borrowing cost.

Quantity can change as well. Suppose the same short position remains, closing futures are 220 and local price 215:

| Actual crop | Physical revenue | Futures profit/loss | Combined revenue | Per actual tonne |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1,000 tonnes | €215,000 | −€20,000 | €195,000 | €195 |
| 500 tonnes | €107,500 | −€20,000 | €87,500 | €175 |

The contract count did not shrink when the crop did. The second case is a two-unit hedge per actual tonne, not the one-unit position originally planned. A cancelled planned purchase can likewise leave a buyer with a standalone futures position.

Product spreads, currency conversion, freight and dates can also move outside the scenarios used to set h. A basis forecast can be wrong at the same time the business needs cash. Closing or rolling may incur spread and liquidity costs, and a contract's delivery or settlement rules still need attention.

<MCQ client:visible allowMultiple question="Select the two conclusions the model actually supports" options={[{text:'A ratio can reduce the specified terminal variance while leaving margin-funding risk',correct:true},{text:'A change in actual physical quantity changes the effective unit ratio',correct:true},{text:'The fitted ratio guarantees the same variance reduction in every future period'},{text:'A local basis must disappear just because the hedge contract reaches its own reference settlement'}]} explanation="The hedge is a conditional risk-management choice. Quantity, timing, local basis and the distribution of price changes all matter beyond one fitted variance number." />

### When to reassess the hedge

Review it when the forecast quantity, purchase date, available contract or price relationship changes. Evaluate the physical and futures positions together, then examine the cash dates separately. A low-variance terminal result is useful evidence for a stated model; it is not permission to ignore contractual obligations or liquidity.
