Your call lets you buy at \$95 when the stock can be sold for \$100. Exercising seems obvious—until you notice someone will pay \$7 for the option itself. The right is worth \$5 on immediate use, but it can be sold for \$7. What you do with the contract changes what you keep.

The earlier lessons separated [intrinsic value and time value](/options-basics/moneyness-intrinsic-and-time-value) and built [option strategies](/options-basics/basic-option-strategies). Here we follow the operational consequences. All worked amounts use **US dollars**, and each invented standard equity contract covers **100 shares** unless stated otherwise. Prices are executable teaching quotes, not live prices. Adjusted contracts and other products can have different deliverables and multipliers.

## Selling the right is different from using it

To **sell to close**, a holder sells the same option series to offset an existing long position. A **series** identifies matching underlying, type, strike, expiry and contract terms. The option position ends when that closing trade executes; no exercise is needed. A bid is a buyer's executable purchase price, subject to available size and execution.

To **exercise**, the holder invokes the contract's right. A physically settled call creates a stock purchase at the strike; a physically settled put creates a stock sale at the strike. The option disappears, but a stock position and settlement obligations can take its place. Think of a transferable voucher: selling the voucher and redeeming it are different transactions.

### Compare two routes for the same call

Assume one American-style call, multiplier 100 shares, strike \$95 per share, stock sale executable at \$100 and option sale executable at a **\$7 bid** for the whole contract size. There are no fees, taxes, distributions or price moves. The broker permits and funds the exercise plus immediate stock sale, with the required settlement arrangements.

| Route now | Cash and shares | Net value captured before the original premium |
|---|---|---:|
| Exercise, then sell shares | Pay 100 × \$95 = \$9,500; receive 100 shares; sell them for 100 × \$100 = \$10,000 | \$10,000 − \$9,500 = **\$500** |
| Sell the option to close | Sell one contract for 100 × \$7 | **\$700** |

The executable difference is \$700 − \$500 = **\$200**. Per share, intrinsic value is max(\$100 − \$95, 0) = \$5 and bid minus intrinsic is \$7 − \$5 = **\$2**. Exercising gives up that residual value. A screen's midpoint or last trade is not proof you can sell at \$7; this example explicitly assumes that bid is executable. The [OIC exercise FAQ compares closing a long option with exercising it](https://www.optionseducation.org/referencelibrary/faq/options-exercise).

### Value captured is not investment profit

If the original premium was \$8 per share, the option initially cost 100 × \$8 = **\$800**. Selling now for \$700 gives a net option investment result of \$700 − \$800 = **−\$100**, before costs. Exercising and immediately selling gives \$500 − \$800 = **−\$300**. A positive intrinsic value does not imply a profitable investment. The past premium is common to both routes, so it does not change their current \$200 difference.

### When to use it

Compare actual executable quotes, fees, funding, the wish to own or deliver shares and your broker's procedures before choosing a route. Selling to close requires a market and a fill; placing an unfilled order does not close the position. Immediate stock resale after exercise is an explicit assumption here, not a right available in every account.

## The holder chooses and the assigned writer owes

A **long holder** owns the exercise right. A **short writer** has the matching obligation if **assigned**, meaning selected to fulfil an exercise. The writer does not get a second vote after assignment. With cleared options, this is not necessarily the particular person who bought from that writer: exercise notices are allocated through clearing and broker procedures.

### Write the delivery budget before the trade

For **one physical-delivery contract**, strike \$95, multiplier 100 shares, these are the changes **if a valid exercise and matching assignment occur**. Positive means received; negative means paid or delivered. The original premium is excluded because it was exchanged earlier.

| Position | Cash change | Share change | Meaning |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Long call exercised | −\$9,500 | +100 | Buy at the strike |
| Short call assigned | +\$9,500 | −100 | Deliver at the strike |
| Long put exercised | +\$9,500 | −100 | Sell at the strike |
| Short put assigned | −\$9,500 | +100 | Buy at the strike |

Each matched pair nets to zero in this settlement budget: −\$9,500 + \$9,500 = \$0 cash and +100 − 100 = 0 shares. That accounting identity is not a promise that two traders with different entry prices, hedges and costs have opposite total investment returns.

### Assignment can leave a stock exposure

Suppose the assigned short put delivers 100 shares bought for \$9,500, while their market value is 100 × \$90 = **\$9,000**. The new stock has a **\$500 shortfall against its purchase price**. A \$200 premium received earlier would make the simple combined mark-to-market result −\$500 + \$200 = **−\$300**, before costs and later moves. The writer still must fund the \$9,500 purchase under the applicable account arrangements; the premium is not a substitute for that funding.

An assigned uncovered short call may require sourcing shares to deliver or create short-stock exposure. A put holder without shares to deliver also needs a permitted delivery arrangement. A **covered** call has stock available, but assignment still transfers it. [OIC explains assignment allocation and the short side's obligations](https://www.optionseducation.org/referencelibrary/faq/options-assignment).

### When to use it

Prepare both the cash and share sides, including what happens after the option has ended. A short position is normally closed by **buying to close** the matching series, using an executable seller's ask. Selling another contract instead increases the short position. Closing an open position does not undo an exercise or assignment already effective.

For standard OCC equity options, the current specification uses **T+1 exercise settlement**, the first applicable business day after exercise. The broker's display and funding procedures can have their own timing; the [OCC equity specifications](https://www.theocc.com/clearance-and-settlement/clearing/equity-options-product-specifications) state the contract unit, style and settlement cycle. Other products need their own specifications.

## Exercise style and settlement answer different questions

**Exercise style** is a timetable. **Settlement method** is a delivery instruction. A train ticket can specify both the departure time and the destination; one field does not determine the other.

- **American style** permits exercise on allowed dates before and at expiry, within applicable procedures and cutoffs.
- **European style** permits exercise only in its specified expiry exercise window.
- **Physical settlement** exchanges the deliverable against the strike payment.
- **Cash settlement** transfers a contractual monetary amount instead of delivering shares.

For a plain-vanilla cash-settled contract with reference $V$, strike $K$, currency multiplier $M$ per point and $n$ contracts, the call cash amount $A_C$ and put cash amount $A_P$ are:

$$A_C=\max(V-K,0)Mn$$
$$A_P=\max(K-V,0)Mn$$

### Same numerical difference, different delivery

Invent a cash-settled call with reference value **100 points**, strike **95 points**, multiplier **\$100 per point** and one contract. If validly exercised under its terms, the long receives (100 − 95) × \$100 × 1 = **\$500**; the assigned short pays \$500. Neither delivers shares. Compare the physical call: the holder pays \$9,500 and receives 100 shares, not a \$500 cash-only payment.

The cash reference may be determined from an opening-price calculation or another specified method. It need not equal the last displayed index level, an executable stock quote or the price when you can last trade the option. Read the reference calculation, currency multiplier and dates together.

A real counterexample to “cash means European” is Cboe's **OEX** S&P 100 option: its [product specification says American exercise and cash settlement](https://www.cboe.com/tradable_products/sp_100/sp_100_options/oex_specifications). **XEO**, on the same index, has [European exercise and cash settlement](https://www.cboe.com/tradable-products/sp-100/sp-100-index-options/xeo-specifications). The specifications, rather than the index name or continent, resolve the question.

### When to use it

Check style, exercise window, deliverable, multiplier, cash reference and settlement date before treating two contracts as interchangeable. European exercise does not prevent selling to close before expiry while a market is open and a trade can execute. Cash settlement removes share delivery for that contract; it does not remove price risk or guarantee a profit.

## Early exercise has an opportunity cost

An American exercise right is permission, not a recommendation. Exercising a call pays the strike sooner and replaces the option with stock; exercising a put delivers stock and receives strike cash sooner. Using the right ends any remaining optionality.

### Price the value being surrendered

Keep the original strike \$95 and executable stock price \$100, but use a separate executable option bid of **\$5.25** per share. Selling receives 100 × \$5.25 = **\$525**. Exercising and immediately selling the stock captures 100 × (\$100 − \$95) = **\$500**. Residual value surrendered = \$525 − \$500 = **\$25**.

<ExerciseAssignmentFlow
  client:visible
  title={"Inspect the residual value before exercising early"}
  optionBid={5.25}
/>

Now suppose a dividend of **\$0.50 per share** is due, and valid exercise before the relevant ex-dividend date would establish the holder's entitlement. The 100-share cash dividend would be **\$50**. Comparing \$50 with the \$25 residual flags a possible reason to examine early exercise, not an automatic profit of \$25. The stock normally adjusts for the dividend, funding the \$9,500 strike has a cost, and exercise timing, taxes and execution matter. Compare whole positions and cash flows, not the dividend in isolation.

For a deep-in-the-money put with little remaining value beyond intrinsic, receiving the strike earlier can matter because cash can earn interest. But surrendering optionality, missing subsequent price moves, dividends and stock borrowing/delivery constraints may change the decision. The [OIC exercise overview discusses dividend-related early assignment](https://www.optionseducation.org/optionsoverview/exercising-options); the remaining value surrendered still belongs in the comparison.

### When to use it

Look at the actual sale quote and the complete funding and delivery consequences. A theoretical “never exercise” shortcut can fail under dividends, rates, market frictions or other contract features. Conversely, “in the money” merely identifies intrinsic value; it is not a command to exercise. Writers cannot rely on a holder making the economically best decision, and should not treat apparently unattractive early exercise as impossible.

## Expiration is a process with several clocks

Keep these events separate: **last option trading opportunity**, **broker instruction deadline**, **exercise processing**, **expiry** and **settlement**. They can occur at different times or on different days. A notice displayed later is not proof that the economic obligation began only when you saw it.

Some eligible positions go through **exercise by exception**, an administrative process with threshold and contrary-instruction rules. Do not turn that into “every profitable option automatically exercises.” Procedures apply at the clearing and account levels, with product exceptions and broker policies. A positive intrinsic amount is not the same as investment profit. The [OIC FAQ distinguishes exercise by exception from an unconditional automatic rule](https://www.optionseducation.org/referencelibrary/faq/options-exercise).

### Follow an invented near-strike expiry

**Pin risk** is uncertainty about exercise and resulting exposure when a price is near the strike. For one physically settled short call, strike \$100 and multiplier 100, assume the following **invented schedule**. The option's trading session has ended, but this hypothetical broker is still accepting valid holder instructions until the stated cutoff. These clock times are not a schedule for any real product or broker.

| Local time in the invented example | Observation | Consequence to consider |
|---|---|---|
| 16:00 | Stock is \$99.99 at its regular close | Call is \$0.01 out of the money on that quote |
| 16:20 | News moves an executable stock quote to \$101 | A holder may prefer to exercise if still permitted |
| 16:30 | Hypothetical holder-instruction cutoff | New instructions stop under this assumed policy |
| Later processing | Assignment may be reported | Earlier valid instructions can create delivery obligations |

If assigned, the short call receives 100 × \$100 = **\$10,000** and must deliver **100 shares**. If an uncovered writer then has to source those shares at \$101, purchase cost is **\$10,100** and the difference is **−\$100**, before the original option premium and any costs. The actual later price could be higher or lower. Without assignment, that delivery does not arise from this contract. Near the strike, the uncertainty can therefore be a discrete **zero-or-100-share** outcome, not merely a tiny option quote.

### A spread does not send instructions for you

Holding a protective long option alongside a short one does not make all operational steps automatic. One leg can be assigned while the other expires unused, particularly around after-hours moves and deadlines. Identify and manage the resulting shares and funding rather than assuming the payoff diagram itself submits exercise instructions. [FINRA and OCC illustrate this after-hours assignment problem](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/trading-options-understanding-assignment).

### When to use it

Before the final trading opportunity, establish the actual last trade, exercise window, broker cutoff, reference price, instructions and settlement requirements for that series. If you intend to close, confirm the order filled; if relying on exercise instructions, confirm how the broker accepts them. Broker risk controls may restrict exercise or close positions when funding is insufficient. No universal clock time, threshold or liquidation policy is assumed in this lesson.
