A foreign deposit pays 9% while your home deposit pays 1%. There is an eight-percentage-point difference on the rate sheet. Before treating it as a return, bring the foreign cash home, repay any borrowing and check whether you could keep the position open until maturity. The exchange rate gets a vote in every step.

A **currency carry trade** holds a currency investment financed, or compared with an investment, in another currency with a lower interest rate. Leaving the currency conversion unhedged creates exposure to its future price. We will use invented **home currency D** and **foreign currency F**, with a one-year home gross growth factor $G_d=1.01$ and foreign factor $G_f=1.09$. These are known **effective one-year rates** of 1% and 9%, rather than unspecified regulatory APRs. A gross factor is the final value of one unit invested over the stated period. Both cash flows start on the same value date and mature together.

## Put units on the quote before calculating a move

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Q means foreign units per home unit. It rises from 30 to 36. What happens to the home-currency price of one foreign unit?" options={[
 {text:'It falls from 1/30 to 1/36, a decline of one sixth',correct:true},
 {text:'It rises by 20%, just like Q'},
 {text:'It falls by exactly 20%, because reciprocal percentage changes are opposites'},
]} explanation="Taking reciprocals reverses direction, but percentage changes also use different starting values. A 20% rise in Q corresponds to a 16.666667% fall in X = 1/Q." />

A currency quote is a price with a numerator and denominator. Define

$$
X_t=\frac{\text{home currency D}}{\text{one foreign unit F}},\qquad Q_t=\frac{\text{foreign currency F}}{\text{one home unit D}}=\frac{1}{X_t}.
$$

The subscript t identifies the date. We begin at $Q_0=30$, so $X_0=1/30$. Exchanging D 1,000 buys $1000/X_0=30{,}000$ foreign units. Converting F 30,000 back at a later Q requires **division by Q**, equivalently multiplication by X. It does not require multiplying by both.

If Q rises from 30 to 36, one home unit buys more foreign currency. The foreign currency has **depreciated against home**. Its home price changes by

$$
\frac{X_T}{X_0}-1=\frac{1/36}{1/30}-1=\frac{30}{36}-1=-16.666667\%.
$$

Meanwhile, $Q_T/Q_0-1=36/30-1=20\%$. These are two descriptions of the same relative price move, with different denominators. They are not two independent currency losses to subtract from a trade.

**Pitfall:** writing “spot = 30” after defining spot as home per foreign. Units are part of the input. The same principle applies to forward quotes, returns and expected values. Real currency-pair conventions may choose either direction; neither the labels “domestic” nor “high yield” identify a permanently fixed country.

### When to use the reciprocal

When converting a quotation or translating cash back to the investor's reporting currency. Write one conversion line with units before calculating a return. For Q = 31, foreign maturity cash F 32,700 converts to $32700/31=\text{D }1054.838710$.

## Separate a covered price from an uncovered expected return

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A forward fixes the future conversion rate under a contract. Does that alone make it the physical expected future spot quote?" options={[
 {text:'No; equality with the expected spot needs an additional uncovered-parity hypothesis',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; any price agreed today must equal the average future outcome'},
 {text:'Yes; covered replication eliminates every risk in the underlying deposits'},
]} explanation="Covered pricing matches dated cash flows under trading and funding assumptions. A physical expectation averages future spot outcomes under a probability law. Those are different operations." />

**Covered interest parity (CIP)** compares matched borrowing, deposits and a forward conversion. In the ideal model, home and foreign borrowing/lending use their respective common known rates, the forward delivers on the deposit maturity date, the instruments pay as promised, and there are no trading costs, taxes or balance-sheet constraints. If the required trades are available in both directions,

$$
F_X=X_0\frac{G_d}{G_f}=\frac{1}{30}\frac{1.01}{1.09}=\frac{101}{3270}=0.030886850153\ \text{D/F}.
$$

D 1,000 becomes F 30,000, then F 32,700. Selling all that maturity cash forward produces $32700F_X=\text{D }1010$, matching the home deposit. The reciprocal forward is $F_Q=1/F_X=32.376237624\ \text{F/D}$. Covered execution in real markets needs the bid/ask, funding and collateral analysis from the preceding lesson; a midpoint benchmark gap alone need not be an executable arbitrage. The [BIS account of cross-currency basis](https://www.bis.org/publications/qr-201609/covered-interest-parity-lost-understanding-cross-currency-basis) explains why funding constraints matter.

**Uncovered interest parity (UIP)** additionally hypothesizes equality of expected home-currency deposit returns without the forward. Write $\mathbb E^{P}_0$ for a conditional expectation at the initial date under a **physical probability law P**, meaning assigned probabilities for actual future outcomes. With known Gf,

$$
G_f\frac{\mathbb E^{P}_0[X_T]}{X_0}=G_d,\qquad
\mathbb E^{P}_0[X_T]=X_0\frac{G_d}{G_f}.
$$

Under **both** ideal CIP and this level-UIP hypothesis, $\mathbb E^{P}_0[X_T]=F_X$. This does not say the realized X must equal the forward, nor does it tell us what the physical expectation actually is. Risk premia, beliefs and empirical measurement are additional questions. [CFA Institute's currency reading](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/currency-exchange-rates-understanding-equilibrium-value) distinguishes the parity relationships and carry strategies.

In this example the required expected change **in X** is $1.01/1.09-1=-7.339450\%$. The reciprocal of the required mean level is 32.376238 F/D; we have **not** established that this is $\mathbb E[Q_T]$. An average and a reciprocal cannot usually exchange places. We will calculate that difference shortly.

<FxParityCarryLab client:visible view="parity" title="Compare covered cash with an uncovered outcome" tableLabel="Home, covered foreign and uncovered foreign deposits over one matching year" chartLabel="Maturity home-currency proceeds of three investments with the same initial home amount" caption="Initial rates are 1% home and 9% foreign. Ideal forward conversion fixes covered maturity cash at D 1,010. With terminal Q = 36, uncovered cash is D 908.333333. Moving the outcome does not turn the forward into a guaranteed spot forecast. Deposits and the forward are assumed to perform as promised." />

**Pitfall:** treating a failed expected-return hypothesis as a locked profit. A deviation from UIP leaves an uncertain future conversion; it does not close an arbitrage budget. Nor does the exact level equation above automatically establish an equality between expected log returns: logarithms create another nonlinear expectation.

### When the distinction matters

Use covered replication to check a matched forward under feasible trading terms. Use UIP as a specified expected-return benchmark to investigate. A historical regression test also depends on quote direction, dates, sampling and an expectations model; its rejection is not a universal trading instruction.

## Close the carry ledger with the exact compound return

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="The foreign investment earns 9%, its home-currency price falls 12%, and home financing costs 1%. Is the excess return 8% − 12% = −4%?" hint="The foreign interest is converted at the changed exchange rate too." text="No. The foreign asset return is 1.09 × 0.88 − 1 = −4.08%. Subtract home financing of 1% and the excess on initial funded notional is −5.08%. The missing cross-term is 0.09 × (−0.12) = −1.08 percentage points." buttonLabel="Complete the product" />

Let **N** be the home amount borrowed at the initial date. This is the **funded notional**, the amount used to scale the position. Buy $N/X_0$ foreign units, accrue to $(N/X_0)G_f$, convert at Xₜ and repay NGd. If **C** is a home-currency cost paid at maturity,

$$
\Pi_T=N\left(G_f\frac{X_T}{X_0}-G_d\right)-C.
$$

Here $\Pi_T$ is terminal **profit or loss (P&L)** after that financing and cost. The initial cash budget is $+N-N=0$. Dividing P&L by N gives **excess on initial funded notional**. It is not a return on equity of zero. In contrast, an investor who puts their own N into the foreign deposit has asset return $R_f^{D}=G_fX_T/X_0-1$ before costs, to compare with home return $r_d=G_d-1$.

Define $g=X_T/X_0-1$ and $r_f=G_f-1$. The exact excess is

$$
\frac{\Pi_T}{N}=(r_f-r_d)+g+r_fg-\frac{C}{N}.
$$

For N = D 1,000, rf = 9%, rd = 1%, g = −12% and C = 0: buy F 30,000; receive F 32,700; terminal X is $0.88/30$ D/F; conversion produces D 959.20. Repay D 1,010. The funded P&L is **D −50.80**, or **−5.08%** of initial N. The unlevered foreign asset lost D 40.80, or **−4.08%**. They answer different questions. Adding a terminal fee D 2 changes funded P&L to **D −52.80**, or **−5.28%** of N.

<FxParityCarryLab client:visible view="ledger" title="Reconcile the funded cash budget and its return contributions" tableLabel="Initial financing, foreign conversion, maturity repayment and terminal costs" chartLabel="Signed percentage-point contributions to exact funded-notional excess" caption="Initial foreign-price move −12% gives an asset return of −4.08% and funded-notional excess −5.08%. The bars retain the interest × currency cross-term. Foreign units in the conversion row are the quantity sold; the final row has no foreign inventory or debt left. A zero initial cash balance does not define an equity return." />

The sign also depends on the actual outcome. At Q = 31, terminal cash is D 1,054.838710, leaving **D 44.838710** after D 1,010 repayment. At Q = 36, terminal cash is D 908.333333 and funded P&L is **D −101.666667**. The no-cost break-even terminal quote is $X_T=X_0G_d/G_f$, equivalently Q = 32.376238. With terminal costs, solve $X_T=X_0(G_d+C/N)/G_f$ instead.

**Pitfall:** using the yield spread alone as the trade's return, calling excess return “total asset return”, or quoting a leveraged percentage without its equity denominator. The common additive FX-return expression is an approximation when it drops rf × g. Its error can be material even in a simple one-year example.

### When to use the ledger

When comparing an unhedged deposit with domestic cash, budgeting a financed overlay, or checking a backtest's reported denominator. Date actual fees and funding rather than treating every charge as the same terminal C. Early payments need their financing effects as well.

## Average converted cash rather than invert an average quote

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Invented terminal Q values are 20 and 40, each with probability 50%. Their mean is 30, equal to initial Q. Does that force the foreign investment’s mean home return to equal its 9% local return?" options={[
 {text:'No; conversion uses 1/Q, whose mean differs from 1 divided by mean Q',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; a mean unchanged exchange rate removes every currency effect'},
 {text:'Yes; expected returns ignore the scenario distribution'},
]} explanation="Mean Q is not enough to calculate a payoff proportional to 1/Q. Convert in each state, then average those converted amounts using the assigned probabilities." />

Averages are useful; they are not a universal permission to reorder arithmetic. For these **invented, equally likely** Q outcomes,

$$
\mathbb E[Q_T]=\tfrac12(20)+\tfrac12(40)=30,
$$

$$
\mathbb E[1/Q_T]=\tfrac12(1/20)+\tfrac12(1/40)=0.0375,
\qquad \frac{1}{\mathbb E[Q_T]}=\frac1{30}=0.0333333333.
$$

With initial Q₀ = 30 and Gf = 1.09, the two foreign asset gross returns are $1.09(30/20)=1.635$ and $1.09(30/40)=0.8175$. The asset returns are **+63.5%** and **−18.25%**. Their assigned mean is

$$
\mathbb E[R_f^D]=\tfrac12(0.635)+\tfrac12(-0.1825)=0.22625=22.625\%.
$$

Mean excess over the 1% home return is **21.625%**. Inserting mean Q directly would give $1.09(30/30)-1=9\%$, the wrong expectation. The high mean comes from this particular invented distribution and its nonlinear conversion; it is **not an observable expected carry premium or an estimate for a real currency**.

For positive Q, the function 1/Q is **convex**, meaning its chord lies at or above the curve. Its second derivative is $2/Q^3>0$. **Jensen's inequality** gives $\mathbb E[1/Q]\ge1/\mathbb E[Q]$ when these expectations exist. Equality holds when Q is constant among states with positive probability. This is why a UIP mean condition stated in X cannot simply be inverted into a mean condition in Q.

<FxParityCarryLab client:visible view="expectations" title="Compare the mean conversion with conversion at the mean quote" tableLabel="Two assigned terminal quotes, probabilities and exact converted asset returns" chartLabel="Convex reciprocal conversion function and the difference between mean 1/Q and 1/mean Q" caption="Initial example assigns 50% each to Q = 20 and Q = 40. The cyan point is mean 1/Q, above the blue curve at mean Q. The conditional mean asset return is 22.625%; it follows from this invented law. Equal quotes or probability 0%/100% remove the Jensen gap. The plot’s two quote axes have different units, as labeled." />

**Pitfall:** taking a currency forecast quoted in F/D, inverting its reported mean, and treating that as the mean of D/F. A point forecast, a median and a full probability distribution are different inputs. A positive mean also coexists here with a 50% probability of negative funded P&L.

### When the distribution is necessary

When a cash payoff is nonlinear in the quoted variable. Value each scenario in the reporting currency first; then average under the intended physical law. Pricing weights used to value securities need not be physical probabilities for forecasting returns.

## Fund the equity and survive the intermediate date

<ThinkReveal client:visible prompt="A position would finish above its starting equity if held for a year. Is that enough to establish that a financed investor receives that result?" hint="Check the cash or collateral required before maturity." text="No. A funding agreement can require an earlier close or additional cash. Once the position is closed, a later currency recovery belongs to a different, counterfactual path. A terminal backtest alone does not establish that the financing was sustainable." buttonLabel="Inspect the earlier date" />

**Leverage** here is foreign investment notional divided by initial own equity: $\lambda=N/E$. With own equity E = D 1,000 and λ = 5, invest N = D 5,000 and borrow **D 4,000**. This is different from the previous fully borrowed N overlay. At terminal Q = 36,

$$
W_T=5000(1.09)(30/36)-4000(1.01)=4541.666667-4040=501.666667.
$$

Final **equity wealth Wₜ** is assets minus debt, and its return is $W_T/E-1=-49.833333\%$. The unlevered asset return was −9.166667%. For terminal costs C,

$$
\frac{W_T}{E}-1=r_d+\lambda(R_f^D-r_d)-\frac CE.
$$

The home-rate term appears because the investor contributes E of their own money. Losses are not clipped at zero wealth: with sufficient borrowing, asset proceeds can fail to repay debt. Limited liability or bankruptcy treatment would require separate contractual and legal assumptions.

Now use a **different path**: Q = 38 at half a year, recovering to Q = 31 at one year. Assume fractional-year accrual uses $G^t$, and the asset and loan can actually be closed at their accrued balances with no fees or spread. At the half-year check,

$$
A_{1/2}=5000\sqrt{1.09}(30/38)=4121.173622,
$$

$$
B_{1/2}=4000\sqrt{1.01}=4019.950248,\qquad
W_{1/2}=101.223373.
$$

Invent a contract requiring closure when equity is **strictly below 20% of initial equity**, D 200, at this check. It triggers. Convert the investment, repay the loan and hold the remaining D 101.223373 in home cash for the final half-year. Terminal wealth is $101.223373\sqrt{1.01}\approx\text{D }101.728231$. If the balance were negative, it would remain a funded debt in this ideal model. It would not disappear.

Had the investor been able to hold instead, terminal equity at Q = 31 would have been $5000(1.09)(30/31)-4040=\text{D }1234.193548$. That attractive later figure is **unavailable after the stipulated closure**. The two results correspond to different trading paths.

**Pitfall:** reading an initial-equity floor as a running-peak drawdown limit, a universal broker rule or a guaranteed loss cap. The threshold, timing, collateral valuation, closeout access and legal recourse are contract-specific. Real gaps, illiquidity, deposit restrictions or funding repricing can make closing at the displayed mark impossible. A stop order does not guarantee the price needed to preserve a floor.

### When to include the path

When a strategy uses borrowing, collateral or other intermediate obligations. Specify how gains and losses are funded, what triggers a cash call or closure, and the subsequent cash position. A smooth terminal scenario table can miss a funding failure halfway through.

## Treat the carry premium as a question about risk and probabilities

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Two analysts agree on the 9% foreign and 1% home rates but assign different probabilities to a currency loss. Must their expected carry excess have the same sign?" options={[
 {text:'No; the yield difference does not determine the probability-weighted currency payoff',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes; a positive rate difference forces positive expected excess'},
 {text:'Yes; negative skew determines the expected return without probabilities'},
]} explanation="Expected excess depends on the converted payoff distribution and costs as well as rates. A positive spread is not an observed physical probability law." />

The **forward premium puzzle** refers to empirical difficulties of uncovered-parity predictions and familiar exchange-rate regressions in specified samples. It does not mean every high-rate currency appreciates or every carry strategy has positive expected profit. Nor can an observed past average directly reveal today's conditional physical mean. Quote conventions, horizons, expectation errors and the model used in the test matter.

Use a deliberately simple two-state law. In the **calm state**, terminal Q = 30, so funded excess is $1.09(30/30)-1.01=8\%$. In the **adverse state**, Q = 45, so excess is $1.09(30/45)-1.01=-28.333333\%$. Assigning 90% calm probability gives

$$
\mathbb E[\Pi_T/N]=0.9(0.08)+0.1(-0.2833333333)=4.366667\%.
$$

Assigning 70% instead gives $0.7(0.08)+0.3(-0.2833333333)=-2.9\%$. The rate sheet did not change. The invented physical law did. Costs would reduce both results.

The 90% case has **negative skewness**, a standardized third central moment: occasional large negative outcomes pull its tail to the loss side. Its standard deviation is 10.9 percentage points and skewness is −2.666667. These are properties of this two-state example, not estimates for all carry portfolios. If one state has probability one, dispersion is zero and standardized skewness is undefined; the simulator says so.

<FxParityCarryLab client:visible view="risk" title="Change the probability law while keeping the rate difference" tableLabel="Assigned calm and adverse currency outcomes with their funded-notional excess returns" chartLabel="Mean funded-notional excess in percentage points across assigned calm-state probabilities" caption="Initial invented law: calm Q = 30 with probability 90%, adverse Q = 45 with probability 10%. **Risk premium** means compensation investors require for bearing risk in a specified model; observing a positive historical mean does not identify its cause. [Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen's 2008 NBER working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14473), documents negative-skew and funding-related unwind evidence in its sample and studies a model consistent with those mechanisms. It does not establish that every carry loss has one cause or that another sample must have a positive premium. [NBIM's discussion of currency carry](https://www.nbim.no/en/news-and-insights/publications/discussion-notes/2014/currency-carry-trade/) also considers several explanations and prolonged loss periods.

### When evidence helps and where it stops

Use historical evidence to choose scenarios, investigate funding channels and assess whether a proposed expectation is plausible. Test sensitivity to probabilities, costs, dependence, sample choice and changing regimes. Positions can move together in stress, but correlation does not universally become one. Diversification and volatility-based sizing change exposures; neither guarantees a warning before a gap or a favorable next return. Distinguish a conditional empirical finding from a theorem and a risky forecast from a complete arbitrage budget.
