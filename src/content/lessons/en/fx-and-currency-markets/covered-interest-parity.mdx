A screen shows a forward above your textbook calculation. Have you found a profitable covered trade? First identify which price you can trade and which interest rate you can borrow at. A midquote and an accessible funding price can give very different answers.

**Covered interest parity (CIP)** compares two ways of reaching the same future currency amount: deposit at home, or convert, deposit abroad and fix the conversion back with a forward. We will derive the ideal equality, then build the actual cash-flow tests with separate borrowing and deposit rates and bid–ask prices. All quotes and amounts below are invented.

We choose **USD as domestic currency** and **EUR as foreign currency**. Both spot $S$ and forward $F$ are quoted in **USD per one EUR**. These are labels for the calculation, not a claim about where the learner lives.

A **spot exchange** exchanges currencies on an agreed initial value date. A **forward** fixes an exchange rate now for a specified later value date. In our model, all deposits and loans begin on that initial value date and mature on the forward’s date. The time interval need not start on the trading date.

Unit cancellation tells you which operation to use:

$$\frac{1200\ \mathrm{USD}}{1.20\ \mathrm{USD/EUR}}=1000\ \mathrm{EUR}$$

$$1030\ \mathrm{EUR}\times F\ \mathrm{USD/EUR}=1030F\ \mathrm{USD}$$

### When to use it

Write currency units on every intermediate balance. A forward covering 1,030 EUR is different from one covering the original 1,000 EUR: interest changes the amount that must be converted. Match start dates, maturity dates, settlement conventions and the amount delivered before comparing investment paths.

## Two matched cash-flow paths derive the ideal forward

Let $G_d$ and $G_f$ be **gross growth factors over the same interval**: one unit deposited becomes $G$ units at maturity. For the first example, the interval is one year and the fixed one-year returns are 5% in USD and 3% in EUR, so $G_d=1.05$ and $G_f=1.03$.

Think of two routes ending at the same station. Under the idealized assumptions, both start with the same USD amount and must deliver the same USD payoff. The assumptions matter: one accessible rate for borrowing and depositing in each currency, no trading spreads or fees, a forward with no upfront premium, matched dates, comparable credit and collateral terms, and full performance of the contracts.

### Follow 1,200 USD through both routes

| Route and step | Initial value date | Maturity |
|---|---|---|
| A: deposit USD | Invest 1,200 USD | Receive 1,200 ×1.05 = **1,260 USD** |
| B: buy EUR at spot | Pay 1,200 USD; receive 1,000 EUR | — |
| B: deposit EUR | Invest 1,000 EUR | Receive 1,030 EUR |
| B: sell all future EUR forward | Contract now to sell 1,030 EUR at F | Deliver 1,030 EUR; receive 1,030 × F USD |

For a general initial domestic amount $X$, the covered foreign route ends with $XG_fF/S$ USD. Equating it with $XG_d$ gives

$$XG_d=\frac{X}{S}G_fF\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F^*=S\frac{G_d}{G_f}$$

For the example,

$$F^*=1.20\frac{1.05}{1.03}=1.2233009709\ldots\ \mathrm{USD/EUR}$$

The covered route gives 1,030 ×1.2233009709… = **1,260 USD**, matching the USD deposit. Use the unrounded forward for that exact equality. A displayed 1.2233 is rounded.

### What “covered” removes

The forward fixes the exchange rate for the specified terminal EUR amount. The unknown future spot rate does not enter that conversion **if the deposits and forward perform as assumed**. It does not remove counterparty default, an unexpected cash need, collateral calls or a mismatch between the hedged amount and actual proceeds. “Covered” identifies the exchange exposure being hedged; it is not a synonym for every risk disappearing.

### When to use it

Use the equality as a replication benchmark when its financing and trading assumptions apply. It contains no equation requiring the realized future spot to equal $F^*$. A traded forward can reflect funding, collateral and market conditions; this pricing argument does not establish that it is an unbiased forecast. [The BIS explains the matched cash-market and forward mechanics and their ideal assumptions](https://www.bis.org/publications/qr-201609/covered-interest-parity-lost-understanding-cross-currency-basis).

## Accrual factors and the quote determine the premium

If rates are annual **simple-accrual rates**, construct each gross factor using its own contractual year fraction:

$$G_d=1+r_d\tau_d,\qquad G_f=1+r_f\tau_f,\qquad F^*=S\frac{1+r_d\tau_d}{1+r_f\tau_f}$$

The same dates do not necessarily imply the same year fraction. As a separate 90-calendar-day example, explicitly choose USD Actual/365 Fixed and EUR Actual/360, with simple rates 5% and 3%:

$$G_d=1+0.05\frac{90}{365}=1.0123287671\ldots$$

$$G_f=1+0.03\frac{90}{360}=1.0075$$

At $S=1.20$, the forward is **1.2057513852 USD/EUR**. These day counts and simple accrual are invented contractual choices, not universal conventions for USD or EUR products. A compounding quote needs its corresponding growth factor instead.

### Premium belongs to a currency in a quote

With USD per EUR, $F>S$ means **EUR is at a forward premium**: buying one EUR costs more USD forward than spot. Under the ideal equality and positive gross factors,

$$F>S\iff G_d>G_f$$

In the one-year example, the foreign EUR return is lower, and its forward premium is

$$\frac{F^*}{S}-1=\frac{1.05}{1.03}-1\approx1.9417476\%$$

This is not exactly the two-percentage-point interest difference, and it is not a promised EUR spot appreciation.

Reverse the quote and both prices must be inverted: spot is 1/1.20 ≈ **0.8333333 EUR/USD**, while the forward is 1/1.2233009709… ≈ **0.8174603 EUR/USD**. Now USD is at a forward discount. Its relative discount is about 1.9047619%, which is not the same percentage with a minus sign because the denominator changed.

| Ideal one-year returns | Forward in USD/EUR | EUR relative to USD |
|---|---:|---|
| USD 5%, EUR 3% | 1.2233009709… | Forward premium |
| USD 4%, EUR 4% | 1.2000000000 | Neither premium nor discount |
| USD 3%, EUR 6% | 1.1660377358… | Forward discount |

### When to use it

Compare gross factors over the same matched horizon. The familiar low-rate-currency premium result belongs to the frictionless model with consistent return conventions. An observed market quote with funding spreads and a basis need not obey a universal ordering inferred from two headline annual rates.

## Borrowing, depositing, buying and selling use different prices

An executable comparison separates **deposit returns** from **borrowing costs**. Let $D_d,D_f$ be gross deposit factors and $B_d,B_f$ gross loan-repayment factors. The letters identify a funding direction, not two names for the same rate.

The FX **bid** is the price at which the dealer buys foreign currency from you; the **ask** is the price at which the dealer sells it to you. Under USD per EUR, you **buy EUR at the ask** and **sell EUR at the bid**, both spot and forward. The [IMF’s FX glossary defines this bid–ask distinction and discusses the cross-currency basis](https://meetings.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/GFSR/2025/October/English/ch2annex.ashx).

### Set a separate one-year execution example

| Input | Invented executable value |
|---|---:|
| Spot bid / ask, USD per EUR | 1.1990 /1.2010 |
| USD deposit / borrowing return | 4% /6% |
| EUR deposit / borrowing return | 2% /4% |
| Forward bid / ask, USD per EUR | 1.2300 /1.2320 |
| Gross factors | Dd 1.04; Bd 1.06; Df 1.02; Bf 1.04 |

All loans and deposits have fixed one-year returns for matching dates. We first exclude other costs to isolate these quoted cash flows. These rates differ from the ideal benchmark’s 5% and 3%; an illustrative mid-reference rate is not automatically a rate available for both borrowing and depositing.

### Fund in USD and sell the EUR proceeds forward

Borrow $X=1{,}000{,}000$ USD. Buy EUR at **spot ask 1.2010**, not the bid. Deposit the EUR at **2%**, not its 4% loan rate. Sell the exact future EUR amount at **forward bid 1.2300**:

| Leg | Initial value date | Maturity |
|---|---|---|
| USD loan | +1,000,000 USD | Repay 1,060,000 USD |
| Buy EUR at ask | −1,000,000 USD; +832,639.467111… EUR | — |
| EUR deposit | Invest 832,639.467111… EUR | Receive 849,292.256453… EUR |
| Sell deposit proceeds forward at bid | Contract at the outset | Deliver 849,292.256453… EUR; receive 1,044,629.475437… USD |
| Net listed cash flows | Zero net in both currencies | **−15,370.524563… USD** |

The terminal USD remainder is

$$\Pi_d=X\left(\frac{F_{\mathrm{bid}}D_f}{S_{\mathrm{ask}}}-B_d\right)$$

Substitution gives a **15,370.52 USD deficit** before other costs. The forward bid exceeds the earlier ideal 1.2233009709… benchmark, yet this funded route loses money at the executable inputs.

### Fund in EUR and buy the EUR repayment forward

For a comparable domestic spot amount, borrow $E=X/S_{\mathrm{bid}}=1{,}000{,}000/1.1990\approx834{,}028.356964$ EUR. Sell it at spot **bid**, producing 1,000,000 USD; deposit those USD at **4%** to receive 1,040,000 USD. The EUR loan requires $EB_f\approx867{,}389.491243$ EUR at maturity. Buy that amount forward at **ask 1.2320**, costing 1,068,623.853211… USD, and use the delivered EUR to repay the loan.

The USD remainder is

$$\Pi_f=E\left(S_{\mathrm{bid}}D_d-B_fF_{\mathrm{ask}}\right)=X\left(D_d-\frac{B_fF_{\mathrm{ask}}}{S_{\mathrm{bid}}}\right)$$

This route has a **28,623.85 USD deficit** before other costs. It borrows a different currency, and its EUR principal was deliberately chosen to yield the same initial USD spot proceeds. Currency labels prevent comparing unlike notionals by accident.

### When to use it

Draw both loan-repayment amounts and forward deliveries. A zero net initial cash flow in this simplified funding ledger does not establish zero collateral, capital or liquidity needs in practice. An exchange hedge also depends on the counterparties performing. Use firm simultaneous prices and financing terms available to the same participant before interpreting a calculated remainder.

## The funded-route bounds replace a single midprice test

Require the first route’s listed remainder not to be positive. Since the gross factors and spot prices are positive,

$$\Pi_d\leq0\iff F_{\mathrm{bid}}\leq U,\qquad U=S_{\mathrm{ask}}\frac{B_d}{D_f}$$

For the opposite route,

$$\Pi_f\leq0\iff F_{\mathrm{ask}}\geq L,\qquad L=S_{\mathrm{bid}}\frac{D_d}{B_f}$$

These are two **executable-price bounds** for the two listed strategies. When borrowing is no cheaper than depositing in each currency and spot bid does not exceed ask, $L\leq U$. If spreads and borrowing/deposit differences vanish, both bounds collapse to $SG_d/G_f$.

### Calculate the thresholds

For the execution example,

$$L=1.1990\frac{1.04}{1.04}=1.1990$$

$$U=1.2010\frac{1.06}{1.02}\approx1.2480980392$$

The quoted forward bid 1.2300 is below $U$, and ask 1.2320 is above $L$. Neither listed funded direction has a positive terminal remainder. This conclusion tests these routes; it is not a proof that every risk or possible transaction has been examined.

| Forward bid / ask | USD-funded remainder | EUR-funded remainder, reported in USD |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1.1800 /1.1820 | −57,835.14 USD | +14,745.62 USD |
| 1.2300 /1.2320 | −15,370.52 USD | −28,623.85 USD |
| 1.2600 /1.2620 | +10,108.24 USD | −54,645.54 USD |

The same 1,000,000 USD initial funding normalization is used in all rows. Move the forward bid control to 1.180 or 1.260 to reproduce the corresponding rows; its ask stays 0.002 higher. A positive listed remainder identifies a direction worth checking against **all omitted costs and execution constraints**, not an automatic promise of riskless profit.

### Compare with the frictionless arbitrage example

If instead the original ideal rates 5%/3% are available for both borrowing and depositing, with one spot 1.20 and no costs, a forward 1.2350 gives

$$1{,}000{,}000\left(\frac{1.2350(1.03)}{1.20}-1.05\right)\approx10{,}041.67\ \mathrm{USD}$$

That textbook surplus follows under its stated executable and full-performance assumptions. Applying it to the different borrowing/deposit/bid–ask example silently substitutes the very inputs that must be checked.

### When to use it

Test **bid against the upper bound** and **ask against the lower bound**. A midpoint outside a chosen benchmark is not the relevant trading price. Real credit, collateral, settlement, balance-sheet and market-access terms can widen practical constraints further; the ideal equality remains a useful reference when its assumptions are kept visible.

## A basis needs a reference curve and a sign convention

A **cross-currency basis** describes a wedge between FX-implied funding and selected cash-market reference rates. Its reported sign depends on the currency quote, which leg receives a spread and the instrument’s quotation convention. It is not simply “forward minus spot,” and there is no universal rule that it must always be negative or small.

The BIS documents the role of hedging demand and intermediary balance-sheet constraints in basis deviations; its [2025 derivatives-market review discusses covered basis trades separately from unhedged carry trades](https://www.bis.org/publications/qr-202512/international-finance-through-lens-bis-statistics-derivatives-markets). A hedge demand or funding premium can persist without implying that every participant has access to a costless trade.

### Compute an explicitly defined one-period residual

For a **separate simple one-year reference calculation**, use $S=1.20$, $F=1.23$, $r_d=0.05$ and $r_f=0.03$. Define an additive spread **on the foreign return** by

$$F=S\frac{1+r_d}{1+r_f+b_f}$$

Then

$$b_f=\frac{S(1+r_d)}{F}-1-r_f=\frac{1.26}{1.23}-1.03\approx-0.0056097561$$

That is **−56.0976 bp** under this declared foreign-leg equation. If instead a spread is added to the domestic reference return,

$$F=S\frac{1+r_d+b_d}{1+r_f}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_d=\frac{F}{S}(1+r_f)-1-r_d=0.00575$$

Now the residual is **+57.5 bp**. These are different definitions of a residual on different legs, not contradictory observations. Actual floating-rate cross-currency basis-swap quotations use their own curves, payment schedules and collateral conventions; these elementary residuals are not universal market-quote conversions.

### When to use it

State the reference rates, dates, currency units, leg, accrual and sign convention before interpreting a basis. Separate a benchmark residual from the profit of an executable funded trade. The bid–ask example already demonstrates why a quoted gap can coexist with no positive listed remainder. Persistent deviations can reflect funding and balance-sheet constraints as well as other pricing differences; their presence alone does not identify one exclusive cause.
