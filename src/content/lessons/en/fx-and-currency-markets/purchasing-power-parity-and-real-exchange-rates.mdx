Two currencies can exchange at a stable nominal rate while the purchasing power of their goods moves apart. Conversely, a currency can look expensive against a basket benchmark without offering a timed, executable trading opportunity.

This lesson connects the currency quote to goods prices. Every number below is invented. We distinguish a definition that can be calculated from observed inputs, a parity hypothesis that adds assumptions, and a statistical price comparison whose purpose need not be currency trading.

Fix the quote: **S is USD per one EUR**. At S=1.10, a EUR 100 purchase costs USD 110 before other costs. Multiply a EUR amount by S to obtain USD. The unit suffix USD/EUR in our displays means dollars per euro; the corresponding market pair name is commonly EUR/USD, with EUR as base currency. Written units prevent a pair-name convention from silently reversing a calculation.

Let $P_D$ be the USD price of a domestic basket and $P_F$ the EUR price of a foreign basket with comparable quantities and quality. **Absolute purchasing power parity (PPP)** imposes equal basket cost after conversion:

$$S_{\mathrm{PPP}}P_F=P_D,\qquad S_{\mathrm{PPP}}=\frac{P_D}{P_F}$$

For a USD 120 basket and its EUR 100 counterpart, the benchmark is $120/100=1.20$ USD per EUR. At the selected market S=1.10, the foreign basket converts to $1.10(100)=110$ USD. It costs 10 USD less than the domestic basket before any other differences or costs.

That is a price comparison, not a forward contract. Official PPP statistics aggregate carefully specified products and expenditure weights; one basket arithmetic exercise is not an estimate of GDP-wide PPP. [The OECD explains comparable volume and quality, product-level ratios and aggregate PPPs](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/insights/data-explainers/2024/06/purchasing-power-parities---frequently-asked-questions-faqs.html).

### When to use it

Use a PPP conversion to compare purchasing power under a defined basket and date. Check the consumption, GDP or other expenditure concept, price collection and weights. A time-series consumer price index with base 100 is a different object; its level does not tell you that the basket costs 100 units of currency.

## A real exchange rate compares goods prices in a common currency

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"With q = S × foreign price / domestic price, what does a rise in q mean?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "Foreign goods become costlier relative to domestic goods",

  {
    "text": "Foreign goods become cheaper relative to domestic goods",
    "correct": false

  {

    "correct": false
  }

  explanation={"Under this chosen quote and formula, a rise is domestic real depreciation."}
  pretest
/>


For the same quote and comparable baskets, define the bilateral **real exchange-rate ratio**

$$q=\frac{S P_F}{P_D}$$

The numerator converts the foreign basket to USD. Dividing by the domestic USD basket cost cancels currency units. Under this convention, q above one means the foreign basket is more expensive; q below one means it is cheaper. q=1 corresponds to absolute PPP for these baskets. [The IMF derives the same nominal-rate and price-level construction](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/series/back-to-basics/real-exchange-rates).

At S=1.10, q is $110/120=11/12\approx0.916667$. At S=1.40 with unchanged basket prices, q becomes $140/120=7/6\approx1.166667$. A rise in this q is a **real depreciation of the domestic USD**: foreign goods become more expensive relative to domestic goods. Some published measures use the inverse convention, so inspect the formula before interpreting a rising index.

### Reciprocal percentages use different denominators

The EUR price relative to the basket benchmark changes by $1.10/1.20-1=-1/12$, or −8.333333%. The USD value in EUR uses reciprocal quotes:

$$\frac{1/1.10}{1/1.20}-1=\frac{1.20}{1.10}-1=\frac1{11}\approx9.090909\%$$

Those percentages are not exact negatives because their starting denominators differ. They describe a gap against this benchmark, not an observed future return or proof of fundamental mispricing.

### When to use it

Name the domestic currency, foreign currency, quote direction, basket and formula. Distinguish a bilateral ratio from a published real effective exchange-rate index, which combines partners using a stated methodology. An index's numerical level and direction need its metadata; 100 is often a normalization, not evidence of PPP.

## Relative PPP holds a real ratio constant over time

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Domestic and foreign inflation are 5% and 2%. Is the relative-PPP nominal change exactly 3%?"}

  {

    "correct": false

  explanation={"The exact change is (0.05 − 0.02) / 1.02; the simple difference is an approximation."}
  pretest

Let the two price levels grow over one matched interval by factors $1+\pi_D$ and $1+\pi_F$, where each inflation rate is greater than −100%. Taking the ratio of real rates gives an accounting identity:

$$\frac{q_1}{q_0}=\frac{S_1}{S_0}\frac{1+\pi_F}{1+\pi_D}$$

**Relative PPP** adds the assumption $q_1=q_0$. Solving for the nominal quote gives

$$\frac{S_1}{S_0}=\frac{1+\pi_D}{1+\pi_F},\qquad\frac{S_1}{S_0}-1=\frac{\pi_D-\pi_F}{1+\pi_F}$$

With domestic inflation 5%, foreign inflation 2% and S₀=1.20, the constant-real-ratio nominal quote is

$$S_1=1.20\frac{1.05}{1.02}=\frac{21}{17}\approx1.23529412\ \mathrm{USD/EUR}$$

The exact increase in USD per EUR is $1.05/1.02-1=1/34\approx2.941176\%$. The EUR appreciates nominally against the USD; the domestic currency depreciates. The small-inflation approximation is $\pi_D-\pi_F=3\%$, not an exact equality.

Relative PPP need not establish absolute PPP. If S₀ were 1.10 with the same starting baskets, q₀ would be 11/12. A new S₁ of $1.10(1.05/1.02)=77/68\approx1.132353$ would preserve q at 11/12, rather than move it to one.

### When to use it

Use matched realized inflation changes to assess a relative-PPP restriction, or explicitly supplied inflation scenarios to calculate its implication. Plugging inflation forecasts into a nonlinear ratio is a scenario calculation; it need not equal the expected future spot when inflation is uncertain. Constant q is a hypothesis, not a promised nominal-rate outcome. [CFA Institute distinguishes parity conditions from the difficulty of assessing and forecasting currencies](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/currency-exchange-rates-understanding-equilibrium-value).

## CPI indices identify changes without identifying comparable price levels

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Both countries set CPI to 100 in their base year. What can you infer about their basket price levels?"}

    "text": "Both baskets must cost 100 units of their currencies",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "Their market exchange rate must be exactly one",
    "correct": false

  explanation={"An arbitrary index base describes a reference, not a comparable price level."}
  pretest
/>


A **consumer price index (CPI)** tracks prices over time relative to its own reference base. Two countries can both publish CPI 100 while their comparable baskets cost very different amounts. They can also cover different goods and expenditure weights. Setting both starting indices to 100 does not impose absolute PPP.

If the domestic CPI goes from 100 to 105 and the foreign CPI from 100 to 102, their respective growth factors are 1.05 and 1.02. For a CPI-based real-rate change index constructed from those factors, with unchanged nominal S,

$$\frac{q_1}{q_0}=1\frac{102/100}{105/100}=\frac{34}{35}\approx0.971429$$

Normalize a real-rate **change index** to 100 initially. It ends at $100(34/35)\approx97.142857$, a decline of $1/35\approx2.857143\%$. Under our q convention this is domestic real appreciation, even though the nominal currency quote did not move.

Now rebase only the foreign CPI to 200 initially and 204 finally:

$$\frac{204}{200}=\frac{102}{100}=1.02$$

The normalized real change remains 34/35. By contrast, a raw expression S × foreign CPI / domestic CPI would double if you changed only that CPI's base. That raw level is not a comparable-basket q, and its distance from one does not measure an absolute-PPP gap.

### When to use it

Use each index relative to its own starting value when calculating a change. Inspect baskets, weighting, dates, seasonal treatment and revisions. To make a price-level comparison across countries, use suitable comparable-price information or a specifically constructed PPP measure, not arbitrary CPI base numbers. Rebasing should not change the growth answer.

## Costs and non-tradables limit the one-price argument

A lower converted sticker price need not be a lower delivered cost. For one invented identical, transferable item, retain domestic price USD 120, foreign price EUR 100 and an executable conversion of 1.10 USD per EUR. Add a supplied one-way delivery cost of USD 15, with other differences held aside:

$$\text{landed foreign cost}=1.10(100)+15=125\ \mathrm{USD}$$

The domestic price minus that cost is $120-125=-5$ USD. The original 10 USD sticker-price gap is insufficient to establish a gain even in this limited comparison. The real basket ratio q still uses the unadjusted comparable prices; adding a delivery assumption does not redefine it.

For broad baskets, the issue goes beyond delivery. Housing and many personal services are not readily transported across borders. Quality, local taxes, margins, consumption weights and relative productivity can differ. Real relative prices can change with fundamentals; a persistent gap is not automatically an error waiting to disappear. [The IMF discusses non-tradables, frictions and changes in equilibrium real rates](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/series/back-to-basics/real-exchange-rates).

### When to use it

Treat the basket benchmark as one reference alongside its construction and economic context. A trading claim also needs executable bid/ask prices, funding, delivery, resale, scale and risk assumptions. PPP does not supply a convergence date, a carry return or protection from losses while a gap persists.

The [covered-parity lesson](/fx-and-currency-markets/covered-interest-parity) derives a forward from matched currency funding cash flows. PPP compares goods prices. The real-rate formula measures a relative price ratio. Their inputs and conclusions differ.

Keep S₀=1.20. Under an ideal covered-parity example with one-period USD funding 4% and EUR funding 3%,

$$F^*=1.20\frac{1.04}{1.03}\approx1.21165049\ \mathrm{USD/EUR}$$

Under the separate inflation scenario of 5% and 2%, the relative-PPP quote needed to keep q constant is 1.23529412. These are different calculations; neither makes that number the certain future spot.

Suppose the observed terminal spot is instead 1.18. The realized real-rate change identity remains valid:

$$\frac{q_1}{q_0}=\frac{1.18}{1.20}\frac{1.02}{1.05}=\frac{1003}{1050}\approx0.955238$$

That is about −4.476190% for this q, or domestic real appreciation under our convention. Calculating the identity does not require either PPP or uncovered interest parity to predict correctly.
