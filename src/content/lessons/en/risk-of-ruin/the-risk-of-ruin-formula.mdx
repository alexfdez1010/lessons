A player has €1,000 and loses exactly €100 on each losing bet. Another player loses 10% of whatever remains. Their first loss looks identical. After that, the first player's losses stay at €100; the second player's losses shrink. A formula that quietly swaps these rules midway through the calculation has already lost the game.

We will calculate the probability of reaching a **specified floor**, under a **specified sizing rule**, over a **specified horizon**. Those three choices come before the percentage on the calculator.

## Count the distance to a fixed floor

Think of a staircase with a locked exit at the bottom. A win moves you one step up; a loss moves you one step down. Reaching the exit ends the experiment. This **absorbing floor** cannot be crossed and then forgotten while you wait for a recovery.

Let $W_0$ be initial wealth, $F$ the fixed monetary floor and $u>0$ the fixed monetary gain or loss per bet. Measure distance in units of $u$:

$$
N=\frac{W_0-F}{u}.
$$

For the clean walk, $N$ is a positive integer and every step is exactly $+1$ or $-1$. The floor lies on the same grid as the possible balances. A non-integer distance, unequal payoffs or losses that jump across the floor need an explicitly adapted model; rounding $N$ to the nearest integer does not solve those problems.

| Rule | Start | After one loss | After two losses | After three losses |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Fixed €100 loss | €1,000 | €900 | €800 | €700 |
| Lose 10% of current wealth | €1,000 | €900 | €810 | €729 |

With a €400 floor, the first rule starts six fixed units above it. Six immediate losses reach €400, but **ruin does not require six consecutive losses**. Wins and losses can be interleaved. Starting three steps above zero, the path $3\to2\to3\to2\to1\to0$ reaches the floor without three consecutive losses.

The formula below concerns independent, identically distributed bets: each has the same win probability $p$, loss probability $q=1-p$, and fixed payoff magnitude $u$. It includes no costs, deposits, withdrawals, changing stake, upper profit target or compulsory deadline. Costs can be included only by specifying a new increment model; subtracting a fee generally destroys the exact $\pm1$ structure.

### When to use this model

Use it to study an ideal fixed cash stake, or to understand how a buffer and a positive edge interact. Before applying it to an account, write down the actual floor and loss rule. A stop-loss budget is a planned loss amount; it does not establish that every realised loss will equal that amount.

## The exact infinite-horizon walk

<MCQ client:visible pretest

For this walk, **eventual ruin** means reaching the floor at any finite time, with no final date and no upper target. The exact probability is

$$
r_N=\begin{cases}
\left(\dfrac{q}{p}\right)^N,&p>q,\\
1,&p\le q.
\end{cases}
$$

At the floor itself, $r_0=1$. If $p=1$ and $N>0$, losses are impossible and $r_N=0$. If $p\le0.5$, every finite starting distance is eventually absorbed with probability one in this model. That is an infinite-horizon statement, not a claim that ruin must happen during next month's trading. The classical result and its finite-upper-target version are derived in [Grinstead and Snell's probability textbook](https://stats.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Probability_Theory/Introductory_Probability_(Grinstead_and_Snell)/12:_Random_Walks/12.02:_Gambler's_Ruin).

For $p=0.55$, $q=0.45$ and $q/p=9/11\approx0.8181818$. With a €100 step and a floor €1,000 below the start, $N=10$:

$$
r_{10}=\left(\frac{0.45}{0.55}\right)^{10}
=\left(\frac9{11}\right)^{10}
\approx0.1344306=13.4431\%.
$$

| Initial distance $N$ | Exact expression | Eventual ruin |
| ---: | --- | ---: |
| 5 | $(9/11)^5$ | 36.6648% |
| 10 | $(9/11)^{10}$ | 13.4431% |
| 20 | $(9/11)^{20}$ | 1.8072% |
| 40 | $(9/11)^{40}$ | 0.0327% |

Every extra unit multiplies the probability by $q/p$. At the same $p>0.5$, doubling the distance **exactly squares the probability**: $r_{2N}=r_N^2$. Square the decimal probability: $0.1344306^2\approx0.0180716$, which is 1.8072%, not 1.8072 as a probability. The initial €100 step stays fixed throughout each run; this calculation does not rebalance it to 10% of wealth.

### A deadline changes the event

Let $T$ be the first bet on which the floor is reached. The infinite-horizon result is $P(T<\infty)$. A three-bet limit asks for $P(T\le3)$. Starting three units above the floor, only three immediate losses can reach it by then:

$$
P(T\le3)=q^3=0.45^3=0.091125=9.1125\%.
$$

The eventual probability is much larger:

$$
P(T<\infty)=(9/11)^3\approx54.7708\%.
$$

For instance, the five-bet path loss–win–loss–loss–loss also hits the floor. Its probability is $q\,p\,q^3=pq^4=0.0225534375$, or 2.2553%; it is absent from the three-bet calculation. Other first-hitting paths add further probability. Do not confuse a terminal balance below a threshold with having touched it earlier: a path absorbed earlier cannot recover in this experiment.

A finite upper target is another change. In a fair walk starting at $N$ and ending at either 0 or $M$, ruin before the target is $(M-N)/M$. With $N=20$, $M=100$, that is 80%, whereas playing indefinitely without the target gives 100%.

<Reveal client:visible>
  <details className="rounded-card border border-ink-100 bg-surface px-4 py-3">
    <summary className="cursor-pointer font-medium">Why does the power appear?</summary>

    Conditioning on the next step gives $r_N=p\,r_{N+1}+q\,r_{N-1}$. Try a geometric solution $r_N=x^N$: $px^2-x+q=(px-q)(x-1)=0$. For $0<p<1$ and $p\ne q$, the solutions are a constant and $(q/p)^N$. With an upper absorbing target $M$, the boundary values $r_0=1$ and $r_M=0$ give
    $$
r_N^{(M)}=\frac{(q/p)^N-(q/p)^M}{1-(q/p)^M}.
$$
    For $p>q$, send $M$ to infinity: $(q/p)^M\to0$, leaving $(q/p)^N$. For $p<q$, the same limit is 1. At $p=q$, the finite-target result $1-N/M$ also tends to 1. The upper-target limit justifies the infinite-horizon boundary; it is not an extra payoff assumption.
  </details>
</Reveal>

### When to use the exact probability

Use it for the stated walk and event, and as a benchmark for a simulator. For a fixed deadline, compute first-hitting probabilities up to that deadline or simulate that same horizon. Adding a profit target, changing the stake or allowing unequal wins and losses changes the problem.

## An additive Brownian model with matching units

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="A candidate exponent contains drift in euros per day, distance in euros and variance in euros squared per day. Which combination has no units?"
  options={[

    { text: 'Drift × distance ÷ standard deviation' },

  explanation="(€/day) × € ÷ (€/day × €) = 1. A dimensionless exponent is necessary, although units alone do not prove a probabilistic formula."
/>

A continuous approximation replaces discrete steps with a noisy additive motion. Write the distance above a fixed floor as

$$
X_t=d+\mu t+\sigma B_t.
$$

Here $d>0$ is the initial monetary distance, $\mu$ is the mean monetary gain per unit of time, and $B_t$ is **standard Brownian motion**, whose independent increments over a time interval of length $h$ are normal with mean zero and variance $h$. Consequently $\sigma^2$ is the monetary **variance per unit of time**, while $\sigma$ has units of money divided by the square root of time. Mean and variance scale with elapsed time; the money changes additively, rather than as a percentage of current wealth.

For $\mu>0$ and $\sigma^2>0$, the probability of ever touching zero is

$$
P\!\left(\inf_{t\ge0}X_t\le0\right)
=\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\mu d}{\sigma^2}\right).
$$

This is exact **for that Brownian model**. Used for discrete bets or market returns, it is an approximation whose suitability needs checking. For $\mu\le0$ with nonzero diffusion, eventual floor hitting is one. [Ward Whitt's Columbia lecture notes](https://www.columbia.edu/~ww2040/4106S11/lec0421.pdf) derive the Brownian two-barrier probability; letting the upper target grow without bound gives the expression above. This limiting step and the comparisons below are our calculations.

<Reveal client:visible>
  <details className="rounded-card border border-ink-100 bg-surface px-4 py-3">
    <summary className="cursor-pointer font-medium">A short derivation in distance coordinates</summary>

    Let $h(d)$ be the eventual hitting probability. The Brownian backward equation is $\mu h'(d)+(\sigma^2/2)h''(d)=0$. For positive drift, the bounded solution with $h(0)=1$ and $h(d)\to0$ as $d\to\infty$ is $h(d)=\exp(-2\mu d/\sigma^2)$. Differentiating this expression verifies the equation. The exponent contains drift once, distance once and variance in the denominator; inserting another copy of expected profit changes the mathematics and usually the units.
  </details>
</Reveal>

### Match the walk before comparing it

Take independent gains of €100 with probability 0.55 and losses of €100 with probability 0.45, once per bet. The initial distance is €1,000. Matching the first two moments gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu&=0.55(100)+0.45(-100)=10\quad\text{euros per bet},\\
\sigma^2&=E[Y^2]-E[Y]^2=10{,}000-10^2=9{,}900\quad\text{euros squared per bet},\\
d&=1{,}000\quad\text{euros}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the Brownian model gives

$$
e^{-2(10)(1{,}000)/9{,}900}=e^{-2.020202\ldots}\approx0.1326287=13.2629\%.
$$

The exact walk gives 13.4431%. The difference is about **0.1802 percentage points**. Matching moments does not make discrete jump paths continuous, and the approximation is not a safety bound.

| Walk inputs | Exact walk | Matched Brownian model |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $p=0.55$, $N=10$ | 13.4431% | 13.2629% |
| $p=0.55$, $N=20$ | 1.8072% | 1.7590% |
| $p=0.70$, $N=5$ | 1.4458% | 0.8549% |

For steps of one unit, $\mu=p-q$ and $\sigma^2=1-(p-q)^2=4pq$. At $p=0.70$, $N=5$, the continuous model gives $e^{-2(0.4)(5)/0.84}\approx0.0085493$, substantially below $(0.3/0.7)^5\approx0.0144583$. A smooth curve can be smoothly wrong for your decision.

### When the approximation helps

It is useful as an analytical benchmark when an additive, continuous diffusion is a defensible simplification. It assumes constant drift, constant variance and independent normal increments. Those properties are model choices, not facts established by observing a profitable backtest. Jumps, dependence, changing conditions and uncertain parameters need separate treatment; the approximation is neither a universal fixed-fraction formula nor automatically a conservative estimate.

## Fractional stakes and the floor you actually care about

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="In an ideal even-money game, you stake 10% of current wealth each time. After a finite run of losses, which statement is correct?"

  explanation="Each loss multiplies wealth by 0.9. A finite product of positive factors is positive; that does not guarantee enough money remains to meet a practical requirement."
/>

With fractional stake $0<f<1$, an ideal loss gives $W_{t+1}=W_t(1-f)$ and an even-money win gives $W_{t+1}=W_t(1+f)$. After $k$ consecutive losses,

$$
W_k=W_0(1-f)^k>0\quad\text{for every finite }k.
$$

With initial wealth of €1,000 and $f=0.10$, three losses leave $1{,}000\times0.9^3=729$ euros. That is above zero and below a €750 practical floor. If the rule is to stop at or below €750, this path has suffered practical ruin on the third loss. It has not hit zero. An indefinitely repeated losing path can tend towards zero; a limit over infinitely many steps is different from reaching zero in finitely many steps.

A positive floor may represent a minimum operating balance, a withdrawal obligation or a risk limit. Leverage, fees, minimum stake sizes and jumps can also break the ideal positive-factor calculation. Defining ruin as a floor crossing makes the question useful without pretending that the fixed-unit power formula applies.

### Initial-wealth floors and running peaks

A fixed floor $F=0.75W_0$ stays in one place. A 25% **running-peak drawdown** barrier is $0.75M_t$, where $M_t=\max_{s\le t}W_s$ is the highest wealth reached so far. It rises after a new peak.

| Point in one wealth path | Wealth | Fixed floor at 75% of start | Floor at 75% of running peak |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Start | €1,000 | €750 | €750 |
| New peak | €1,500 | €750 | €1,125 |
| Later value | €1,100 | €750 | €1,125 |

At €1,100 the fixed initial floor is unbroken, but drawdown from the peak is $(1{,}500-1{,}100)/1{,}500=26.6667\%$, exceeding 25%. Probability of ever losing a quarter of initial wealth and probability of ever drawing down a quarter from a running peak are different events. Neither is supplied by a generic “fraction of Kelly” exponent without a specified stochastic model and barrier.

### Put uncertainty and execution back into the calculation

Even the clean walk is sensitive to its inputs. At $N=10$, changing the assumed $p$ from 0.55 to 0.50 changes eventual ruin from 13.4431% to 100%; assuming 0.60 gives $(2/3)^{10}\approx1.7342\%$. Those are conditional model outputs, not confidence statements about the true win probability. A short sample cannot turn an estimated edge into a known constant.

A stop order also does not turn a trading loss into an exact $-1$ step. Buying 100 shares at €100 with a €95 stop plans a €500 loss if execution is at €95. A fill at €90 loses $100\times(100-90)=1{,}000$ euros, before costs. The [SEC's stop-order bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-15) explains that the trigger price is not a guaranteed execution price; a stop-limit can instead remain unexecuted. The ±1 formula no longer describes those loss outcomes.

### When to use a richer model

For fractional stakes, cash flows, variable payoffs or peak-based barriers, specify the wealth recurrence and first-crossing rule directly. Then use an appropriate analytical model, a finite-state calculation or simulation, stress the assumptions and report the horizon. Independence, a stable known edge and normally distributed increments are not general properties of trading. A simulated low probability can reflect optimistic assumptions as easily as a resilient strategy.
