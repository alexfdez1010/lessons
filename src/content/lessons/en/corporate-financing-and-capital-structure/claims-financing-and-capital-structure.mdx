A company needs €300 for a new production line. It could use cash, borrow, issue shares, or combine them. The machine does not become more productive because the funding slide uses a particularly tasteful shade of blue. Financing changes who supplies money, who receives future cash, and which constraints can stop the project.

**Capital structure** is the mix of financing claims supporting a business, especially debt and equity. This course studies a **non-financial operating company**. Bank deposits and bank capital require the separate [banking balance-sheet course](/banking-and-monetary-balance-sheets). Here we build on operating DCF, portfolio risk and bond payments.

All businesses, amounts, probabilities, contracts and tax rules are invented. Throughout this course, displayed decimal results are rounded to six places. “Value” always needs a date, a claim and a set of assumptions. We begin with the cash allocation, before debating which mix might be preferable.

## Separate the operating assets from their financing claims

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"Assets pay 60 and senior debt promises 100. Who receives the available 60 in our two-claim model?"}
 options={[
  "Debt holders",
  "Equity holders",
  "Each group receives 60"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Debt receives min(100,60) = 60; equity receives zero."}
/>

Imagine one cash collection box with two contractual queues. **Debt** has the senior payment specified in this model; **common equity** receives the residual. Senior means earlier in this stated waterfall, not immune to loss. Limited liability means shareholders do not automatically owe the debt shortfall from their personal accounts.

Let $A_1$ be the operating assets' distributable cash at the sole terminal date, and $F$ the debt payment due then. With no other claims or costs,

$$
D_1=\min(F,A_1),\qquad E_1=\max(A_1-F,0),\qquad D_1+E_1=A_1.
$$

At $A_1=120$ and $F=100$, debt receives $100$ and equity $20$. If the same contract meets only €60 of available assets, debt receives €60, its unpaid promise is €40, and equity receives zero. The missing €40 is not an extra payment funded by a formula.

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"claims"}
 title={"Change the terminal claim allocation"}
/>

Change the asset cash before changing the promise. When the senior claim is already fully paid, another euro goes to equity. Below the debt boundary, another euro first improves creditor recovery. The kink is the contractual allocation, not a new source of operating cash.

Actual priority depends on the instrument, security, entity perimeter, applicable law and resolution process. Preferred shares, leases, collateral pools and different subsidiaries can require more queues than these two. This is a stated contract model, not a universal bankruptcy waterfall.

### When to use the distinction

Use it whenever a forecast says “cash for investors”. Decide whether it is before financing payments or already belongs to one claim. Adding the values of debt and equity can recover the value of their underlying asset pool; adding both to that same pool counts it twice.

## Keep market value, carrying amount and promised payment apart

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"A debt claim trades at 80 and promises 100 next year. Which amount is its date-zero market value?"}
 options={[
  "80",
  "100",
  "180"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"The price and the promise have different dates and meanings."}
/>

A debt contract can promise €100 while trading for €80. The **promised payment** specifies the future contract; **market value** is its current transaction or model price; **carrying amount** is the recorded accounting amount under the reporting rules. Those numbers answer different questions. A reported face amount is not automatically the correct weight in a valuation.

In a separate one-year example, the operating assets pay €60 or €140. The debt promise is €100. Given date-zero market prices are debt €80 and equity €20, so the operating claim costs €100. In the two states the combined receipts are respectively $60+0=60$ and $100+40=140$.

For later expected-return calculations we stipulate physical probabilities of 25 % and 75 %. These describe the model's future outcomes, not the pricing weights. To make the given prices internally transparent, state prices of 0.5 for each terminal state and a zero safe rate give $D_0=0.5(60)+0.5(100)=80$ and $E_0=0.5(0)+0.5(40)=20$. A **state price** is the price today of €1 delivered only in that state. It need not equal the physical probability.

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"default"}
 title={"Compare promises, prices and expected receipts"}
/>

The market-value debt weight is $80/(80+20)=80\%$. Inserting the €100 promise alongside €20 equity would instead produce $100/120=83.3333\%$ and mix a future contractual amount with a current asset price. This is not a harmless rounding choice.

### When to use each amount

Use market values for the financing weights in this course's required-return equations. Use the contractual schedule to determine payments and test funding needs. Use properly defined carrying amounts for an accounting ratio. [CFA Institute's capital-structure overview](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/capital-structure) places the debt/equity choice alongside its effect on required capital returns; our state-by-state prices above are an independently specified teaching case.

## Balance financing sources and uses before calculating returns

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"An equity issue costs 5 % of gross proceeds. To receive 90 net, which budget works?"}
 options={[
  "Raise 90/0.95",
  "Raise 90 × 0.95",
  "Raise exactly 90"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Gross proceeds less the fee must equal 90."}
/>

A financing plan is a dated cash budget. **Sources** bring cash into that budget; **uses** spend it. An issue announcement reports gross funding, while a project can spend only the proceeds left after costs and other commitments.

Our date-zero project costs €300. Opening cash is €80, but €20 must remain available for operations. Therefore usable internal cash is $80-20=60$. Suppose a new loan provides €150 at par, with no debt issue fee. Net new equity must fill $300-60-150=90$.

An invented equity issue fee is 5 % of **gross cash raised**, $G$. Net proceeds are $G(1-0.05)$, so

$$
G=\frac{90}{0.95}=94.736842,\qquad
\text{fee}=0.05G=4.736842.
$$

The €20 reserve is already inside the opening €80. It is neither another source nor another project cost. Subtracting 5 % from €90 would deliver only €85.50 net, leaving a €4.50 funding gap. The fee's denominator matters.

### When to prepare the budget

Prepare it before comparing EPS, discount rates or financing ratios. A favourable valuation does not pay an invoice if the proceeds are missing. In the final chapter we will limit the loan using a specific covenant rather than assuming that every requested euro is available.

## Treat retained cash as shareholders' capital with an opportunity cost

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"A project uses retained cash. What happens to its opportunity cost?"}
 options={[
  "It still needs assessment",
  "It automatically becomes zero",
  "It equals the cash balance"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Funding internally does not remove alternative uses or risk."}
/>

**Retained earnings** are accumulated accounting profits not distributed to owners. They are part of equity, not a separate vault containing the same amount of cash. Earnings may already be invested in inventory, receivables or equipment. Retained **cash** is the cash actually available, subject to commitments.

Even available internal cash has an opportunity cost: the return owners could require from an alternative with matching risk, timing and currency. Suppose, separately, the company can retain €100 for a project expected to pay €105 after one year. A suitable required return for that project is an assumed 10 %. Its present value and net present value are

$$
PV=\frac{105}{1.10}=95.454545,\qquad NPV=95.454545-100=-4.545455.
$$

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"cash"}
 title={"Test a retained-cash project against its opportunity cost"}
/>

The project earns €5 in the simple forecast, yet fails this required-return test. Paying no external issuance fee does not turn its required return into zero. A different risk assessment or cash forecast could change the result; “internal funding” by itself cannot.

Keep investment and financing questions distinct: first estimate the project's incremental operating cash and opportunity cost, then check whether the funding plan adds costs or other effects. [Damodaran's financing framework](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/lectures/capstr.html) compares benefits and costs of borrowing; it does not make retained resources free.

### When to use the opportunity-cost test

Use it when a proposal is defended because “we already have the money”. The cash might be distributable, needed for resilience, or supporting another project. Write the alternative and any liquidity constraint explicitly instead of pretending the accounting source decides the investment's quality.
