Suppose two packages deliver exactly the same cash in every possible state and require the same commitments. If both can be traded on matching terms, paying more merely because one package has a corporate logo is hard to defend. This is the starting point of financing replication.

The **Modigliani–Miller**, or MM, capital-structure propositions describe conditional results. They are useful because they identify which omitted friction must explain a claimed financing benefit. They do not say taxes, borrowing constraints or bankruptcy negotiations are imaginary.

## State the ideal market before stating irrelevance

In our no-tax model, **Proposition I** says that changing the division of unchanged operating cash between debt and equity does not change the combined value: $V_L=V_U$. The subscripts mean levered and unlevered. We need the same operating assets and investment policy, the same information and appropriately comparable claims.

For the explicit replication below, assume no taxes, issuance costs, trading costs or real distress costs; divisible tradable positions; matched corporate and personal borrowing/lending opportunities; enforceable identical payments; and no constraint that prevents either portfolio. The debt used below is safe under the specified terminal cash. **No arbitrage** means there is no feasible zero-net-cost trade with nonnegative future receipts in all model states and a positive receipt in at least one relevant state.

If the whole operating asset pays €1,100 at year 1 and debt is owed €420, creditors receive €420 and equity €680. Without debt, owners of the asset receive all €1,100. Splitting the same €1,100 is not itself a €420 operating gain.

This example shows the resource identity; the next sections supply the matching portfolios and their initial budgets. An identity alone is not enough to prove a price equality if one portfolio cannot be bought, shorted, financed or enforced.

### When to use the benchmark

Use it to organise an investigation. If a proposal claims leverage creates value, ask whether taxes, information, incentives, costs, investment policy or access changed. [ACCA's explanation of gearing and capital costs](https://www.accaglobal.com/sg/en/student/exam-support-resources/professional-exams-study-resources/p4/technical-articles/cost-of-capital-gearing-and-capm-part-2.html) describes the offset between equity cost and debt weights in the ideal no-tax case. Its usefulness comes from its assumptions.

## Reproduce levered equity with personal borrowing

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"You buy an asset stake for 100 and borrow 40 to fund it. What is your date-zero net outlay?"}
 options={[
  "60",
  "100",
  "140"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"100 paid less 40 received leaves 60 of your own funding."}
/>

Take two firms with identical operating assets worth €1,000. One is unlevered. The other has safe debt worth €400, paying €420 at year 1, and equity worth €600. Buy **10 %** of the levered equity for €60. Its year-1 receipt is $0.10(A_1-420)$.

The **homemade-leverage** portfolio instead buys 10 % of the unlevered operating claim for €100 and borrows €40 personally at the same 5 % rate. Its net date-zero cash outlay is $100-40=60$, and its year-1 receipt is $0.10A_1-42$.

$$
0.10(A_1-420)=0.10A_1-42.
$$

Both portfolios cost €60 and deliver the same receipt in each state. If instead personal borrowing costs 8 %, the personal repayment is $40(1.08)=43.20$: the homemade receipt is €1.20 lower in every state. The identical-payoff argument using €42 no longer applies. Collateral requirements, limited recourse and personal liability can also differ even when quoted rates look equal.

### When to construct the replica

Use it to test whether a financing label changes cash or merely packages it. Write the date-zero borrowing as a source and the entire dated repayment as a use. Subtracting only the €2 interest while forgetting the €40 principal would manufacture a spectacular but fictional return.

## Undo leverage by holding the matching senior claim too

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"To undo corporate leverage exactly in our case, which extra claim accompanies equity?"}
 options={[
  "The matching corporate debt",
  "Any account with a similar label",
  "Another leveraged equity stake"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Matching debt and equity reconstruct the same underlying asset cash."}
/>

An investor can also reconstruct the unlevered claim from both financing layers. Buy 10 % of the levered equity for €60 and 10 % of the same company's safe debt for €40. Total date-zero outlay is €100. At year 1 the debt stake pays €42 and the equity stake pays $0.10(A_1-420)$, so

$$
0.10(A_1-420)+42=0.10A_1.
$$

In the middle state, €68 plus €42 is €110, exactly the receipt of the €100 unlevered stake. In the low and high states the sums are €104 and €116. This **reverse replication** uses the actual matching debt claim, not an unrelated deposit chosen because it also says “5 %”.

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"reverse"}
 title={"Combine the matching senior and residual claims"}
/>

For a complete pair of claims, debt plus equity still sums to available assets in default. But replacing risky corporate debt by a safe personal loan need not reproduce that payoff: the recovery, recourse and states differ. That is why our earlier homemade-leverage illustration deliberately used safe debt and specified matching borrowing terms.

If trading permits an investor to buy the cheaper complete portfolio and sell the more expensive equivalent portfolio, a persistent price difference would contradict the stipulated no-arbitrage condition. Short-sale and financing restrictions can prevent that trade. Equal expected cash alone is weaker than equal cash **in every state**: two claims can have the same mean but different bad-state losses.

### When to combine the claims

Use complete-claim replication to check a model's financing boundary and valuation arithmetic. [Damodaran's ideal financing benchmark](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/lectures/capstr.html) makes the no-tax, no-conflict setting explicit. The benchmark should make missing assumptions visible, not conceal them.

## Derive the no-tax required-return equation

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"No-tax MM holds operating risk fixed. As debt rises, what offsets its lower required return?"}
 options={[
  "A higher required equity return",
  "A lower required equity return",
  "An automatic increase in assets"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"The residual equity becomes more exposed."}
/>

A portfolio holding all debt and equity owns the operating asset cash. With same-date market prices $D$ and $E$, total value $V=D+E$, and expected received returns measured over the same period,

$$
r_A=\frac{E}{V}r_E+\frac{D}{V}r_D.
$$

Rearrange by multiplying by $V$, subtracting $Dr_D$ and dividing by $E>0$:

$$
r_E=r_A+\frac{D}{E}(r_A-r_D).
$$

In our ideal model set $V=1{,}000$, $D=400$, $E=600$, $r_A=10\%$ and safe $r_D=5\%$. Then

$$
r_E=10\%+\frac{400}{600}(10\%-5\%)=13.333333\%,
$$

and $0.60(13.333333\%)+0.40(5\%)=10\%$. More low-return debt does not lower this no-tax weighted return because the required equity return rises. At debt €800 and equity €200, the same formula gives equity 30 %; the weighted return remains $0.20(30\%)+0.80(5\%)=10\%$.

/>

**Proposition II** describes that rising required equity return in the ideal benchmark. The portfolio identity itself uses **expected received debt return**. If debt can default, replacing it with promised YTM is not justified by this algebra. Nor does the identity say $r_A$ and every claim's risk stay fixed when borrowing changes operations, costs or feasible investments.

### When to use the equation

Use it as a consistency check under the declared model, rather than as a cheap-debt calculator. [ACCA's capital-cost discussion](https://www.accaglobal.com/sg/en/student/exam-support-resources/professional-exams-study-resources/p4/technical-articles/cost-of-capital-gearing-and-capm-part-2.html) also stresses the fit between business risk, gearing and the rate used for an investment. Market-value weights and matched cash-flow risk are essential to that fit.
