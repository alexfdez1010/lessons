Two firms own the same operating business. One has no debt; the other has borrowed and repurchased shares. The second can report a higher profit per remaining share in a good year. That calculation is real. Whether shareholders gained value requires another calculation.

We will keep **operating risk**, uncertainty in the business cash flow, separate from **financial leverage**, the use of debt that reallocates that uncertainty among financing claims. Each case below has its own dates and prices; the safe recapitalisation and the default example are not the same company.

## Trace a debt-funded repurchase through EPS and equity returns

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"A debt-funded repurchase raises EPS in one forecast. What can you infer about total value from that fact alone?"}
 options={[
  "No definite value increase",
  "A guaranteed value increase",
  "The project no longer has risk"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"EPS changes with interest and the share denominator; value requires cash and risk."}
/>

Our unlevered company has an operating asset worth €1,000 at date zero, financed by 100 shares at €10. “Unlevered” here means no financing debt. There is no tax, issue cost, depreciation or reinvestment. Annual operating profit before interest and tax, **EBIT**, is also the operating cash generated during the year.

The company borrows €400 at 5 %, receiving €400 at par, then spends exactly €400 repurchasing 40 shares at €10. The remaining equity is €600 across 60 shares; the operating asset remains €1,000. At year-end the model liquidates that asset for €1,000 in addition to EBIT. Debt is owed $400+0.05(400)=420$.

**Earnings per share**, EPS, divides the stated net income by the relevant share count. Here it is $\text{EBIT}/100$ without debt and $(\text{EBIT}-20)/60$ after the recap. With no taxes, the annual income after interest is the numerator.

For the middle state, equity receives $1{,}100-420=680$, so its holding-period return is $(680-600)/600=13.333333\%$. **Return on equity**, ROE, here also equals $80/600$ because we stipulate the opening recorded equity equals €600 and these accounting simplifications hold. In actual statements ROE and a market holding-period return need not coincide.

EPS breaks even between these two setups at EBIT €50: $(50-20)/60=50/100=0.50$. A higher EPS in one state is therefore not a proof of higher enterprise value. We have held the operating value fixed as a model assumption and made the residual more sensitive.

### When to use the comparison

Use it to audit a buyback or borrowing proposal that advertises EPS accretion, meaning increased EPS. Reconcile proceeds, repurchase price, interest, shares and terminal capital. The supported debt is safe only because the stipulated terminal asset cash always covers it; do not carry that conclusion into a distressed company.

## Price expected receipts rather than treating promised yield as received return

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"Debt may default. Which cash amount belongs in its expected received return?"}
 options={[
  "Expected actual receipts",
  "The promise in every state",
  "The equity cash in every state"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Default recovery changes actual receipts."}
/>

Return needs a price and what the holder actually receives. In the separate two-state case from the first lesson, assets pay €60 or €140, the debt promise is €100, physical probabilities are 25 % and 75 %, and initial prices are debt €80 and equity €20.

The debt's one-year **promised yield** uses the promise: $100/80-1=25\%$. Its physical expected cash receipt instead is

$$
\mathbb E[D_1]=0.25(60)+0.75(100)=90,
\qquad \mathbb E[R_D]=90/80-1=12.5\%.
$$

Equity receives zero or €40. Its expected receipt is €30 and its expected return is $30/20-1=50\%$. Expected asset cash is $0.25(60)+0.75(140)=120$, giving a 20 % expected return on the €100 operating claim. The market-value-weighted received returns reconcile:

$$
0.80(12.5\%)+0.20(50\%)=20\%.
$$

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"default"}
 title={"Compare promises, prices and expected receipts"}
/>

These expectations are not promised gains. One equity state loses the entire €20 purchase price. The physical probabilities are model inputs, and the pricing weights used earlier are different. Replacing the debt's 12.5 % expected received return with its 25 % promised yield breaks the reconciliation.

### When to distinguish the two

Use this distinction whenever debt may default, especially when interpreting a spread or inserting a debt-return input into an identity about expected portfolio returns. The [bond price and yield lesson](/bonds-and-rates/bond-prices-and-yields) separately explains scheduled-cash-flow YTM and realised return. A market yield may be a practical proxy in a particular valuation workflow; the proxy does not become a mathematical equality when expected losses are material.

## Separate creditor losses from real costs of financial distress

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"A debt shortfall is 40, but all 60 of existing assets are distributed. How much real resource loss has this statement established?"}
 options={[
  "None from the shortfall alone",
  "Exactly 40 of destroyed assets",
  "Exactly 100 of destroyed assets"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"A contractual shortfall and resources consumed by distress are different."}
/>

**Financial distress** means difficulty meeting financing obligations. A debt shortfall is a loss to the creditor relative to the contract. It is not automatically an equally sized destruction of operating resources: part of a financing change can simply transfer value between claimants.

First keep the low asset state at €60 and the promise at €100 with no real costs. The owners of debt receive €60 and equity zero; all €60 remains distributed. Now assume that default triggers an additional €10 of real resolution costs in that low state only. The distributable pool falls to $60-10=50$. Debt receives €50, equity zero, and €10 leaves the investors' pool.

At the stated 25 % low-state probability, expected resources lost are $0.25(10)=2.50$. Expected combined distributions fall from €120 to €117.50. If the original debt/equity purchase prices remain €80/€20 for this sensitivity, debt's expected receipt becomes $0.25(50)+0.75(100)=87.50$ and its expected holding return becomes 9.375 %. We are measuring a changed outcome from the original purchase price, not claiming that new fair debt prices would stay €80.

Direct costs can include legal and administration resources; indirect costs can include disrupted trade or foregone investment. Their timing and risk also matter in valuation. [Damodaran's financing discussion](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/CFTheory/deriv/ch17der.html) distinguishes those channels. Our €10 cost is an assumption, not a measured typical bankruptcy percentage.

### When to use the distinction

Use it before subtracting “debt losses” as an extra operating cost. If a forecast or price already contains the same lost cash, subtracting it again double counts. Conversely, a pure transfer calculation can miss lost customers, implementation costs and delayed projects that change total value.

## Examine incentives when debt and equity share gains differently

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"Can a shareholder prefer a decision that lowers combined debt-plus-equity value?"}
 options={[
  "Yes, if value transfers to equity",
  "No, every claim has identical incentives",
  "Only if no debt exists at all"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Different claim payoffs can create conflicting incentives."}
/>

Existing debt can make a decision look different to shareholders than to the company as a whole. Two stripped-down **one-date, zero-discount** examples expose the mechanism. They are not recommendations to breach a contract.

**Debt overhang** can discourage new equity-funded investment. Existing assets pay €80 against debt promised €100. A new project costs shareholders €10 now and adds €15 at the terminal date. Its total NPV is $15-10=5$. Yet total assets would reach only €95, so creditors gain €15 and existing equity still receives zero. The shareholders asked to fund it lose €10 net. Renegotiation or different financing might resolve the conflict; its availability cannot be assumed.

**Risk shifting** can create the reverse conflict. Start instead with certain terminal assets €100 and a debt promise €80: creditors get €80, equity €20. Suppose equity can replace the assets, without another cash payment, by equally likely receipts €40 and €150. Expected total cash becomes $0.5(40)+0.5(150)=95$, a €5 loss. Expected creditor receipts are $0.5(40)+0.5(80)=60$; equity's are $0.5(0)+0.5(70)=35$.

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"incentives"}
 title={"Compare the total decision with each claimant’s incentive"}
/>

Thus equity gains €15, debt loses €20, and the combined change is −€5. The equity gain is not evidence that the project created €15 of enterprise value. [CFA Institute's course outline](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/capital-structure) frames financing as a choice affected by issuer and investor conditions; these particular incentive calculations are our own examples.

### When to use an incentive map

Use it when assessing investment restrictions, collateral, security priority or refinancing negotiations. A covenant, a contractual condition, may limit a harmful transfer but can also obstruct a useful project. Map who pays and who gains in each state before deciding that one party's metric represents everyone's interests.
