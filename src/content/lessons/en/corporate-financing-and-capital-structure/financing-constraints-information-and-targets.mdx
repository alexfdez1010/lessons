The financing plan with the lowest estimated cost may be unavailable, fragile, or based on an issue price nobody will pay. A useful decision therefore needs both valuation and a feasible cash budget.

We will use explicit invented constraints rather than a universal recipe such as “always borrow first”. A **target capital structure** is a financing mix a company aims to maintain; its suitability depends on the business, available contracts and uncertainty. The examples remain non-financial companies.

## Size the project funding after cash reserves and covenant headroom

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"A covenant permits total debt of 350 and existing debt is 200. How much new headroom is shown?"}
 options={[
  "150",
  "350",
  "550"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"New headroom is the ceiling less existing debt."}
/>

A **covenant** is a contractual condition. Our fictional lender permits total debt no greater than **3.5 times annual EBITDA**. EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation; its contractual definition would need checking. It is an earnings measure, not a cash balance or a universally standard covenant numerator.

Existing debt is €200. At EBITDA €100, the stated total-debt ceiling is $3.5(100)=350$, leaving new debt headroom $350-200=150$. The project costs €300. Cash €80 includes an operating reserve €20, so only €60 is available. Therefore net equity proceeds must be $300-60-150=90$. At a 5 % gross issue fee the equity cash raised is $90/0.95=94.736842$.

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"budget"}
 title={"Fund a project within a stated debt covenant"}
/>

If EBITDA falls to €80, the ceiling becomes €280 and headroom €80. Net required equity rises to $300-60-80=160$, gross $160/0.95=168.421053$. The same project is not financed by simply wishing the old headroom back into existence.

At EBITDA €140, headroom is €290, but only €240 is needed after internal cash. The viewer borrows €240 and leaves €50 of this test's headroom unused; it does not raise unnecessary debt and report negative equity issuance.

### When to use the constraint

Use the actual contract's definitions, testing date, cure rights and permitted adjustments. Passing one numerical test does not guarantee funding approval or compliance with every covenant. A prospective lender can also impose collateral, cash reserves, limits on distributions or conditions tied to the financed project.

## Test interest coverage and dated cash debt service separately

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"A company covers interest from earnings. Which obligation may still be missing from that test?"}
 options={[
  "Principal due",
  "The same interest counted again",
  "A guaranteed new equity dividend"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Interest-only coverage omits principal and can omit cash timing."}
/>

**Interest coverage** compares a stated income measure with interest expense. **Debt-service coverage** compares a stated cash measure with interest plus principal due. They need different numerators and denominators; neither should be named without its definition.

In the EBITDA €100 funding case, existing annual interest is €10 and the €150 new debt costs 5 %, adding €7.50. Total annual interest is €17.50. Our EBITDA-based coverage is

$$
\frac{100}{17.50}=5.714286.
$$

Now stipulate a **separate** operating cash forecast of €90 available before debt service in the test year, and principal repayment of €40. Cash service due is $17.50+40=57.50$, giving

$$
\frac{90}{57.50}=1.565217.
$$

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"coverage"}
 title={"Compare earnings coverage with cash service due"}
/>

The €90 is not derived from EBITDA €100. Tax, working-capital movements and investment can make cash differ from earnings. Even the €90 annual amount does not prove the money arrives before a payment due in March: dates within the year can create a funding gap despite acceptable annual coverage.

### When to build the dated test

Use a payment calendar alongside annual ratios when maturities or seasonal collections matter. Our example assumes the €90 is available when service is due and omits refinancing. Never replace a missing principal payment with an automatic new loan merely because interest coverage looks comfortable.

## Distinguish ownership dilution from a value transfer at the issue price

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"Old ownership falls after a fair-price capital increase. Must old owners lose intrinsic wealth?"}
 options={[
  "No, new capital can offset it",
  "Yes, any lower percentage destroys wealth",
  "Yes, even with no financing change"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Ownership percentage and claim value are distinct."}
/>

**Information asymmetry** means parties have different information. A financing choice may be influenced by how managers and investors estimate value, but an observed share issue does not prove which estimate is correct. A **pecking-order** hypothesis describes a possible preference for internal finance, then debt, then equity under particular information costs; it is not an obligation or a universal ranking.

Consider an independent scenario with 100 old shares and **assumed intrinsic equity value €900**, or €9 per share. New investors contribute €100 at an issue price €8. This funds a zero-NPV project whose intrinsic value is also €100. There are no fees, so post-investment intrinsic equity value is €1,000.

New shares are $100/8=12.5$; total shares 112.5. The old owners' stake is now $100/112.5=88.888889\%$, worth

$$
1{,}000\frac{100}{112.5}=888.888889.
$$

Old owners lose €11.111111 relative to the stipulated €900 value. New owners receive a claim worth €111.111111 for their €100 cash, an offsetting transfer. Nothing here proves that €900 is the true value of a real company.

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"issue"}
 title={"Separate ownership dilution from scenario value transfers"}
/>

At an issue price €9, ownership still falls, but the old claim remains €900: $1{,}000(100)/(100+100/9)=900$. A lower ownership percentage need not be a wealth loss when fair new capital enters. At price €10, the same intrinsic-value assumptions transfer value in the other direction. Rights, voting changes, costs and project NPV would require additional entries; they are absent here.

### When to assess an issuance decision

Separate ownership arithmetic, the estimated pre-issue value, the actual issue price and the new project's value. Do not infer that shares are definitely overvalued because managers issue them, or definitely undervalued because they avoid doing so. [Damodaran's financing framework](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/lectures/capstr.html) includes financing flexibility and agency costs; those trade-offs need explicit assumptions, rather than a one-word funding hierarchy.

## Restore a market-value target with a complete transaction budget

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"Debt stays at 400 while operating value falls from 1000 to 800. What is the new debt weight?"}
 options={[
  "50 %",
  "40 %",
  "32 %"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"400/800 is 50 %, under the unchanged debt-value assumption."}
/>

A target based on market values can drift without the company borrowing another euro. Initially the operating asset is worth €1,000, debt €400 and equity €600: debt weight 40 %. Suppose operating value falls to €800 while the debt's market value remains €400 under this scenario. Equity is now €400 and debt weight is $400/800=50\%$.

A specified 40 % target requires debt $0.40(800)=320$. In our cost-free transaction, the existing owners subscribe **€80 of additional cash**, the company uses all €80 to retire debt at its stated value, and operating assets remain worth €800. Debt falls to €320 and equity becomes €480.

Debt weight is now $320/800=40\%$. The €80 came from outside the company; it was not a free conversion of debt into equity. Existing owners collectively hold €480 after paying €80, leaving net wealth €400 before other changes. If instead the operating value rises to €1,200 with debt still €400, reaching 40 % would require new debt €80 paid out to owners. That is an alternative policy action, not a requirement to borrow.

### When to rebalance the financing mix

Check issue terms, repurchase rights, transaction costs, tax and debt market values before implementing a target. A fixed debt face amount need not have a fixed market value when credit or rates change. Keeping debt value unchanged here is an explicit scenario assumption, not a general rule of market shocks.

## Choose a policy that survives more than the central forecast

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"Two feasible funding plans have different fees and spare debt capacity. Which comparison is justified?"}
 options={[
  "Compare costs and flexibility",
  "Always choose the larger debt amount",
  "Always choose the lowest equity amount"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"A central financing metric does not settle every trade-off."}
/>

The plan should say which conditions would change it. A **trade-off** approach compares tax benefits and real financing costs; an information-based approach focuses on financing frictions; a liquidity policy preserves resources for dated obligations. These are conditional lenses, not competing slogans from which one must be crowned champion.

For the €300 project, compare at least two feasible budgets at the same 5 % gross equity fee. With EBITDA €100, using €150 new debt requires €90 net equity and a €4.736842 fee. Using only €100 debt requires €140 net equity, gross $140/0.95=147.368421$, fee €7.368421, while preserving €50 more of the stated covenant headroom. The extra current fee is €2.631579. It is a visible cost; it does not by itself measure the future value of that headroom.

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"sources"}
 title={"Compare funding fees and debt capacity left unused"}
 initial={{
  "newDebt": 100

/>

A lower-debt plan can be useful if cash flows or future investment needs are uncertain, but it can also forgo financing benefits. [CFA Institute's financing overview](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/capital-structure) treats target structures as part of the issuer's decision. There is no universal debt ratio supplied by our examples.

### When to document the decision

Record the operating perimeter, debt maturities, source of each contribution, market-value assumptions, tax capacity, constraints, scenario range and decision triggers. Keep an investment's expected NPV separate from whether the company can fund it. Then explain what the selected mix sacrifices, what it preserves, and which facts would overturn the choice.
