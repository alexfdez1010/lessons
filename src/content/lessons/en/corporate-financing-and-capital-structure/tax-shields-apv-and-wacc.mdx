Interest can reduce a company's tax bill under a particular tax system. That benefit is a cash-flow question before it is a discount-rate shortcut. Who can use the deduction, when can it be used, and how risky is it?

This lesson uses **invented tax rules**, not tax advice or a statement of any country's law. The operating company is not a bank. We will first value explicit financing cash flows, then compare APV and WACC in a separate case whose assumptions actually match.

## Calculate the tax actually saved this year

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"EBIT is 10, interest 20, tax 25 %, no refund or carryforward. How much current tax can interest save?"}
 options={[
  "2.50",
  "5.00",
  "20.00"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Tax without interest is only 2.50."}
/>

An **interest tax shield** is tax saved because deductible interest reduces taxable income. It is not the interest payment itself. In our simple current-tax system, EBIT is nonnegative, interest is deductible only against this year's EBIT, tax cannot be negative, and there are no refunds, carryforwards or additional interest limits.

Let $t$ be the tax rate and $I$ annual interest. Then

$$
T_U=t\,\text{EBIT},\qquad
T_L=t\max(\text{EBIT}-I,0),\qquad
S=T_U-T_L=t\min(\text{EBIT},I).
$$

With EBIT €30, interest €20 and tax 25 %, tax without debt is $30(0.25)=7.50$; with debt it is $(30-20)(0.25)=2.50$. The shield is €5. With EBIT only €10, unlevered tax is €2.50 and levered tax zero, so the usable shield is **€2.50**, not €5. At EBIT zero both current tax bills are zero.

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"shield"}
 title={"Test the current capacity to use interest deductions"}
/>

The unused current deduction might matter later in a different system, but our model has deliberately omitted that mechanism. A future usable deduction would need its own timing, constraints and risk. [Damodaran's discussion of tax benefits](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/CFTheory/deriv/ch17der.html) highlights both marginal tax rates and the need for usable income. Actual tax regimes can add limits and loss rules beyond this illustration.

### When to model the tax explicitly

Use an explicit schedule when profits, interest limits or tax capacity vary. Principal repayment is not an interest deduction in this model. Do not multiply total debt service by the tax rate or count tax benefits already included elsewhere in the forecast.

A shield received in year 3 is not worth the same today as an equal shield received in year 1. Nor does a loan repaid after three years create an eternal deduction. We now assume full tax capacity at 25 %, debt interest at 5 %, year-end tax savings, and this fixed repayment policy:

There is no debt or later shield after year 3. Assume, expressly, that **5 % is the appropriate annual required return for these shield cash flows**. Then

$$
PV(S)=\frac{5}{1.05}+\frac{3.75}{1.05^2}+\frac{2.50}{1.05^3}
\approx10.322859.
$$

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"schedule"}
 title={"Discount the finite dated shield schedule"}
/>

The undiscounted sum is €11.25. At a zero discount rate the PV is also €11.25; zero does not break the direct sum. The initial debt times tax rate, $400(0.25)=100$, is not the value of this finite schedule.

The familiar $PV(S)=tD$ follows in a special perpetual setting: fixed debt $D$, constant annual interest $r_DD$, fully usable constant tax rate, and shield discount rate equal to $r_D$. Then $t r_DD/r_D=tD$ for $r_D>0$. Changing the debt policy to a target market-value ratio, changing usability, or changing shield risk requires another derivation. The operating WACC is not automatically the shield discount rate.

### When to choose a debt-policy model

Use the policy the company can plausibly follow: scheduled repayment, a fixed amount, refinancing or a target ratio. [Damodaran's APV explanation](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/valquestions/apv.htm) distinguishes a debt-amount tax benefit from financing assumptions implicit in capital-cost methods. Label the rule before applying a formula.

## Assemble adjusted present value without counting principal as a gain

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"APV adds financing effects to which starting value in this firm-value lesson?"}
 options={[
  "Unlevered operating value",
  "Gross debt proceeds alone",
  "Equity after subtracting debt twice"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"The base excludes the financing effects added separately."}
/>

**Adjusted present value**, APV, separates the operating value as if financed entirely by equity from separately valued financing effects. A firm-value version is

$$
V_L=V_U+PV(\text{financing benefits})-PV(\text{financing costs}).
$$

For a project **net** present value, the unlevered base must already subtract the investment cost. Do not switch between a gross asset value and a net project appraisal halfway through the bridge.

Take an independently estimated unlevered operating value €1,000. Add the finite shield PV from the previous section, €10.322859. Suppose issue costs paid now are €3 and the separately estimated present value of real distress costs is €8; neither is already in the operating base. Then

$$
V_L=1{,}000+10.322859-3-8=999.322859.
$$

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"apv"}
 title={"Assemble operating value and separate financing effects"}
/>

Financing's net effect is approximately **−€0.677141** under these assumptions. A positive tax shield need not make the full package beneficial. The €400 loan proceeds are not another €400 gain added to enterprise value: investors receive a debt claim in exchange, and borrowing/repayment belong in the funding account.

[Damodaran's APV framework](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/valquestions/apv.htm) separates the base, tax effects and distress estimates. Our costs are invented present values. An expected-cost calculation using probabilities must also match dates and risk; a physical probability times an undiscounted future loss is not automatically today's market value.

### When to use APV

Use it when financing effects or debt amounts change enough that a separate schedule clarifies the model. Reconcile each cost against the base first: adding a financing cost separately after already reducing FCFF for the same cash loss charges it twice.

## Match WACC to the same financing assumptions

<MCQ client:visible
 pretest={true}
 question={"A matched after-tax WACC already values a tax shield. Should the same shield be added again?"}
 options={[
  "No, that duplicates the benefit",
  "Yes, tax benefits are always doubled",
  "Only if shares are publicly traded"
]}
 correct={0}
 explanation={"Use one consistent treatment of the same financing effect."}
/>

The **weighted average cost of capital**, WACC, can incorporate financing effects through the discount rate instead of adding them as another value line. A common after-tax formula is

$$
w=\frac{E}{D+E}r_E+\frac{D}{D+E}r_D(1-t).
$$

It needs market-value weights, cash flows before financing distributions, and compatible risk, debt policy and tax assumptions. It is not permission to hold the equity cost fixed while changing debt indefinitely.

Here is a **separate ideal perpetual case**, not the finite loan above. Expected annual level FCFF is €87; unlevered required return is $29/300=9.666667\%$, so $V_U=87/(29/300)=900$. Fixed safe perpetual debt is €400 at 5 %, with a certain, fully usable 25 % deduction and no other financing costs. The annual shield is €5 and its assumed 5 % required return gives PV €100. Thus $V_L=1{,}000$ and equity is €600.

The compatible tax-adjusted MM equity return is

$$
r_E=\frac{29}{300}+(1-0.25)\frac{400}{600}\left(\frac{29}{300}-0.05\right)=12\%.
$$

Consequently $w=0.60(12\%)+0.40(5\%)(0.75)=8.7\%$, and

$$
\frac{87}{0.087}=1{,}000=900+100.
$$

<CorporateCapitalLab client:visible
 view={"wacc"}
 title={"Reconcile two methods under matched perpetual assumptions"}
/>

Adding the same €100 shield to the WACC result would give an incorrect €1,100. Agreement required the **same** perpetual policy and assumptions; it is not proof that any APV and any WACC spreadsheet agree. Expected FCFF is not guaranteed, and the assumed safe debt and certain usable shield need justification in an actual valuation. Defaulting debt, time-varying leverage and taxes can require richer models.

### When to choose the rate approach

Use a matched WACC when the cash-flow and financing-policy assumptions support it. Use APV when keeping the components separate is clearer. [ACCA's gearing discussion](https://www.accaglobal.com/sg/en/student/exam-support-resources/professional-exams-study-resources/p4/technical-articles/cost-of-capital-gearing-and-capm-part-2.html) treats the fit between risk, gearing and discount rates. Whichever method you use, the accounting and financing boundaries must remain consistent.

The €200 alternative has the highest value **among these four estimates**. Nothing in the table establishes a continuous optimum, an actual borrowing offer, or a universally desirable debt percentage. If the assumed distress costs change, the ranking can change.

At half the entered distress costs, the €400 and €600 alternatives tie at €1,045: $1{,}000+80-35=1{,}045$ and $1{,}000+120-75=1{,}045$. Reporting a single precise “optimal debt” would hide that sensitivity. [CFA Institute's overview](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/capital-structure) includes both optimal and target structures; a target chosen under uncertainty is not the same as a known universal optimum.

### When to use a scenario frontier

Use it to make assumptions and near-ties visible. Investigate the operating consequences, usable tax benefits, financing access and measurement uncertainty before treating a table maximum as a decision. A practical target may preserve funding flexibility even when a narrow central estimate favours more debt.
