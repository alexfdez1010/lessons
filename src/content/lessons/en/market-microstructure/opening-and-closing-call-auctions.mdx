A buyer offers up to €10.30. A seller accepts €10.00 or more. In ordinary continuous trading, compatible orders can match immediately. During a **call auction**, those instructions can wait together while more orders arrive. When the auction uncrosses, they may both trade at €10.20.

The limits define acceptable prices. They do not require each side to trade at its own limit. Opening and closing auctions gather eligible interest and use a venue's rules to choose a common execution price. We will build one from a small invented book, then separate that calculation from the information and execution risks of a real auction.

## A call collects orders before it uncrosses

A **call auction** collects eligible buy and sell orders for a matching event. **Uncrossing** is the event at which the venue determines the execution price and matches the eligible quantity. In the single-price model studied here, every executed share in that cross trades at the same price.

Continuous trading instead processes incoming orders against currently available opposite-side liquidity. A normal uncrossed continuous book therefore differs from a call collection book: the latter can contain overlapping buy and sell limits without having executed them yet.

| Feature | Continuous matching | Single-price call auction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When matching occurs | As eligible incoming orders interact | At the auction uncrossing event |
| Prices of one large execution | May walk through several levels | One price for that auction cross |
| Overlapping limits while waiting | Normally matched rather than left crossed | Can accumulate during collection |
| Main quantity question | What liquidity can this order access now? | How much eligible interest can pair at a candidate price? |

The [Nasdaq cross guide](https://www.nasdaqtrader.com/content/ProductsServices/Trading/Crosses/openclose_faqs.pdf) describes opening and closing crosses as single-price facilities, and distinguishes auction-specific interest from eligible continuous-book interest. “The auction book” is not necessarily an isolated list containing every order your broker displays; eligibility depends on the venue and order instructions.

Opening auctions help bring accumulated interest into a trading session. Closing auctions can concentrate interest at a common closing benchmark. Neither purpose establishes a unique fundamental value or a promise that every submitted order will execute.

### When this distinction matters

Use it when reading a crossed pre-open book, interpreting an indicative price or comparing an on-close instruction with a continuous market order. An immediate-or-cancel instruction designed for continuous trading does not automatically become an auction-only instruction.

## Count acceptable buys and sells at every candidate price

Take an invented auction with allowed prices **€10.00, €10.10, €10.20 and €10.30**. Its tick is deliberately coarse for teaching. There are no hidden orders, price collars, fees, special order types or changing eligibility rules in this calculation.

| Buy limit | Shares | Sell limit | Shares |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| €10.30 | 30 | €10.00 | 40 |
| €10.20 | 50 | €10.10 | 50 |
| €10.10 | 40 | €10.20 | 40 |
| €10.00 | 20 | €10.30 | 30 |

Add **60 market-buy shares and 10 market-sell shares**. Within this model, those market orders accept every allowed candidate price. For a candidate $P$, define eligible demand $D(P)$ and supply $S(P)$:

$$
D(P)=M_B+\sum_{i:L_i^B\ge P}Q_i^B,\qquad
S(P)=M_S+\sum_{j:L_j^S\le P}Q_j^S.
$$

$M_B$ and $M_S$ are the auction market quantities, $L$ is an order's limit and $Q$ its share quantity. A buy limit accepts a price **at or below** its limit; a sell limit accepts a price **at or above** its limit. These are cumulative quantities, not just orders priced exactly at $P$.

The matched quantity is the shorter side:

$$
V(P)=\min\{D(P),S(P)\}.
$$

At €10.20, buyers include the 60 market shares, 30 shares limited at €10.30 and 50 at €10.20: $D=60+30+50=140$. Sellers include 10 market shares plus 40 at €10.00, 50 at €10.10 and 40 at €10.20: $S=10+40+50+40=140$.

| Candidate price | Eligible buys $D$ | Eligible sells $S$ | Matched $V$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| €10.00 | 200 | 50 | 50 |
| €10.10 | 180 | 100 | 100 |
| €10.20 | 140 | 140 | **140** |
| €10.30 | 90 | 170 | 90 |

Maximum executable volume is 140 shares, uniquely at €10.20. The cash exchanged is $140\times10.20=1{,}428$ euros from buyers to sellers, before costs. Count the trade once; its two sides do not double either volume or economic value.

A buyer limited at €10.30 who is allocated shares pays €10.20 in this cross. A seller limited at €10.00 receives €10.20. Those are acceptable prices for both; the common price need not equal either original limit.

### When to use cumulative quantities

Use them to reproduce a transparent toy auction or audit the arithmetic of an auction model. Real venue calculations can include additional eligible interest, special handling and price constraints. Maximum volume is a selection objective within an admissible set, not permission to ignore those rules.

## Imbalance and tie-breaks answer different questions

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="One candidate matches 100 shares with 40 excess buys. Another matches only 50 shares but is perfectly balanced. Under a maximum-volume-first rule, which survives the first comparison?"
  options={[

    { text: 'The balanced 50-share candidate' },
    { text: 'Both, because imbalance is always ignored' },
    { text: 'Neither, because every auction must balance fully' },
  ]}
  explanation="The first objective is maximum executed volume. A later imbalance rule resolves ties among maximum-volume candidates; it does not replace that first objective."
/>

Define the **signed imbalance** in our model as

$$
I(P)=D(P)-S(P).
$$

A positive value is excess eligible buying; a negative value is excess eligible selling. $I=0$ means balance at that candidate price. An auction can execute while leaving an imbalance; the longer side need not fill completely.

Now reduce the market-buy quantity from 60 to **20 shares**, holding the limit book and 10 market sells fixed:

| Candidate | $D$ | $S$ | $V$ | Signed $I$ | Absolute imbalance |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| €10.00 | 160 | 50 | 50 | +110 | 110 |
| €10.10 | 140 | 100 | **100** | +40 | **40** |
| €10.20 | 100 | 140 | **100** | −40 | **40** |
| €10.30 | 50 | 170 | 50 | −120 | 120 |

Two prices maximize volume and have the same absolute imbalance. We therefore need declared tie-breaks. This lesson's **invented hierarchy** is:

1. Maximize $V$.
2. Among those prices, minimize $|I|$.
3. Among remaining ties, choose the price nearest the stated reference.
4. If reference distances also tie, choose the lower price.

With a €10.14 reference, distances are €0.04 to €10.10 and €0.06 to €10.20, so our rule selects €10.10. With reference €10.16 it selects €10.20. At €10.15 the distances tie and **our final lower-price convention** selects €10.10. The reference can lie between allowed execution ticks; it is an input to a tie-break, not a promised execution price.

This is not a universal exchange algorithm. [BME's own auction explanation](https://www.bolsasymercados.es/es/sala-de-comunicacion/noticias/2023/las-subastas-en-la-bolsa-parte-1.html) includes a market-pressure stage, while Nasdaq's published cross guide describes its own imbalance and reference-price tests. The relevant eligible orders, reference, pressure tests, collars and final tie conventions must come from the venue's rules.

### When imbalance helps

Use it to describe a particular eligible snapshot at a particular calculation price. Published feeds distinguish fields such as market imbalance, total imbalance, reference price and paired quantity; those definitions need not equal this simplified all-order $D-S$ calculation. A buy-side sign is not a forecast that the next trade must rise.

## Auction instructions separate price limits from participation

<FillBlank client:visible
  question="Recall the acceptance rule before choosing an auction instruction"
  text="A buy limit is a {{maximum acceptable price|minimum acceptable price|guaranteed execution price}}. A sell limit is a {{minimum acceptable price|maximum acceptable price|guaranteed execution price}}. Passing the price test establishes {{eligibility, not a guaranteed full fill|priority over every other order|a right to execute at the order’s own limit}}."
  explanation="Acceptable price, auction eligibility, execution priority and the quantity allocated are separate parts of the instruction and matching rule."
/>

An **on-open** or **on-close** instruction targets the specified auction rather than an arbitrary continuous-market trade. Common US labels, defined in the [Nasdaq equity trading rules](https://listingcenter.nasdaq.com/rulebook/nasdaq/rules/nasdaq-equity-4), include:

| Instruction | Price constraint | Intended matching event |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market on open, MOO | No investor-specified limit price | Opening auction |
| Limit on open, LOO | A maximum buy or minimum sell price | Opening auction |
| Market on close, MOC | No investor-specified limit price | Closing auction |
| Limit on close, LOC | A maximum buy or minimum sell price | Closing auction |

The exact supported instructions, eligibility, cutoffs and unfilled-order treatment vary. For example, [Borsa Italiana’s ETC/ETN opening-auction description](https://www.borsaitaliana.it/etc-etn/perintermediari/astadiapertura/astadiapertura.en.htm) permits limit orders but not market orders in that particular auction. A normal market order sent near the close is not automatically an MOC. A buy LOC at €10.15 cannot execute at an auction price of €10.20; accepting uncertainty through a market instruction instead does not guarantee a particular price or even a cross when the auction cannot execute.

**Allocation** assigns the matched shares to eligible orders after the price is selected. In the 20-market-buy example, €10.10 has 140 eligible buys but only 100 eligible sells, so at least 40 eligible buy shares remain unexecuted.

For an additional **illustrative allocation rule**, suppose market buys have first priority, then better-priced buy limits, then buy limits exactly at the auction price. The 20 market buys plus 30 buys at €10.30 and 50 at €10.20 consume all 100 matched shares. The 40 buys limited at €10.10 are eligible but receive none. A different venue's priority rules must be checked before claiming a particular order's fill.

### When to use auction-specific instructions

Use the venue and broker's actual instruction when execution at the opening or closing event is the objective. A limit expresses price discipline while accepting nonexecution risk. An unlimited auction instruction accepts the resulting price subject to venue protections; it does not promise last night's close, the current quote or unlimited liquidity.

## An indicative price is a conditional snapshot

An **indicative auction price** reports what a specified calculation would produce from the eligible information at that moment. It is conditional on that information and the venue's calculation rules. A **paired quantity** reports the shares currently matchable under a specified calculation; it does not reserve them for your order.

In the toy book, compare three allowed snapshots before the freeze:

| Market buys | Market sells | Selected price with reference €10.14 | Matched shares | Imbalance there |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 20 | 10 | €10.10 | 100 | +40 buys |
| 20 | 50 | €10.10 | 140 | 0 |
| 60 | 10 | €10.20 | 140 | 0 |

Adding 40 opposing market sells removes the initial buy imbalance and increases matched volume **without raising the selected price**. Adding 40 market buys instead selects €10.20, holding everything else fixed. These are conditional calculations, not probabilities of future orders arriving.

### No match means no execution price

Consider another book with 100 shares willing to buy only at €10.00 and 100 willing to sell only at €10.30, with no market orders. Every allowed price has $V=0$. A reference of €10.14 cannot manufacture a trade between incompatible instructions. Our model returns **no cross and no execution price**, not the candidate closest to the reference.

### When to read the feed

Read the field definitions, timestamp, venue, eligible population and calculation price together. A total imbalance, a market-only imbalance and a reference-price paired quantity can differ without any arithmetic error. A fast update is still a snapshot, and a broker display may add another delivery delay.

## Deadlines, collars and benchmarks change the practical decision

<MCQ client:visible pretest

    { text: 'Whether any close order must fill at the current indication', correct: false },

/>

A **cutoff** or **freeze** restricts certain order actions before uncrossing. A **price collar** constrains an auction calculation or execution relative to defined references. Neither term supplies a universal timetable or fallback rule.

The [NYSE auction page](https://www.nyse.com/trade/auctions) gives NYSE a 3:50 p.m. Eastern MOC/LOC cutoff with defined offsetting-order exceptions, while Arca has different freezes. A NYSE security can open after the 9:30 a.m. process start. These are venue and session details, not universal deadlines.

Borsa Italiana’s linked ETC/ETN description provides for extensions after a static price-threshold breach. More generally, if an indicated price breaches a protection threshold, the relevant rule may extend, delay, constrain or prevent the auction. Do not assume the venue simply pins the price to your preferred boundary and fills everything. Our numerical panel omits those mechanisms and allocation details; its tick grid is an explicit modelling assumption, not a reproduction of an exchange collar.

### A closing benchmark does not remove economic cost

An investor may want an execution matched to an official close, for example when tracking an index. [NYSE's research on rebalance auctions](https://www.nyse.com/data-insights/closing-auction-liquidity-momentum-around-rebalances) discusses this concentration of closing interest. That common benchmark is useful, but it is not a guarantee of negligible price impact or a price known at order entry.

Suppose an invented buyer's decision reference is €20.00, and 1,000 shares execute in the closing cross at €20.02. Ignoring fees, the cost relative to that reference is

$$
1{,}000(20.02-20.00)=20\quad\text{euros},\qquad
\left(\frac{20.02}{20.00}-1\right)10{,}000=10\quad\text{basis points}.
$$

Against the €20.02 official closing benchmark, price slippage is zero. Against the €20.00 decision reference, it is €20. Both statements can be true. Neither identifies how much of the price change your own order caused; that requires a counterfactual model. Fees and any unexecuted quantity are separate costs or risks.

### When to choose an auction benchmark

Define whether the objective is a price limit, participation in a specific event, execution quantity or tracking a closing benchmark. Check the exact security, venue, broker deadline, permitted amendments and unfilled-order treatment. Pooling liquidity changes how prices are discovered; it does not eliminate adverse selection, information risk or the need to state the benchmark used to measure cost.
