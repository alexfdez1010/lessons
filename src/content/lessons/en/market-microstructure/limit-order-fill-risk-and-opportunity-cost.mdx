A buy limit at **99.98** is cheaper than buying immediately at **100.02**. That is a price comparison for shares that actually trade. It does not yet compare the cost of acquiring **1,000 shares by a deadline**.

If the passive order misses a rally and the investor later buys at 100.30, its four-cent saving on other fills can lose the comparison. We will state a purchase objective, build joint price-and-fill scenarios, and distinguish cash spent, a completed purchase and the performance of shares already acquired.

The examples use dollars and one invented stock during normal continuous trading, not an opening or closing auction. Prices, queues, probabilities, fees and future liquidity are hypothetical. A later quote is assumed executable for the stated remaining quantity, with enough funding. Those are model assumptions, not guarantees about real market orders.

## Price protection does not determine your execution quantity

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Your buy limit equals a price seen on the tape, but 600 eligible shares are ahead of you in the toy queue. Does that trade print prove your 200-share order filled?" options={[
 {text:'No. Eligibility, priority and the opposing volume reaching your order still matter',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes. A price touching the limit fills every waiting order'},
 {text:'Yes. The order must fill even if its venue receives no eligible seller'},
]} explanation="A trade price is not an allocation record for your order. Earlier orders may consume the eligible flow, and another venue’s trade need not reach your queue." />

A **buy limit** sets a maximum execution price; a **sell limit** sets a minimum. A **passive order** waits rather than immediately crossing available opposite-side liquidity. A limit can also be marketable, so “limit” and “passive” are not synonyms. The [SEC's order-type bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-14) explains price protection, possible nonexecution, partial fills and additional instructions.

A queue resembles a ticket line: seeing someone served does not mean your ticket was called. Under our **invented FIFO rule**, first in gets served first at one fixed price. Let $A$ be shares ahead, $V$ eligible opposing volume processed there and $Q$ your order quantity. With no cancellations or competing priority rules,

$$
F=\min\{Q,\max(0,V-A)\}
$$

is your filled quantity. This is a toy allocation, not a rule attributed to every exchange or broker. Actual price, display and time priorities, reserve interest, routing and cancellation rules must be checked.

### Follow the queue before counting your fill

Place 200 shares behind 600. The first 500 eligible opposing shares execute against earlier orders: **your fill is zero** and 100 remain ahead. A second arrival of 250 consumes those 100 and reaches 150 of your shares. Total eligible volume is now 750:

$$
F=\min(200,750-600)=150,\qquad Q-F=50.
$$

If total eligible volume reaches 1,000, only 200 can fill your order; the other 200 beyond the earlier 600 and your 200 belong elsewhere. Neither a larger print nor a stronger wish increases the order you actually submitted.

## Compare policies against the same purchase objective

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="One strategy spends 99,980 acquiring all 1,000 shares. Another spends 39,992 acquiring only 400. Which comparison is justified before considering the missing quantity?" options={[
 {text:'Only that the second spent less cash and acquired fewer shares',correct:true},
 {text:'The second completed the same purchase more cheaply'},
 {text:'The second must have earned the difference as profit'},
]} explanation="Different quantities are different outcomes. Define whether completing 1,000 shares is required before ranking strategies by the amount paid." />

Our objective is **buy 1,000 shares by a stated decision deadline**. At arrival, the midpoint reference is 100, best bid 99.98 and best ask 100.02. Assume sufficient initial ask depth for an immediate 1,000-share purchase, costing $1{,}000(100.02)=100{,}020$ before fees. The arrival reference value is $1{,}000(100)=100{,}000$, so its cost relative to that reference is **20**.

The alternative first posts a passive limit at 99.98. Before the completion deadline, it requests cancellation at a prescribed review time, receives confirmation of the final passive quantity, and completes the missing amount at the specified later ask. We assume this sequence can finish by the deadline. We assume the listed later quantity is available and funding is sufficient; for this initial case, 100,400 cash covers every listed purchase even with the later hypothetical fees. This does not establish a buffer for unlisted market outcomes.

We need a **joint scenario**: price and fill quantity together. Treating a fill probability as independent of every future price is an extra assumption, not something a limit order supplies.

| Joint scenario | Assumed probability | Passive fill at 99.98 | Deadline ask for the rest | Total purchase cash | Cost versus 100,000 reference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Rally, no passive fill | 25% | 0 | 100.30 | 100,300 | +300 |
| Stable, full passive fill | 35% | 1,000 | 100.02 | 99,980 | −20 |
| Full fill before a selloff | 25% | 1,000 | 99.72 | 99,980 | −20 |
| Partial fill, then a higher ask | 15% | 400 | 100.10 | 100,052 | +52 |

The full fills occur **before** the later state price. The selloff row does not claim that a resting buy at 99.98 trades above the contemporaneous ask of 99.72. It says the shares were purchased and prices subsequently fell.

In the partial row, passive cash is $400(99.98)=39{,}992$. Completing the remaining 600 costs $600(100.10)=60{,}060$. Total cash is $39{,}992+60{,}060=100{,}052$, so reference cost is 52.

### Expected cost needs explicit scenario probabilities

An **expected value** weights each possible amount by its assumed probability. It is not the amount that must occur on one order. The passive-then-complete policy has expected reference cost

$$
E[C]=0.25(300)+0.35(-20)+0.25(-20)+0.15(52)
=75-7-5+7.8=70.80.
$$

The immediate policy costs 20 in this same model. The expected difference is $70.80-20=50.80$ against waiting, even though every passive fill is four cents below the initial ask. This is a result of these joint scenarios, not a universal ranking of market and limit orders.

Best execution is not established by inspecting only the cheapest filled share.

## Separate some execution, full execution and expected quantity

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="The four states have probabilities 25%, 35%, 25% and 15%, with passive fills 0, 1,000, 1,000 and 400. Which pair describes this model?" options={[
 {text:'75% chance of some execution; 660 expected passive-filled shares',correct:true},
 {text:'75% chance of a full fill; 750 expected shares'},
 {text:'66% chance of any execution; 1,000 shares in every state'},
]} explanation="Some execution occurs in the last three states: 35% + 25% + 15% = 75%. Expected quantity is 0.35 × 1,000 + 0.25 × 1,000 + 0.15 × 400 = 660." />

The **probability of any execution** is $P(F>0)=75\%$. The **probability of a full fill** is $P(F=1{,}000)=35\%+25\%=60\%$. The **expected filled fraction** is

$$
\frac{E[F]}{Q}=\frac{350+250+60}{1{,}000}=66\%.
$$

These answer three different questions. None says a particular order will fill exactly 660 shares. A reported “fill rate” also needs a denominator, time window, eligible order population and treatment of partial fills. The [SEC staff's 2026 Rule 605 FAQs](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/trading-markets-frequently-asked-questions/frequently-asked-questions-rule-605-regulation-nms) discuss defined reporting populations and unexecuted portions; our illustrative probabilities are not that reporting metric or evidence about a real broker.

### Cancellation leaves a quantity question

Suppose the investor **cancels the remainder and does not complete**. Expected passive purchase cash is $660(99.98)=65{,}986.80$ before fees; expected missing quantity is $1{,}000-660=340$. This lower expenditure does not achieve the full-purchase objective.

A separate opportunity measure can value the missing target at a declared **deadline midpoint** $M_T$. For an actual passive fill $F$, its signed reference metric is

$$
C_{cancel}=F(L-P_0)+(Q-F)(M_T-P_0),
$$

One way to derive it is to compare the intended target’s marked profit, $Q(M_T-P_0)$, with the passive position’s marked profit, $F(M_T-L)$. Subtracting the second from the first and rearranging gives the two terms above. Here $L=99.98$ is the fill price and $P_0=100$ the arrival reference. The second term is an **opportunity cost relative to that benchmark**, not an invoice. An unfilled 200-share target after a midpoint rise from 100 to 100.30 has a +60 term; a fall to 99.70 gives −60. Neither result changes the cash actually paid for those unbought shares, which is zero.

For our original states, deadline midpoints are **100.28, 100.00, 99.70 and 100.08**. In the partial state,

$$
C_{cancel}=400(99.98-100)+600(100.08-100)=-8+48=40.
$$

The four cancellation metrics are 280, −20, −20 and 40, giving $0.25(280)+0.60(-20)+0.15(40)=64$. That is distinct from both the 65,986.80 expected cash outlay and the 70.80 full-completion reference cost. The 6.80 difference from completing is the expected later ask-minus-midpoint payment on the previously missing shares in these states.

### When an opportunity measure helps

Use it to ask whether a cheaper recorded fill left the investment objective unfulfilled. State the benchmark and time. Do not add the missing-target term again to the completed policy: once those shares have been bought, their actual purchase cash already enters that policy's reference cost. Different terminal holdings also have different subsequent risks.

## Ask which price states generated the fills

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Could the same expected purchase cost hide worse post-fill inventory performance if more full fills happen before a price fall rather than in the stable state?" options={[
 {text:'Yes. The earlier purchase price can stay the same while later inventory value changes',correct:true},
 {text:'No. Equal purchase cash makes every future price distribution identical'},
 {text:'No. A passive fill must always be followed by a price increase'},
]} explanation="Stable and selloff full fills both cost 99,980 here. Their different subsequent midpoints change the value of the shares held, not the cash already paid." />

**Selection** means the executed subset need not resemble all intended orders. **Adverse selection** concerns trading in circumstances where information or subsequent price changes work against the liquidity provider. A negative observed post-fill return by itself does not prove that the counterparty had private information: ordinary price moves and the chosen measurement horizon can also contribute.

Define **post-fill markout** here as $(M_T-L)$ per passively filled share, measured at the same stated deadline. This uses one decision endpoint, rather than a fixed elapsed interval after every individual fill. It is not the order's transaction price and is not guaranteed realised sale profit. In the stable state it is $100-99.98=+0.02$; before the selloff it is $99.70-99.98=-0.28$; in the partial state it is $100.08-99.98=+0.10$.

### Keep the share weighting explicit

There are no passively filled shares in the rally row. The probability-weighted markout of the passive positions is

$$
E[F(M_T-L)]=0.35(1{,}000)(0.02)+0.25(1{,}000)(-0.28)
+0.15(400)(0.10)=7-70+6=-57.
$$

Divide by **expected passive-filled shares**, not by the target or merely by the probability of any fill:

$$
\frac{E[F(M_T-L)]}{E[F]}=\frac{-57}{660}\approx-0.086364
$$

per expected executed share. This is a share-weighted statistic of the stated joint model. It is not the same as averaging one per-order markout across only the orders with some execution; partial order sizes change the weighting.

If ten percentage points move from stable to selloff, the probabilities become **25%, 25%, 35%, 15%**. The expected quantity stays 660 and complete purchase cost stays 70.80, since both full-fill rows have the same cash cost. Expected passive markout falls to $5-98+6=-87$, or $-87/660\approx-0.131818$ per expected filled share.

### When to examine conditional performance

Use it when evaluating whether a price saving comes with an undesirable inventory path. Match the horizon, side, reference and weighting. The immediate purchaser also suffers a price fall in the selloff state; a negative passive markout alone does not establish that the passive policy loses to every alternative in that state. Likewise, do not add the −57 again to the full-completion purchase cost: one measures later asset performance, the other measures purchase cash versus arrival.

## Test beliefs and fees before choosing the cheaper policy

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="In the original no-fee model, suppose rally probability falls from 25% to 9%, replacing only stable probability. Selloff remains 25% and partial 15%. Does a past 70.80 expected cost automatically remain valid?" options={[
 {text:'No. Recalculate with the new joint probabilities',correct:true},
 {text:'Yes. A fill probability has no effect on expected cost'},
 {text:'No. The actual future cost must now equal zero'},
]} explanation="Expected cost is conditional on all the scenario probabilities. A changed input changes the calculation; it does not establish which model is empirically correct." />

Let rally probability be $r$, keeping the selloff at 25%, partial state at 15%, original partial quantity and prices fixed. Stable probability is the remainder, $0.60-r$, so $0\le r\le0.60$. Then

$$
E[C]=300r-20(0.60-r)-20(0.25)+52(0.15)
=320r-9.20.
$$

This equals the immediate reference cost of 20 when $r=(20+9.20)/320=0.09125$, or **9.125%**. At 9% it is $320(0.09)-9.20=19.60$; at 10% it is 22.80. Those inputs reverse the expected ranking. The threshold is a property of this invented model, not a known probability that decides all real limit orders.

### Fees follow the quantity that actually trades

Now assume the broker passes through an invented **0.002 maker rebate per passive-filled share** and charges **0.003 per aggressively filled share**. There are no minimum fees or intermediate rounding. These are assumptions, not current universal tariffs, and a venue rebate need not be passed to a client.

| State | Maker credit | Fee to complete | Complete reference cost after these fees |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Rally | 0 | $1{,}000(0.003)=3$ | 303 |
| Stable full fill | $1{,}000(0.002)=2$ | 0 | −22 |
| Full fill before selloff | 2 | 0 | −22 |
| Partial | $400(0.002)=0.80$ | $600(0.003)=1.80$ | 53 |

Expected maker credit is $660(0.002)=1.32$ and expected completion fee is $340(0.003)=1.02$. Net fees are $1.02-1.32=-0.30$. Expected full reference cost becomes $70.80-0.30=70.50$; the immediate policy pays $1{,}000(0.003)=3$, taking its cost from 20 to **23**. The expected difference is still **47.50** against the passive-then-complete policy at the original probabilities.

<LimitOrderScenarioBook client:visible title="Attach hypothetical fees and rebates to executed quantities" showFees makerRebate={0.002} takerFee={0.003} />

With the other probabilities fixed, $E[F]=1{,}000(0.85-r)+0.15(400)=910-1{,}000r$. Net fees are $0.003(1{,}000-E[F])-0.002E[F]=3-0.005(910-1{,}000r)=-1.55+5r$. Adding this to the price cost $320r-9.20$ gives the fee-adjusted sensitivity $E[C]=325r-10.75$. It equals the immediate 23 at $r=33.75/325\approx10.3846\%$. The fee assumptions change the threshold, rather than making every passive order worthwhile. Use one consistent fill-price basis; do not subtract the spread again after using actual ask prices.

### When to challenge the forecast

Ask how probabilities were estimated, whether the sample contains unfilled orders, whether price and fills were modelled jointly, and whether the future quote quantities could really trade. Include uncertainty and alternative plausible assumptions. Comparing two expected costs is useful only after checking the objective, constraints and consequences of the states the model omits.

## Make the remainder and the deadline part of the order decision

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Your passive order appears unfilled, so you submit a replacement purchase before confirming the cancellation. Which additional risk have you introduced?" options={[
 {text:'Both orders could execute and exceed the intended quantity',correct:true},
 {text:'The first order can no longer trade merely because a cancellation was requested'},
 {text:'The limit automatically follows the price to avoid a duplicate position'},
]} explanation="A cancellation request is not the same as a confirmed cancelled remainder. Track execution reports and the broker's handling rules before assuming the old order cannot fill." />

An **order policy** includes what to submit, how long to wait, what changes trigger review and what happens to the unfilled remainder. It may use a passive limit, a marketable limit with a price boundary, an auction-specific instruction for an auction objective, or no trade. An investor who values a particular price more than completion has a different objective from one required to acquire a fixed quantity promptly.

**IOC**, immediate-or-cancel, allows an immediate eligible partial fill and cancels the rest. **FOK**, fill-or-kill, requires immediate full execution or cancellation. Their precise availability and handling depend on the venue and broker. Neither instruction creates a seller. The order-type bulletin distinguishes such conditions; the **limit price** and the **time/quantity instruction** describe different constraints.

### Audit one complete decision

For the initial passive-then-complete example, a checkable plan records:

| Question | Explicit assumption or instruction in our example |
|---|---|
| What must be bought? | 1,000 shares of one stock |
| What is the cost reference? | Arrival midpoint 100 |
| First action | Passive limit 99.98 |
| Remainder policy | Request cancellation early enough to confirm the final fill, then complete by the deadline under the stated assumptions |
| What makes that completion calculation possible? | Stated deadline ask and depth, funding and operational availability are assumed |
| Which scenarios enter the estimate? | Four joint price/fill states with named probabilities |
| Which other performance measure is separate? | Later midpoint markout on the shares already held |
| What does the estimate not prove? | Actual fills, the true probabilities or a maximum possible future cost |

At arrival, the reference value is 100,000. In the rally row, the completed policy pays 100,300 before fees, a +300 reference cost; against the immediate 100,020, waiting costs 280 more in that row. In the stable row, passive completion costs 99,980, saving 40 against the immediate policy. State-by-state differences and their weights produce the expected 50.80 difference; no individual row is replaced by the average.

### When to wait, cross or abandon the plan

Use the policy that fits the actual price, quantity and timing objective under plausible scenarios and constraints. Check halts, outages, partial fills, routing, queue changes, changing information and the money needed to finish. A marketable limit can bound its execution price while accepting remaining execution risk; replacing one instruction with another does not make all constraints disappear. Recheck after material new information rather than treating an old estimate as permanent.
