You pay \$100 now and receive \$105 in a year. The cash gain is \$5. Yet if the comparison rate is 10%, the schedule has a negative net present value. The two statements use different references, so they can both be correct.

**Net present value (NPV)** applies the signed timeline method you already know: convert every receipt and outlay to date zero, then add them. It measures value in money units under stated assumptions. It is not a percentage return or a guarantee that the forecast will happen.

## Separate the cash gain from value at the comparison rate

Think of a target line: finishing ahead of the starting point does not necessarily put you ahead of the target. Here the target is the value implied by a **comparable alternative**, not a universal return everyone can obtain.

The **opportunity-cost discount rate** is the assumed return required for giving up a comparable alternative. It should match the payments' timing, currency and risk. Choosing 10% in an example does not mean a safe account offers it, and discounting a receipt does not make it certain.

Let $C_t$ be a signed cash flow at date $t$, with receipts positive and outlays negative. At a constant effective rate $r$ per period:

$$NPV=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}.$$

The date-zero amount has exponent zero and factor one. If the initial outlay is $I>0$, then $C_0=-I$. Do not include that negative flow and subtract the same initial cost again.

### Work through a nominal gain with negative NPV

Assume a \$100 payment now and a \$105 receipt exactly one year later, in the same currency, with no other costs. Use 10% as an illustrative effective annual comparison rate.

Under the comparison model, \$100 today corresponds to \$110 in a year. The scheduled \$105 is \$5 below that future reference. Bringing the shortfall back gives $-5/1.10\approx-4.545455$ dollars, agreeing with the NPV.

### When to use the sign of NPV

Positive NPV means the scheduled receipts exceed the scheduled outlays in present-value terms at the chosen rate. Zero means they just match that reference. Negative means they fall short. None of the signs establishes affordability, payment certainty or personal suitability.

The [CFA Research Foundation's NPV appendix](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/sites/default/files/-/media/documents/book/rf-publication/1996/rf-v1996-n2-4456-pdf.pdf) includes both inflows and outflows and relates the discount rate to the required return on capital. The rate is part of the model being assessed.

## Include every relevant dated receipt and outlay

<MCQ client:visible pretest

A receipt-only valuation leaves the bill outside the calculation. Start from a complete signed schedule, using **incremental cash flows**: the cash amounts that change because the option is undertaken rather than the stated alternative.

That can include a genuine opportunity cost, such as cash income forgone by using a resource for this option. A cost already included elsewhere must not be counted again. This example simply lists all the assumed cash changes; it does not treat accounting revenue as an automatic cash receipt.

### Add two receipts and a future cost

Use an assumed 10% effective annual rate.

  <thead><tr><th scope="col">Date</th><th scope="col">Cash change</th><th scope="col">Present-value calculation</th><th scope="col">Value today</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>

    <tr><th scope="row">One year</th><td>Receipt +\$110</td><td>$110/1.10$</td><td>+\$100</td></tr>
    <tr><th scope="row">Two years</th><td>Receipt +\$242</td><td>$242/1.10^2$</td><td>+\$200</td></tr>

Therefore:

$$NPV=-280+\frac{110}{1.10}+\frac{242}{1.21}-\frac{12.10}{1.21}=10.$$

The receipts have present value \$300, and the outlays have present cost \$290. Their difference is **\$10 today**. The nominal signed total is $-280+110+242-12.10=59.90$ dollars, which is a different quantity.

### When to trust the schedule

Check that all receipts and costs use the same viewpoint, currency and price assumptions, including any relevant charges or taxes. Payments must be funded when due: a positive NPV is not a bank balance available to pay the initial bill.

These examples use cash paid and received without a separate loan model. Borrowing adds obligations and timing constraints; a borrowing rate is not automatically a suitable project discount rate. Financing flows and the discounting framework need consistent treatment rather than mixing costs into the model twice.

OpenStax's [NPV method](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance/pages/16-2-net-present-value-npv-method) likewise converts individual receipts and outlays before comparing their present values.

A percentage is a rate per unit of starting money; NPV is an amount of value. Neither unit can stand in for the other.

### State which alternatives are actually available

**Feasible** means possible under the stated constraints. **Mutually exclusive** means selecting one rules out the other. If A and B are each available only once, only one may be selected, both can be funded and the comparison assumptions fit, B has the larger model NPV.

With only \$1,000 available and no financing, B is not feasible. Its larger NPV does not supply its own \$10,000 outlay. A is the feasible positive-NPV project in that restricted set; choosing not to proceed remains an alternative.

A different rule changes the problem again. If A can be repeated **ten times independently** with unchanged amounts and risk, a \$10,000 package has receipt \$12,000 and NPV:

$$-10000+\frac{12000}{1.10}\approx909.090909.$$

That package adds more model value than a single B. The original comparison did not establish that copies were available. Do not assume unlimited repetition from one attractive percentage.

### When to use a value ranking

Use NPV to compare value added among **comparable feasible choices**. Specify the budget, exclusivity, availability of repeat copies, horizon and how unused capital is treated. In the simple one-year comparison, assume unused funds can remain in the stated comparable alternative; their NPV against that reference is zero. A real borrowing plan or a different risk basis may change the feasible set and the appropriate inputs.

OpenStax's [project-comparison discussion](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance/pages/16-5-choosing-between-projects) distinguishes scale, mutually exclusive choices and limited resources. The higher-NPV rule needs a clearly defined decision problem.

## Test the rate and the receipts without inventing probabilities

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="You increase the discount rate while keeping the schedule's cash amounts fixed. What have you changed?"
  options={[

A **sensitivity check** here changes one valuation input while holding the rest fixed. A **scenario** specifies a different set of assumed cash amounts. They answer different questions, and neither supplies probabilities unless those are separately justified.

### Change only the discount rate

Keep the multi-date schedule: −\$280 now, +\$110 in year one, +\$242 and −\$12.10 in year two.

At 15%, for example:

$$NPV=-280+\frac{110}{1.15}+\frac{242-12.10}{1.15^2}\approx-10.510397.$$

The later amounts have not changed. In this schedule an initial outlay is followed by positive **net** receipts, so raising the positive discount rate reduces NPV. That is not a universal claim about every possible arrangement of later negative and positive flows.

### Change a receipt instead

Now hold the rate at 10%, the initial outlay at \$280, the first receipt at \$110 and cleanup at \$12.10. Vary only the second receipt:

Reducing the second receipt by \$40 changes NPV by $-40/1.10^2\approx-33.057851$ dollars. Thus $10-33.057851\approx-23.057851$. The arithmetic isolates the effect of that input.

These are three assumed cases, not a measured probability distribution. The lower receipt is not a guaranteed worst case, and taking an unweighted average of the rows does not establish an expected NPV.

### When to investigate further

A result that changes sign under plausible inputs deserves closer examination of the forecast and required rate. Consider how payment delays, missing receipts, costs and financing could interact. NPV summarises the selected cash-flow model; it does not measure every possible loss, nonfinancial consequence or need for cash before a receipt arrives.
