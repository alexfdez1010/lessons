Three equal payments can have different values even when the amount, count and rate match. Move every payment one period earlier and you change every conversion factor. You have not added a payment; you have changed the calendar.

The previous lesson valued each dated flow separately. Here a repeating pattern lets you shorten the sum. The timeline remains the way to check the shortcut. Our examples assume fixed payments and a stated constant effective rate per period, with no fees or taxes. Those assumptions do not guarantee real payments or investment returns.

## Identify the payment pattern and its first date

A **finite level-payment annuity** is a known number of equal payments at equal intervals. Here “annuity” names a mathematical payment schedule; it does not identify an insurance product or guarantee anything about who will pay.

Let $P$ be each payment, $n$ the payment count and $r$ the effective rate for one payment interval. The first date determines which standard schedule you have.

- An **ordinary annuity** pays at the **end** of each period: dates $1,2,\ldots, n$.
- An **annuity due** pays at the **beginning** of each period: dates $0,1,\ldots, n-1$.

Think of two identical sets of appointment cards. Moving each appointment earlier changes the dates, not the number of cards.

### Count the payments, not the largest date label

For three annual receipts of \$100:

| Schedule | Date zero | Date one | Date two | Date three | Payment count |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary | — | \$100 | \$100 | \$100 | Three |
| Due | \$100 | \$100 | \$100 | — | Three |

Both nominal totals are $3(100)=300$ dollars. The ordinary schedule's last payment date is three; the due schedule's is two. The interval between the first and last payments is two periods in either schedule. None of those facts changes the count of three payments.

### When to recognise the pattern

Use it for a finite sequence such as fixed monthly payments or equal annual receipts. Check whether the first payment is immediate, whether there is a final extra payment, and whether the amounts really stay equal. A label such as “three-year plan” does not specify the dates by itself.

## Derive the present value of an ordinary annuity

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="An ordinary schedule begins one period from now. What is the correct first step in valuing it today?"
  options={[
    {text:'Discount the first payment for one period', correct:true},
    {text:'Treat the first payment as an immediate receipt'},
    {text:'Discount every payment only for the final date'},
    {text:'Multiply the payment count by the last payment'},
  ]}
  explanation="The first ordinary-annuity payment is at date one. Later payments have longer waits, so each uses its own exponent before the values are added."
/>

For an ordinary annuity, the timeline sum is:

$$PV_{\mathrm{ord}}=P\left(\frac{1}{1+r}+\frac{1}{(1+r)^2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{(1+r)^n}\right).$$

Every term is still one converted payment. A **geometric sum** means adjacent terms share the same multiplying factor. Here that factor is $x=1/(1+r)$.

### Turn the repeated sum into a shortcut

Write the sum of factors as $S=x+x^2+\cdots+x^n$. Multiply it by $x$ and subtract:

$$xS=x^2+x^3+\cdots+x^{n+1}.$$

$$S-xS=x-x^{n+1},\qquad S=\frac{x(1-x^n)}{1-x}.$$

Substituting $x=1/(1+r)$ gives, for $r\ne 0$:

$$PV_{\mathrm{ord}}=P\frac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r}.$$

The shortcut removes repeated arithmetic; it does not change any payment dates. A zero rate needs the direct-sum case below rather than division by zero.

### Work through the three receipts

Use three end-of-year receipts of \$100 and an assumed 10% effective annual rate.

| Payment date | Present-value calculation | Value before rounding to cents |
|---|---|---|
| One | $100/1.10$ | \$90.90909 |
| Two | $100/1.10^2$ | \$82.64463 |
| Three | $100/1.10^3$ | \$75.13148 |

Keeping the unrounded values until the end:

$$PV_{\mathrm{ord}}=100\frac{1-1.10^{-3}}{0.10}\approx 248.685199.$$

The value today is approximately **\$248.69**. Its factor of about 2.486852 is less than the payment count because each positive receipt is discounted at a positive rate.

### When to use the shortcut

Use it when payments and intervals are equal, the periodic rate is constant, and the first payment is one period after the valuation date. If the first payment is delayed further, move the result from its own reference date to the required date. For irregular schedules, return to the individual-flow method.

## Shift the payments earlier without changing their count

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Every positive payment moves one period earlier while the payment count and positive rate stay fixed. How does its value at the original present date change?"
  options={[
    {text:'It increases because every payment is discounted less', correct:true},
    {text:'It stays equal because the nominal payment total is unchanged'},
    {text:'It falls because the payment count loses a period'},
    {text:'It increases by adding a separate extra payment'},
  ]}
  explanation="Shifting the schedule earlier reduces every discount exponent by one. The number and amounts of payments stay unchanged."
/>

An annuity due is the ordinary schedule shifted one period earlier. A payment previously valued as $P/(1+r)^t$ becomes $P/(1+r)^{t-1}$, which is its old value multiplied by $1+r$. The same factor applies to every term:

$$PV_{\mathrm{due}}=(1+r)PV_{\mathrm{ord}}.$$

### Check by both the shift and the direct sum

Move the three \$100 receipts from dates one, two and three to dates zero, one and two. At 10%:

$$PV_{\mathrm{due}}=100+\frac{100}{1.10}+\frac{100}{1.10^2}\approx 273.553719.$$

Check using the ordinary result:

$$248.6851990984(1.10)\approx 273.5537190083.$$

Rounded at the end, the due schedule is worth **\$273.55** today. The difference from the ordinary schedule is about **\$24.87**, not a whole extra \$100.

<CashFlowValuationTimeline client:visible
  title="Three beginning-of-period receipts"
  flows={[{label:'Immediate payment', time:0, amount:100},{label:'Second payment', time:1, amount:100},{label:'Third payment', time:2, amount:100}]}
  maxDate={3} rate={0.1} valuationDate={0}
/>

Adding \$100 to the ordinary present value would leave its last dated payment in place and create a fourth payment. The correct shift adds an immediate receipt **and removes the old final receipt**. Equivalently:

$$PV_{\mathrm{due}}-PV_{\mathrm{ord}}=P-\frac{P}{(1+r)^n}.$$

Here that difference is $100-100/1.10^3\approx 24.868520$ dollars.

### When the timing difference matters

Use the due form for a stated beginning-of-period schedule. For positive receipts and a positive rate it has the greater present value at the same date. For someone making the payments, earlier outflows have a larger present cost instead. “Due” is not a recommendation; the viewpoint still matters.

OpenStax's [annuity explanation separates end-of-period and beginning-of-period schedules](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance-2e/pages/8-2-annuities) and relates their present values through this one-period shift.

## State the future valuation date explicitly

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="You compare future values of ordinary and due schedules with the same payment count. What must be fixed before comparing the amounts?"
  options={[
    {text:'One common future valuation date', correct:true},
    {text:'Each schedule’s own final payment date'},
    {text:'Only the unchanged nominal payment total'},
    {text:'Only the date of the earliest receipt'},
  ]}
  explanation="A due schedule ends its payments earlier. Values calculated on different final payment dates need conversion before they can be compared."
/>

For an ordinary schedule, value the payments at **date $n$, immediately after its last payment**:

$$FV_{\mathrm{ord}, n}=P\left((1+r)^{n-1}+\cdots+(1+r)+1\right).$$

The first payment grows for $n-1$ periods; the last grows for zero. Multiplying this finite sum by $1+r$ and subtracting the original leaves $rFV_{\mathrm{ord}, n}=P((1+r)^n-1)$. Hence, for $r\ne 0$:

$$FV_{\mathrm{ord}, n}=P\frac{(1+r)^n-1}{r}.$$

At the **same date $n$**, every due payment has one additional period of growth:

$$FV_{\mathrm{due}, n}=(1+r)FV_{\mathrm{ord}, n}.$$

### Two schedules valued at the end of period three

| Schedule | Growth of the three payments to date three | Future value |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary, dates one to three | $100(1.10)^2+100(1.10)+100$ | **\$331.00** |
| Due, dates zero to two | $100(1.10)^3+100(1.10)^2+100(1.10)$ | **\$364.10** |

For the ordinary schedule, $100(1.331-1)/0.10=331$ dollars. For the due schedule, $331(1.10)=364.10$ dollars. These also equal their respective date-zero present values compounded for three periods.

Move the common date to two: the due total becomes **\$331**. That matches the ordinary schedule's date-three total numerically, but the dates differ. You cannot call them equal values without another conversion.

### When to use the future-value formula

Use it for a target balance at a specified date, with an explicit reinvestment assumption. The formula describes what the stated flows and constant rate would produce; actual accumulation still depends on payments, rates and costs. The [OpenStax accounting treatment](https://openstax.org/books/principles-managerial-accounting/pages/11-3-explain-the-time-value-of-money-and-calculate-present-and-future-values-of-lump-sums-and-annuities) also builds future value by growing each payment for its remaining periods.

## Check the zero rate and the payment-period unit

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="The assumed periodic rate is zero. How should equal finite payments be valued instead of dividing by the rate in the shortcut?"
  options={[

    {text:'Set the result to zero because interest is zero'},
    {text:'Drop the final payment to avoid division by zero'},
    {text:'Treat the mathematical error as an infinite value'},

  explanation="At a zero rate every conversion factor is one. The direct finite sum remains defined even though the written shortcut has a zero denominator."

For $r=0$, both present and future values are simply:

$$PV=FV=nP.$$

For three \$100 payments, the total is **\$300**, whether the payments begin now or one period later. Timing does not alter value under this zero-rate model; real preferences for access to cash may still differ.

The zero-rate **limit** of each factor is also the payment count. As $r$ approaches zero, each individual conversion factor approaches one, so the finite sum approaches $n$. That explains the limiting value without pretending that division by zero is allowed.

### A monthly schedule uses a monthly effective rate

For three month-end receipts of \$100 at an assumed **1% effective monthly rate**:

$$PV=100\left(\frac{1}{1.01}+\frac{1}{1.01^2}+\frac{1}{1.01^3}\right)\approx 294.098521.$$

The value at the end of month three is:

$$FV=100(1.01^2+1.01+1)=303.01.$$

Here $n=3$ counts monthly payments and $r=0.01$ is per month. Neither input represents years. A quoted annual rate first needs conversion according to its effective or nominal compounding convention.

### When to stop using the shortcut

Return to the individual-flow method if amounts vary, intervals are irregular, the rate changes by period or an extra final payment appears. Keep the equal-payment part separate only when you can state exactly which dates it covers.
