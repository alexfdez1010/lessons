A payment schedule offers you money next year and more the year after. Adding the figures tells you how much cash arrives in total. It does not tell you how much the schedule is worth today. Each payment has its own clock.

You already know how to move one amount between dates. Here you will apply that calculation to every payment separately, then add values expressed at one common date. All examples are invented; the rates are stated assumptions rather than current offers or guaranteed returns.

## Choose a viewpoint before assigning signs

A **cash flow** is money received or paid at a specified date. Use a positive sign for a receipt and a negative sign for a payment from the chosen person's viewpoint. Think of arrows crossing a wallet's boundary: the same transfer leaves one wallet and enters another.

A **timeline** places those flows on a common scale. **Date zero** is the starting valuation date; date one means one complete period later. A period might be a year or a month, but use one unit consistently.

### Record the same transaction from both sides

Suppose you lend \$100 now and receive \$110 exactly one year later.

| Date | Your viewpoint as lender | Other person's viewpoint as borrower |
|---|---|---|
| Zero | −\$100 | +\$100 |
| One year | +\$110 | −\$110 |

The lender's nominal signed sum is $-100+110=10$ dollars; the borrower's is $100-110=-10$ dollars. Those signs describe direction. They do not declare one side's transaction good and the other's bad, and the nominal sum is not a time-adjusted valuation.

  originalViewLabel="Lender viewpoint" reverseViewLabel="Borrower viewpoint"
/>

At the assumed 10% annual rate, the later \$110 has a date-zero value of $110/1.10=100$ dollars. Combined with the initial −\$100, the lender's date-zero sum is zero. Reversing both signs also gives zero; the payments and dates have not changed.

### When to use signs

Use a signed timeline for loans, payment plans, purchases with later receipts or any sequence with money moving both ways. Include a payment at date zero without discounting it. A negative flow is an outflow, not a negative interest rate.

## Discount each payment before adding

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="Two receipts arrive on different dates. To find their combined value today, what should you do?"
  options={[
    {text:'Discount each for its own wait, then add',correct:true},
    {text:'Add both amounts, then discount for the longest wait'},
    {text:'Add both amounts, then discount for the shortest wait'},
    {text:'Average their dates, then ignore their separate timing'},
  ]}
  explanation="Each receipt has a different number of periods between its payment date and today. Convert them separately; their present values can then be added."
/>

Adding dated amounts without conversion is like adding prices quoted in different currencies: the numerical sum hides a missing conversion. Here the difference is the valuation date, not the currency.

For a signed cash flow $C_t$ at date $t$, an assumed effective rate $r$ per period gives:

$$V_0=\sum_t\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}.$$

The symbol $\sum$ means “add the individual terms.” **Effective rate per period** means the complete growth factor for one period is $1+r$. The same rate is used here for each period, with no fees or taxes in the invented schedule.

### Two different waits produce one present value

Consider receipts of \$110 at the end of year one and \$242 at the end of year two. Use an assumed effective annual discount rate of 10%.

| Receipt date | Amount | Conversion to date zero | Value at date zero |
|---|---|---|---|
| One year | \$110 | $110/1.10$ | \$100 |
| Two years | \$242 | $242/1.10^2$ | \$200 |
| Combined | \$352 nominal | Add the converted values | **\$300** |

The first denominator is 1.10; the second is $1.10^2=1.21$. Therefore:

$$V_0=\frac{110}{1.10}+\frac{242}{1.21}=100+200=300.$$

<CashFlowValuationTimeline client:visible
  title="Different payment dates, one present value"
  flows={[{label:'First receipt',time:1,amount:110},{label:'Second receipt',time:2,amount:242}]}
  maxDate={2} rate={0.1} valuationDate={0}
/>

Discounting the combined \$352 for two years would give $352/1.21\approx290.91$ dollars. That incorrectly makes the first receipt wait two years. Discounting both for one year would give $352/1.10=320$ dollars and bring the second receipt forward too little.

### When to use additivity

Use the sum for unequal dated payments and include any outflows with their signs. For example, paying \$280 at date zero for these same receipts gives $-280+100+200=20$ dollars of net value at the assumed rate. That is a valuation difference under the inputs; it is not a promise of profit or a statement that the purchase suits your needs.

CFA Institute identifies [cash-flow additivity as a foundation of valuation](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/time-value-money). The condition that makes the arithmetic meaningful is expressing every term in comparable units at the same date.

## Move the whole schedule to another common date

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="You value a schedule at a date between its two receipts. How should the earlier and later receipts be treated?"
  options={[
    {text:'Compound the earlier one; discount the later one',correct:true},
    {text:'Discount both for the total length of the schedule'},
    {text:'Compound both for the total length of the schedule'},
    {text:'Ignore the earlier one because its payment is past'},
  ]}
  explanation="Compare every payment with the chosen valuation date. Earlier payments move forward and later payments move backward; a payment exactly on the date stays unchanged."
/>

The common date is a meeting point. Payments can approach it from either direction. Moving the meeting point does not change the original payment schedule.

For a common valuation date $\tau$:

$$V_\tau=\sum_t C_t(1+r)^{\tau-t}.$$

If $t$ is earlier than $\tau$, the exponent is positive and you compound. If $t$ is later, the exponent is negative and you discount. Equal dates give exponent zero and factor one.

### Value the two receipts at the end of year two

Keep the same \$110 at year one, \$242 at year two and 10% annual rate.

| Original payment | Distance to date two | Value at date two |
|---|---|---|
| \$110 at date one | Forward one year | $110(1.10)=121$ dollars |
| \$242 at date two | No time difference | \$242 |
| Total | Same common date | **\$363** |

The sum is $121+242=363$ dollars. Check it from the date-zero value:

$$V_2=V_0(1.10)^2=300(1.21)=363.$$

<CashFlowValuationTimeline client:visible
  title="Choose the common date"
  flows={[{label:'First receipt',time:1,amount:110},{label:'Second receipt',time:2,amount:242}]}
  maxDate={2} rate={0.1} valuationDate={2}
/>

At date one, the first receipt stays at \$110 and the second discounts to $242/1.10=220$ dollars. Their sum is **\$330**, also $300(1.10)$. The value changes with its date; the underlying receipts remain \$110 and \$242.

### When to use a future common date

Use it when a question asks for value at the end of a schedule or at a future target date. Treat the reinvestment rate as an assumption: actually accumulating the computed sum requires the stated payments and growth to occur. OpenStax's [mixed-stream examples convert each dated amount before summing](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance/pages/9-1-timing-of-cash-flows), using the same timing logic.

## Compare schedules with an explicit rate and period

<MCQ client:visible pretest
  question="One option pays now and another pays later. What information is needed before comparing their time-adjusted values?"
  options={[
    {text:'A comparable rate and clearly dated payments',correct:true},
    {text:'Only the larger total of the promised payments'},
    {text:'Only the number of items in each schedule'},
    {text:'A rate chosen solely to favour the preferred option'},
  ]}
  explanation="The conversion needs dates, amounts and an explicitly chosen rate. Its suitability depends on such things as risk, currency and timing; picking a convenient rate does not establish a fair comparison."
/>

Suppose a recipient compares **Option A: \$100 today** with **Option B: \$121 in two years**. Assume the payments are equally certain, in the same currency, with no fees or taxes. Compare at date zero.

| Assumed effective annual rate | Value of A | Value of B | Higher model value for the recipient |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | \$100 | $121/1.05^2\approx109.75$ dollars | B |
| 10% | \$100 | $121/1.10^2=100$ dollars | Equal |
| 15% | \$100 | $121/1.15^2\approx91.49$ dollars | A |

The later payment has the same nominal amount in every row. Changing the rate changes its conversion, which can change the ordering. The table is a sensitivity calculation, not a recommendation or a claim that those rates are available.

To display **B minus A**, give A's forgone immediate receipt a negative sign and B's later receipt a positive sign:

<CashFlowValuationTimeline client:visible
  title="Compare the later receipt with the immediate one"
  flows={[{label:'Forgone immediate receipt',time:0,amount:-100},{label:'Later alternative receipt',time:2,amount:121}]}
  maxDate={2} rate={0.1} valuationDate={0}
  totalLabel="Value of B minus A at the common date"
/>

A positive difference favours B under the specified comparison; zero means equal model values. It does not erase differences in access to cash or uncertainty.

### Match the period unit

An effective monthly rate belongs with a count of months. For an assumed **1% effective monthly rate**, a \$102.01 receipt two months away has present value:

$$V_0=\frac{102.01}{1.01^2}=\frac{102.01}{1.0201}=100.$$

Calling the wait two years while keeping that monthly rate would mix units. Dividing an effective annual rate by twelve also does not generally give the effective monthly rate; the growth factors must agree.

### When to trust the comparison

Check amounts, signs, dates, currency, risk and any omitted charges. A single flat rate is a simplifying assumption; this lesson does not choose a market rate for you. The calculation compares model values, while an actual decision may also depend on liquidity needs and whether the payments will arrive.
