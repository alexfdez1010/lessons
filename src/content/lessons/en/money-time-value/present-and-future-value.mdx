Someone offers you **\$100 today or \$100 in a year**. To compare them, suppose a deposit can earn a fixed 5% over that year and both payments have the same certainty. Receiving the money today gives you an extra year of possible interest. The useful question is how to express the two payments at the **same date** before choosing.

## Why Money Has a Clock

At a positive available return, money received today can grow before the later payment arrives. At 5%, \$100 becomes \$100 × 1.05 = **\$105** after one year. By contrast, \$100 received next year is still \$100 at that date. The **opportunity cost** of waiting is the return you give up. Compare both payments at the same date, using a rate appropriate to their risk, currency and timing.

That conversion runs in two directions, and they're mirror images of each other:

| Direction | Name | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Push money **forward** in time | **Future value** | What will today's money grow into? |
| Pull money **back** to today | **Present value** | What is tomorrow's money worth right now? |

<Callout variant="info" title="Keep the rate and the payment comparable">
The examples below assume a fixed positive annual rate, retained interest, no extra deposits, no withdrawals and no fees or taxes. Inflation changes purchasing power; uncertainty affects whether a payment arrives. A discount rate must match these assumptions. Adding a separate risk premium to a rate that already reflects the same risk would count it twice.
</Callout>

## Future Value: Money Growing Forward

**Future value (FV)** is the amount a sum today becomes after a stated number of periods at a stated return. The starting amount is **present value (PV)**. When each period's interest stays invested, the next period earns interest on the enlarged balance: **compounding**.

$$\text{FV} = \text{PV} \times (1 + r)^{t}$$

where **PV** is the amount today, **r** is the rate per period, and **t** is the number
of periods.

### Worked example

Suppose **\$1,000** earns a fixed **8% effective annual rate** for **ten years**, with all interest retained. This rate is an input to the example, not a promise that an account or investment will offer it:

$$\text{FV} = 1000 \times (1.08)^{10} = 1000 \times 2.1589 \approx \$2,159$$

Notice you didn't just earn `8% × 10 = 80%` (which would give \$1,800). You earned
**\$1,159**, because each year's 8% was charged on a bigger and bigger base. That extra
\$359 over the naive straight-line guess *is* the compounding.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The Rule of 72">
For a quick estimate of doubling time at a positive compound rate, divide 72 by the rate expressed as a percentage. At 8%, 72 ÷ 8 = **9 years**; 1.08^9 ≈ 1.999 confirms it is close. The rule is an approximation, most useful for moderate rates. It does not apply to a zero or negative return, and variable investment returns need not follow it.
</Callout>

## Present Value: Tomorrow's Money, Today's Price

Now flip the arrow. **Present value (PV)** answers the question that actually shows up
in real decisions: *someone promises me a sum in the future — what is that promise
worth to me right now?* Since money grows forward by multiplying by `(1 + r)`, pulling
it back to today means **dividing** by `(1 + r)` for each year you have to wait. That's
**discounting** — compounding played in reverse.

$$\text{PV} = \frac{\text{FV}}{(1 + r)^{t}}$$

### Worked example

Consider a single **\$1,000 payment in ten years**. If the annual discount rate appropriate to that payment is **8%**, what is its present value?

$$\text{PV} = \frac{1000}{(1.08)^{10}} = \frac{1000}{2.1589} \approx \$463$$

At the assumed 8% discount rate, the payment has a present value of about **\$463**. Checking the calculation in reverse, \$463.19 × 1.08^10 ≈ \$1,000. The equivalence depends on the chosen rate and the payment's risk; it does not say that any \$1,000 promise can be replicated with a safe deposit.

## The Discount Rate: The Dial That Sets the Price of Time

The rate **r** converts between dates. Going forward it is the growth rate; going backward it is the **discount rate**, the return used to value a later payment today. Holding a future payment fixed, a larger rate means a smaller amount is needed now to reach it. Here is the present value of a \$1,000 payment in thirty years at several assumed rates:

| Discount rate | PV of \$1,000 in 30 years | Cents on the dollar |
|---|---|---|
| 3% | ≈ \$412 | 41¢ |
| 6% | ≈ \$174 | 17¢ |
| 10% | ≈ \$57 | 6¢ |
| 15% | ≈ \$15 | 2¢ |

At 15%, \$1,000 ÷ 1.15^30 ≈ **\$15.10**. The payment is still \$1,000 in thirty years; only its value at today's date changes. Each extra year introduces another division by 1.15. For a zero discount rate, present and future values coincide; with a negative rate above −100%, the direction of the time effect reverses.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Choose a comparable alternative">
Use the required return on an alternative with comparable risk, timing and currency. If the comparison rate already prices the payment's risk, do not add that same risk premium again. The 3%, 6%, 10% and 15% figures above illustrate sensitivity; they are not current market quotes or recommendations.
</Callout>

## Compounding Frequency: The Rate Isn't the Whole Story

One subtlety trips people up: **how often** interest compounds matters, not just the
headline rate. The more frequently interest is added, the sooner it starts earning its
own interest. Take a **12% nominal annual rate** on \$1,000:

| Compounding | Periods/year | Growth factor | Value after 1 year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual | 1 | $(1.12)^1$ | \$1,120.00 |
| Quarterly | 4 | $(1.03)^4$ | \$1,125.51 |
| Monthly | 12 | $(1.01)^{12}$ | \$1,126.83 |
| Daily | 365 | $(1 + 0.12/365)^{365}$ | \$1,127.47 |

Same 12% on the label, but monthly compounding actually earns **12.68%** once you
account for interest-on-interest within the year. That "true" yearly rate has a name —
the *effective annual rate* — and untangling it from the *nominal* rate is exactly what
the **interest & yield** lesson digs into next.
