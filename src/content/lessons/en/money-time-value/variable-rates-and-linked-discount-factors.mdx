One year at 5%, the next at −10%, the last at 20%: what happens to 100? It becomes **113.4**. The three rates do not add to its total return, and replacing them with their arithmetic average gives a different answer. Each interval works on the balance left by the previous one.

[Earlier timelines](/money-time-value/cash-flow-timelines) used one effective rate for every period. Now we replace that single repeated factor with a chain of explicitly dated factors. Every rate and payment below is invented. All intervals are complete, equal **one-year periods**, with one currency, retained growth, no fees or taxes and no unmentioned transfers.

An **effective period rate** includes the complete proportional change over that interval. A rate r₁ = 5% belongs between dates zero and one; r₂ = −10% belongs between one and two; r₃ = 20% belongs between two and three. These are interval inputs, not three alternative quotes for the entire three-year wait.

The **growth factor** for interval j is 1 + $r_j$. A negative rate above −100% has a positive factor below one: −10% means multiply by 0.90, not subtract 10 currency units from every possible balance. [OpenStax develops compounding by applying each period’s growth to the retained balance](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance-2e/pages/7-2-time-value-of-money-tvm-basics). We are extending that same operation to unequal rates.

Let $A_t$ mean the **cumulative growth factor from date zero to date t**. A₀ = 1 because no interval has elapsed. Multiplication, written with the product symbol Π, gives

$$
A_t=\prod_{j=1}^{t}(1+r_j),\qquad FV_t=PV_0 A_t.
$$

Thus A₁ = 1.05, A₂ = 1.05 × 0.90 = 0.945 and A₃ = 0.945 × 1.20 = 1.134. The total three-year change is 100(1.134 − 1) = 13.4 units, or **13.4%**, not the sum 5% − 10% + 20% = 15%. Each percentage used a different balance. The [MIT cash-account construction writes the same dated product](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-450-analytics-of-finance-fall-2010/219406be97b96bc540e0be6aa778c0b7_MIT15_450F10_lec01.pdf), with a factor for every successive period.

### When to use it

Use the product when a scenario or contract supplies a different effective rate for each interval. State the dates and units before multiplying. A monthly effective rate is a whole-month factor; an annual rate cannot silently occupy that same slot. Future scenario inputs also need not be available investment returns.

A payment travelling from date three back to date one crosses two intervals. Going on to date zero would be an extra stop. Use the conversion **between the payment’s own two dates**.

Define the **date-zero discount factor** $D_t$ as the value at zero of one unit paid at date t under this chain:

$$
D_t=\frac1{A_t},\qquad D_0=1.
$$

A payment 113.4 at date three has present value 113.4/1.134 = **100**. A payment 94.5 at date two has present value 94.5/0.945 = **100**. The second discount factor is 1/0.945≈**1.058201**: discount factors need not always be below one when interval rates can be negative.

### Convert from date three to date one

From one to three, only the second and third intervals remain. Their growth factor is 0.90 × 1.20 = 1.08, also A₃/A₁ = 1.134/1.05. The reverse discount factor is 1/1.08 = **0.925926**, so 113.4 at three is worth **105 at one**. Dividing by 1.134 instead would give a value at zero.

For any dates s and t in the same chain:

$$
G_{s\to t}=\frac{A_t}{A_s},\qquad D_{s,t}=\frac{A_s}{A_t}=\frac{D_t}{D_s}\quad(s<t).
$$

These factors link consistently: converting zero→one and one→three gives A₁ × (A₃/A₁) = A₃. Reversing a conversion gives its reciprocal. You do not need to invent a new rate for each calculation.

A **signed cash flow** is a receipt positive or an outlay negative from one chosen viewpoint. To value amount $C_t$ on common date τ, multiply by $A_\tau$/$A_t$. The symbol τ simply names the chosen meeting date; it is not another interest rate.

$$
V_\tau=\sum_{t=0}^{3}C_t\frac{A_\tau}{A_t}=A_\tau\sum_{t=0}^{3}\frac{C_t}{A_t}.
$$

The sum symbol Σ says to add the individually converted terms. At zero, this is the same [signed NPV procedure](/money-time-value/net-present-value-decisions) with a different cumulative factor for each date. [CFA Institute describes cash-flow additivity as a valuation foundation](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/time-value-money); matching the valuation date and assumptions is what lets the terms add.

One constant **equivalent rate** can compress the total growth of an initial sum over three equal years. Its growth factor cubed must equal the entire chain:

$$
(1+r_{\mathrm{eq}})^3=A_3,\qquad r_{\mathrm{eq}}=A_3^{1/3}-1.
$$

Here $r_{\mathrm{eq}}=1.134^{1/3}-1$≈**4.280799% per year**. It is the geometric average of the **growth factors**, minus one. The arithmetic average of rates is(5% − 10% + 20%)/3 = 5%, but 100 × 1.05³ = **115.7625**, rather than 113.4.

### Same final value, different intermediate conversions

Under $r_{\mathrm{eq}}$,100 ends at 113.4 after three years. Yet the first-year factor is 1.042807993, rather than the original 1.05. A 21 payment at year one therefore has value 21/1.042807993≈**20.137935**, versus 21/1.05 = **20** under the original chain.

The endpoint agreement does not make $r_{\mathrm{eq}}$ a replacement for every dated payment. For n **equal periods**, the formula is $A_n^{1/n}-1$ per such period. If intervals have different lengths, counting intervals does not establish an annual rate; the period units and total elapsed time need conversion first.

### Interval rate versus whole-horizon rate

Our third-year interval input is 20%. The constant annual equivalent for the whole three years is 4.280799%. One covers only the last year; the other compresses all three. A market **spot rate** is tied to a whole wait from the valuation date, whereas a **forward rate** concerns an interval starting later. [CFA Institute distinguishes these maturity and interval concepts](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/the-term-structure-of-interest-rates-spot-par-and-forward-curves). Our invented path is not a market curve, nor does it forecast future quoted rates.

### When to use it

Use the equivalent rate to summarize a no-transfer endpoint over its stated horizon. Keep the original factors when valuing intermediate cash flows. With contributions or withdrawals, growth of the original sum is not a money-weighted return on all the saver’s payments.

### When to use it

Use a dated account recurrence when external money moves during the path: end-of-year balance equals previous balance times that year’s factor, **then** the specified end-date transfer. A transfer before growth requires a different recurrence. Compare equal initial capital, the same dated flows and the same costs. Do not call an outside contribution investment profit or infer a return merely from the final balance.

A conversion chain is a calculator supplied with assumptions. It does not choose the correct opportunity-cost rates or lock tomorrow’s account terms. Contractual fixed future factors, market-implied discount factors, realized past returns and forecasts are different inputs, even when their algebra looks similar.

For a concrete boundary check, a zero rate in each interval gives A₀ = A₁ = A₂ = A₃ = 1. The schedule(−200,21,94.5,113.4) then has the same common-date sum at every date: **−200 + 21 + 94.5 + 113.4 = 28.9**. A single interval return of −100%, however, gives factor zero: positive initial money can be wiped out, and the reciprocal discount conversion is undefined. That return can occur in an investment; it lies outside this lesson’s **invertible positive-factor model**. Rates below −100% also lie outside it.

### When to use it

Use these calculations for a fully specified scenario and report its dates, units, transfers and rate interpretation. A future return forecast is not automatically a tradable discount curve, and a higher calculated value does not guarantee a payment. For different currencies, risks, charges or credit conditions, revisit the valuation assumptions before comparing sums.
