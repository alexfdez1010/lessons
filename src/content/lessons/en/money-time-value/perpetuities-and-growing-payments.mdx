A payment of €100 every year forever sounds as though its value must be infinite. Its undiscounted total is. Its value **today** can still approach a finite amount: every later receipt has further to travel back along the timeline.

Use what you learned about [cash-flow timelines](/money-time-value/cash-flow-timelines) and [annuities and payment timing](/money-time-value/annuities-and-payment-timing). We will extend a finite payment list indefinitely, keeping the payment dates explicit. All examples are invented mathematical models, not forecasts of actual permanent payments or growth. The examples use **nominal amounts**: euros measured at their future payment dates. **Real amounts** would instead hold purchasing power constant. Growth and discounting use the same price basis and annual frequency; stated rates are assumed annual effective rates.

## A level perpetuity is the limit of longer payment lists

A **perpetuity** is a model of payments continuing without an end date. A **level perpetuity** keeps every payment at the same positive amount $C$. Write $PV$ for present value. Let $r$ be the constant annual discount rate, written as a decimal, with $r>0$. If the first payment is one year from today:

$$PV_0=\frac{C}{1+r}+\frac{C}{(1+r)^2}+\frac{C}{(1+r)^3}+\cdots$$

The subscript zero says that every term is valued **today, at year zero**. The $N$-payment **partial sum** is the value of only the first $N$ receipts. From the finite annuity calculation:

$$S_N=\frac{C}{r}\left[1-(1+r)^{-N}\right]$$

As $N$ grows, $(1+r)^{-N}$ tends to zero because $1+r>1$. Therefore:

$$PV_0=\lim_{N\to\infty}S_N=\frac{C}{r}$$

Imagine a staircase whose successive steps get smaller. The height can approach a ceiling even as you add more steps. Here the steps represent **discounted payment values**, not shrinking cash payments: the actual level payment remains $C$.

### Worked example with €100 at ten percent

The first receipt is €100 at year one; every following year also pays €100. At $r=0.10$:

- Year one contributes $100/1.10\approx90.9091$ to today's value.
- Year two contributes $100/1.10^2\approx82.6446$.
- Year three contributes $100/1.10^3\approx75.1315$.

Those first three contributions sum to about **€248.6852**. Including more payments gives:

| Payments included | Present value at year zero | Value of payments still omitted |
|---|---:|---:|
| 3 | About €248.69 | About €751.31 |
| 10 | About €614.46 | About €385.54 |
| 30 | About €942.69 | About €57.31 |
| 50 | About €991.48 | About €8.52 |
| Infinite model | €1,000.00 | No later payments excluded |

The limiting model value is €100 ÷ 0.10 = **€1,000**. Fifty payments are close to that limit, but not equal to it.

### When to use it

Use the formula as a benchmark for a level perpetual stream or a check on a long annuity calculation. It does not establish that a real savings account can safely fund that payment forever. Actual returns, costs and payment obligations can change. [OpenStax introduces perpetuity valuation and its cash-flow assumptions](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance/pages/8-1-perpetuities).

The trap is to treat omitted payments as worthless. Every finite future payment here has positive present value; the total omitted value simply becomes smaller as more payments are included.

## Put the formula value one period before the first payment

A formula value needs a **valuation date**: the date to which the cash flows have been discounted. The ordinary $C/r$ result belongs one payment period before the first receipt. If the first payment occurs at year $k$, that formula gives a value at year $k-1$. Move it from there to today:

$$PV_0=\frac{C/r}{(1+r)^{k-1}}$$

The payments still occur annually; only their starting date changes. The amount $C$ means the first actual receipt, not an amount that has secretly been growing during the delay.

### Worked example with the first payment at year three

The schedule contains no payments at years one or two. It then pays €100 at year three, €100 at year four, and so on. With $r=10\%$:

1. Value the stream **at year two**: $PV_2=100/0.10=1{,}000$.
2. Discount that year-two value through two annual periods:

$$PV_0=\frac{1{,}000}{1.10^2}=\frac{1{,}000}{1.21}\approx826.4463$$

Check the beginning of the same series directly: the first payment contributes $100/1.10^3\approx75.1315$, the next contributes $100/1.10^4\approx68.3013$, and later terms follow the same pattern. Every term has moved two years later than in the earlier example.

| First payment year | Date of the €1,000 formula value | Present value today |
|---|---|---:|
| 1 | Year 0 | €1,000.00 |
| 2 | Year 1 | About €909.09 |
| 3 | Year 2 | About €826.45 |

### When to use it

Use this two-stage calculation whenever a perpetual stream starts after a waiting period. Label the first payment, place the formula value one period earlier, then move that value to the requested date. This applies the [same timeline mechanics used by NYU's time-value explanation](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/New_Home_Page/littlebook/pvmechanics.htm).

Discounting €1,000 for **three** years would move a year-two value one year too far. Dividing by no discount factor would incorrectly leave the year-two amount at today's date. If a separate payment is actually due today, add that dated payment separately rather than hiding it inside the ordinary perpetuity formula.

## Growing payments use the next payment in the numerator

A **growing perpetuity** changes its payments by the same proportional growth rate $g$ each period. Let $C_1$ be the first payment, arriving one year from today. The later receipts are $C_1(1+g)$, $C_1(1+g)^2$, and so on. For positive payments, assume $g>-1$, so the multiplier $1+g$ is positive. A negative $g$ means the payments decline; they can still remain positive.

With a constant annual discount rate $r$ greater than $g$:

$$PV_0=\frac{C_1}{1+r}+\frac{C_1(1+g)}{(1+r)^2}+\frac{C_1(1+g)^2}{(1+r)^3}+\cdots=\frac{C_1}{r-g}$$

The finite $N$-payment version keeps the part of that geometric series you actually include:

$$S_N=\frac{C_1}{r-g}\left[1-\left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^N\right]$$

We will examine the requirement $r>g$ in the next section. The finite cash flows can still be added directly when that infinite-stream requirement fails.

### Worked example with a first future payment of €100

Set $C_1=100$, $r=8\%=0.08$ and $g=3\%=0.03$. Actual payments grow, while their present values decline:

| Year | Payment | Calculation of year-zero value | Present value |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | €100.00 | 100 ÷ 1.08 | About €92.5926 |
| 2 | €103.00 | 103 ÷ 1.08² | About €88.3059 |
| 3 | €106.09 | 106.09 ÷ 1.08³ | About €84.2177 |

The first three present values total about **€265.1162**. For the infinite model:

$$PV_0=\frac{100}{0.08-0.03}=\frac{100}{0.05}=2{,}000$$

Sixty payments include about **€1,883.63** of that value; the omitted later payments contribute about **€116.37** under the same assumptions.

### C zero is not C one

Now change the data: **€100 is the payment just made at year zero**, written $C_0$, rather than next year's receipt. If future payments grow 3%:

$$C_1=C_0(1+g)=100\times1.03=103$$

The value of the future stream is therefore:

$$PV_0=\frac{103}{0.08-0.03}=2{,}060$$

Using 100 directly would give 2,000 and omit **€60** of model value. The €100 payment already made is not counted again in this valuation of future receipts. If the first actual receipt instead starts later than year one, date the formula value one period before that receipt and discount it to today, as before.

### When to use it

Use this model to analyse a hypothetical stream with stable payment growth. It is conditional on the payment estimate, the growth rate, the discount rate and the timing. Real payment policies and growth can change; a model value is not a guaranteed purchase price or realised return. [OpenStax's growing-dividend discussion states the next-payment convention and model limitations](https://openstax.org/books/principles-finance/pages/11-2-dividend-discount-models-ddms).

## Positive payments need discounting to outrun growth

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"The first future payment is positive, and annual growth equals the annual discount rate. What happens as you include more payments?"}

  {
    "text": "The infinite sum becomes exactly zero",
    "correct": false

  {
    "text": "The formula gives a finite negative value",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"Equal growth and discount factors cancel between successive terms. Each discounted receipt has the same positive value, so the total has no finite limit."}
  pretest
/>

To test convergence, compare **successive discounted payments**. Their ratio is:

$$q=\frac{1+g}{1+r}$$

Under our positive-payment assumptions, this ratio is positive. The infinite sum converges exactly when $q<1$, which is equivalent to **$r>g$**. When $r=g$, $q=1$ and the discounted terms stay constant. When $r<g$, $q>1$ and the discounted terms grow.

### Worked examples at the boundary

Take $C_1=100$:

| Discount rate | Payment growth | What each successive discounted term does | Infinite-stream result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8% | 3% | Multiplied by 1.03 ÷ 1.08 ≈ 0.953704 | Finite value 2,000 |
| 8% | 8% | Multiplied by 1; every term is 100 ÷ 1.08 ≈ 92.5926 | No finite limit |
| 5% | 8% | Multiplied by 1.08 ÷ 1.05 ≈ 1.028571 | No finite limit |
| 0% | 0% | Every term remains 100 | No finite limit |
| 0% | −5% | Multiplied by 0.95; positive terms decline | Finite value 2,000 |

At equal 8% rates, ten payments have finite present value $10\times100/1.08\approx925.9259$. Twenty have about 1,851.8519. Adding more keeps increasing the total; the formula's zero denominator is not a hidden finite answer.

At 5% discount and 8% growth, the first three discounted receipts are about **95.2381**, **97.9592** and **100.7580**. They increase. Substituting into $100/(0.05-0.08)$ would produce a negative number, which cannot equal a sum of positive discounted receipts. The infinite formula is being used outside its valid condition.

At zero discount and zero growth, $N$ payments simply total $100N$. This is the valid zero-rate finite-annuity case from the preceding lesson; sending $N$ to infinity does not create a finite perpetuity value. Zero discount alone does not rule out convergence if payments decline: with $g=-0.05$, the value is $100/(0-(-0.05))=2{,}000$. The condition remains $r>g$.

### A small rate gap makes a sensitive value

Even when the condition is met, the answer can depend heavily on a small input change. Keeping $C_1=100$ and $r=8\%$:

| Growth assumption | Rate gap | Model present value |
|---|---|---:|
| 3% | 5 percentage points | 100 ÷ 0.05 = 2,000 |
| 4% | 4 percentage points | 100 ÷ 0.04 = 2,500 |
| 7.5% | 0.5 percentage points | 100 ÷ 0.005 = 20,000 |

The last result is a consequence of the assumed tiny gap, not evidence that a real payment stream is worth 20,000. Check plausible alternative inputs and the cash-flow horizon. A finite or changing-growth model may answer a real problem better than a perpetual constant-growth assumption.

### When to use the convergence test

Use it **before** dividing by $r-g$. Keep annual growth with annual discounting, match the currency and nominal-or-real basis, and identify the first receipt. If $r\leq g$, keep calculating the finite schedule that is actually specified or change the model assumptions; do not interpret a negative formula value as the value of positive perpetual receipts.
