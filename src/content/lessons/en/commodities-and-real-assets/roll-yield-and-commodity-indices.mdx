A futures contract expires. Maintaining exposure beyond its life requires a replacement contract, with its own entry price and delivery terms. That replacement can make a futures strategy behave differently from the cash commodity. The important accounting distinction is that **the gap between two delivery prices is not an immediate cash profit or loss**.

## Why you have to roll

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Why must a passive long replace a futures contract to keep exposure beyond its expiry?" options={[{text:'The old contract ends; a later-dated contract represents a different delivery obligation',correct:true},{text:'Every future loses a fixed amount each day, like option time decay'},{text:'Holding a future automatically renews it at the original price'}]} explanation="Rolling closes a held contract and opens another. It changes the contract used for exposure, rather than turning the old contract into a perpetual instrument." />

**Rolling** a long position means selling the held contract to close it and buying another maturity. A **front month** is a nearby delivery contract; the **replacement month** is the later contract chosen by the strategy. Schedules differ, and cash-settled contracts also expire. Delivery or settlement rules determine the deadline for the particular contract.

Suppose an invented oil future represents 1000 barrels. You entered the held June long at 100 and close it at 103, then open September at 106. Closing June produces $1000(103-100)=3000$ of cumulative trading profit. Opening September at its initial mark gives zero immediate marked-to-market profit on that new contract, before execution costs. You do **not** pay $1000(106-103)=3000$ as a separate automatic roll loss. Posted margin, variation settlement and fees have their own cash flows.

### When to use it

Roll when the desired exposure outlasts the held contract and the strategy chooses to retain it. Specify which maturity, quantity, execution prices, costs and settlement deadlines apply. A roll order can execute two trades together; it does not abolish bid-ask costs or funding risk.

## Roll yield describes a return effect under stated assumptions

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Front is 100 and next is 97. Does rolling guarantee a 3/97 return immediately?" options={[{text:'No: that ratio is a curve-gap indicator; a realised return needs subsequent price changes and a capital base',correct:true},{text:'Yes: selling a dear contract and buying a cheaper one always credits that percentage'},{text:'Yes: the next contract must immediately jump to 100'}]} explanation="A cross-sectional gap is not itself a future’s entry-to-exit return. A convergence example can produce that number only after adding a price-path assumption." />

**Roll yield** is used for a futures strategy's return effect relative to a specified price benchmark as contracts change over time. Its exact attribution depends on the index methodology, benchmark and denominator. Contango and backwardation are entry-curve descriptions, not guarantees of later prices.

A frequently used diagnostic is the **normalised curve gap**:

$$
I=\frac{F_{\text{front}}-F_{\text{next}}}{F_{\text{next}}}.
$$

It is negative when the next contract is dearer and positive when it is cheaper. It is not an exact realised return merely from executing a roll.

**Show the missing time assumption.** For an illustrative unlevered notional return, suppose one replacement future is held unchanged in quantity and later converges to the stated unchanged spot level. Ignore costs and collateral interest:

The gains or losses occur **while the replacement quote moves**, not as an extra gap payment at entry. The fixed exit is a scenario assumption: spot can move and the contract can follow another path. If the new future instead exits at its unchanged entry quote, its profit is zero in either curve shape. Profit divided by posted margin would give a different percentage and conceal additional funding needs.

### When to use it

Use the curve as a conditional carry diagnostic, then state the price path or return-attribution method. Do not annualise a single gap without a holding horizon, repeated-contract assumptions and a treatment of changing capital. For a simultaneous long/short position, calculate both legs as taught in [Calendar Spreads](/commodities-and-real-assets/seasonal-calendars-and-calendar-spreads).

## Total return needs a consistent attribution

A collateralised futures strategy earns marked-to-market futures results plus the return on its collateral, less its costs. “Spot plus roll plus collateral” is a common attribution shorthand, not permission to add unrelated multi-period returns or call a maturity gap cash income.

In particular, **spot** can mean different things. A physical cash quote is not necessarily the same series as a commodity index's named “Spot” version. [S&P describes its GSCI Spot series as reflecting constituent futures price levels](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/indices/commodities/sp-gsci/); its excess-return and total-return versions also incorporate contract rolling and collateral as defined by the methodology. Check which series a factsheet reports.

### When to use it

Read return version, rebalance rules, cash remuneration, costs and attribution convention before comparing a commodity product with a price chart. A fully collateralised hypothetical index need not equal a leveraged account's return on margin. Index return also differs from the investor's fund return after fees, tracking differences and tax.

## Commodity indices: GSCI vs BCOM

So how do real products package all this? Through **commodity indices** — rules-based baskets of rolled, collateralized futures. The two benchmarks everyone quotes are the **S&P GSCI** and the **Bloomberg Commodity Index (BCOM)**, and the difference between them is entirely about *weighting philosophy*.

Picture two grocery baskets. The **GSCI** fills its basket in proportion to how much of each commodity the *world produces* — and since the planet produces a staggering amount of oil and gas by dollar value, the GSCI basket is mostly energy. It's a faithful mirror of the global production economy, which also means it lives or dies on crude. The **BCOM** instead writes a rule: *no single commodity or sector may exceed a cap*, so it deliberately tops up metals and agriculture to keep any one bet from swamping the basket. It's the "balanced diet" index.

**Definitions.**
- **S&P GSCI** — world-production-weighted; energy (especially crude) dominates. Maximal commodity-beta, minimal diversification, very sensitive to the oil curve.
- **Bloomberg Commodity Index (BCOM)** — capped weights per commodity and sector, forcing balance across energy, industrial metals, precious metals, grains, softs and livestock.

What they **share** matters just as much: both are **long-only**, both are **front-loaded** (they hold near-dated contracts), their total-return variants assume collateral backing, and both **roll on a fixed published schedule**. That last point is the kicker — because both roll mechanically on a calendar, both remain exposed to the conditional carry effects associated with their chosen futures maturities. The entry slope alone is not a realised gain or loss. Their diversification differs; their exposure to the roll wedge does not disappear.

This is exactly why **"enhanced"** or **second-generation** indices exist. Instead of always rolling into the next listed contract, they try to roll into whichever part of the curve is *cheapest to hold* — picking a contract further out where the curve is flatter, or optimizing the roll date — specifically to reduce the contango drag you now understand cold. They change the maturities and risks held; reducing a modelled carry headwind does not guarantee a better realised return.

<Callout variant="warning">
**Misconception: "BCOM is diversified, so it dodges the roll problem."** Diversification spreads your bet across *commodities*; it does nothing about *curve shape*. A diversified basket still holds dated futures with price-path, collateral and execution risks. Diversification does not establish a realised roll contribution.
</Callout>

### When to use it

Compare published weighting, maturity, rolling and return-version rules against the exposure being analysed. A production-weighted basket and a capped basket have different concentrations; neither guarantees inflation protection, stable returns or a favourable realised roll contribution.

## Reconcile the return before interpreting the curve

<Callout variant="info" title="Keep the accounting separate">
Closing the old contract realises its own price change. The new contract starts a new entry reference; its different delivery quote is not a separate cash payment equal to the gap. A conditional convergence scenario or a specified index methodology can attribute roll effects. It cannot turn a single observed slope into guaranteed realised profit.
</Callout>
