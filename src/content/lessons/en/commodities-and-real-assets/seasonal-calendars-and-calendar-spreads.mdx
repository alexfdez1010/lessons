Summer gas is quoted at 3 and winter gas at 4. “Winter costs more” is already on the screen. It is not a trading profit waiting to be collected. A position in both delivery months earns or loses money as their prices **change relative to one another**.

You already know contract specifications, storage, seasonal demand and rolling. Here we connect them to a two-leg position. All prices and funding figures below are invented USD examples. Each synthetic contract represents **1000 identical commodity units**, not the specification of a named exchange contract. Both legs use the same currency, multiplier and contract count; we close before either delivery process begins.

## Choose a sign before choosing a position

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Define the spread as winter minus summer. Which position profits when that spread increases, with equal quantities on both legs?" options={[{text:'Long winter and short summer',correct:true},{text:'Long summer and short winter'},{text:'Long both, regardless of their relative changes'}]} explanation="Long the first named term in the subtraction and short the second. A different quoted spread convention would reverse the labels, so write the legs explicitly." />

A **calendar spread** combines a long futures position in one delivery month with a short position in another month of the same underlying contract type. A **leg** is either constituent position. This is a comparison between two dated obligations, like comparing two train departures with different ticket prices, rather than buying a single timeless barrel.

We fix the convention:

$$
S_t=F_{\text{winter},t}-F_{\text{summer},t}.
$$

Both quotes are observed at the same trading time $t$. The delivery months are different; the observation dates must not be mixed. In this lesson, a **long spread** means long winter and short summer. Exchanges and platforms can name spread orders differently. For example, [NZX's calendar-spread guide](https://assets.ctfassets.net/m5mydry9e35f/33098bf7rdHzRs9qdt9VEP/e28fccaefa0990f28783cea4e9e80709/NZX29738_NZX_Guide_to_Calendar_Spreads_-_March_2026_v3.pdf) defines its order direction using the near month. Read the convention rather than relying on the word “buy”.

For $n$ contracts per leg and multiplier $Q$ units per contract, our long-spread profit before costs is:

$$
\Pi=nQ\big[(F_{w,1}-F_{w,0})-(F_{s,1}-F_{s,0})\big]=nQ(S_1-S_0).
$$

The short spread has the opposite profit. This compact identity requires matching quantities and units. For different multipliers or unequal legs, calculate each leg separately; subtracting raw quotes is not enough.

### When to use it

Use a calendar spread to express or hedge a relative view across delivery months, rather than a pure view that all commodity prices will rise. [CME's spread overview](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/courses/understanding-futures-spreads/futures-spread-overview) distinguishes calendar spreads from intercommodity and processing spreads. A wheat-versus-corn position or a refinery crack spread has additional underlying-product risks.

## Seasonal demand is already part of the entry curve

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="Both futures prices rise after you buy winter and sell summer. Demand, inventories, transport and production can make particular delivery months more valuable. In US natural gas, storage commonly bridges lower-demand injection months and winter withdrawals. [CME's gas-storage lesson](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/courses/introduction-to-natural-gas/understanding-natural-gas-risk-management-spreads-storage) shows how these seasonal differences already enter futures quotes. Weather and stock levels can change the relationship; a calendar pattern is not a guaranteed price path.

Winter is still more expensive. It even rose 15%. The spread nevertheless lost because summer rose more **in quote units**. For these equal units, percentage changes do not replace the monetary calculation. Conversely, both prices can fall while the long spread gains if summer falls farther in quote units.

A parallel increase of 0.5 in each quote leaves the spread unchanged: $4.5-3.5=1$, so +500 on winter and −500 on summer cancel before costs. That illustrates one offset, not immunity to all market moves. Different months can react differently to the same shock.

### When to use it

Compare a weather, inventory or supply scenario with what the curve already prices. Scenario analysis can reveal sensitivities, not establish a profitable signal or a probability. Historical seasonal averages need date alignment, consistent contracts and an account of selection, costs and changing market structure.

## Physical storage adds local basis and delivery constraints

A futures spread refers to the specified benchmark, grade and delivery dates. A physical operator may buy at a different location, sell a different grade or have limited storage capacity. **Basis** here means local cash price minus the relevant benchmark quote. It can differ between months.

Imagine benchmark summer 3 and winter 4. Local summer costs $3+0.1=3.1$, while the local winter sale price is $4-0.2=3.8$. The local seasonal price difference is $3.8-3.1=0.7$, not the benchmark spread 1:

$$
\text{local difference}=S+(b_w-b_s)=1+(-0.2-0.1)=0.7.
$$

For 1000 units, the local-versus-benchmark difference is $1000(-0.3)=-300$. If storage, financing and handling together cost 0.8 per unit, physical gross price receipts less purchase and those costs give $1000(3.8-3.1-0.8)=-100$. Buying at 3.1 and selling at 3.8 does not by itself prove a profitable storage operation.

This comparison does not model every physical contingency. Injection and withdrawal limits, shrinkage, transport bottlenecks, credit terms and ability to make or take delivery can matter. [ICE's storage-market explanation](https://www.ice.com/white-paper/natural-gas-market-storage-dynamics-and-alpha-generation) describes such physical and collateral constraints. Its historical market examples are not current forecasts or universal seasonal rules.

### When to use it

Use the calendar spread as one input to a physical-margin calculation. Match grade, place, quantity, dates and settlement method; then add costs and constraints. If one leg expires first, leaving the other open changes the position. An exchange-listed spread order can reduce the risk of executing only one leg, but does not erase liquidity, fill-price or subsequent delivery risk.

## A final profit does not fund an earlier margin call

<MCQ client:visible pretest question="A scenario ends with a spread profit of 300 but first produces a 600 loss. Can the final profit guarantee you can keep the position open?" options={[{text:'No: daily settlement can demand cash before the recovery',correct:true},{text:'Yes: all losses wait until the profitable final day'},{text:'Yes: exchange margin is the maximum possible loss'}]} explanation="Cash timing is separate from terminal profit. A position can be closed before the recovery if required funds are unavailable." />

**Initial margin** is collateral required to open or retain the futures position, not its purchase price or maximum loss. **Variation settlement** transfers marked-to-market gains and losses. A **maintenance requirement** can trigger additional funding or liquidation. [CME explains that requirements can change and brokers can require more funds](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/courses/introduction-to-futures/margin-know-what-is-needed). No margin amounts below describe a current exchange policy.

Use one long-winter/short-summer pair of 1000 units and this invented sequence. Both contracts remain tradable and neither enters delivery during the example:

Stipulate initial margin 1000, maintenance 800 and restoration to 1000 after a breach. The interim loss leaves $1000-600=400$. The additional cash needed is $1000-400=600$. If supplied, the later price change earns $1000(1.3-0.4)=900$, leaving account equity $1000+900=1900$. Total contributions were $1000+600=1600$, so net profit is $1900-1600=300$.

If the trader cannot fund the call and closes at the interim marks, equity is 400 and the realised loss is 600. The later hypothetical recovery belongs to a position no longer held. Top-ups are **cash contributions**, not additional trading losses; subtracting them twice would misstate profit.

Spread margin may receive offsets for a qualifying paired position. Lower margin is neither a loss cap nor a promise that the offset or requirement remains unchanged. If settlement receipts on one leg arrive later than payments on the other, gross cash needs can exceed the netted same-time illustration. Liquidity and forced-close prices can make matters worse.

### When to use it

Stress both the terminal spread and the path of cash requirements. Distinguish contract notional, posted margin, available liquid resources and profit. A roll that closes a held month and opens another is a change of exposure: the opening price gap is not an immediate fee or gain equal to that gap. Subsequent marked-to-market changes and execution costs determine money earned or lost.
