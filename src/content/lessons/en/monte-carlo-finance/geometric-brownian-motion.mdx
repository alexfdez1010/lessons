A model with 10 % price drift and 20 % volatility has an expected one-year price of **110.517092** from 100. Its median is **108.328707**. Neither figure is a promise about one future path, and neither says that 10 % is the expected log return. One label on a simulator can hide three different quantities.

**Geometric Brownian motion**, GBM, is a tractable continuous-time model for a positive price. It is useful because we can derive its law and simulate its values exactly on chosen dates. That gives us a benchmark against which to check simulation code. Whether it describes an actual market, and which probability law the task needs, are separate modeling decisions.

All examples below are invented. Price units are USD, time is in **years**, coefficients are constant within a scenario, and there are no costs or corporate actions. We begin with no dividends; the pricing-law section introduces a specified continuous dividend rule.

## 1. Give price drift and log drift different names

Let S_t be price and W_t a **standard Brownian motion**: over a time interval Δt, its increment is normal with mean zero and variance Δt; increments on disjoint intervals are independent. Thus ΔW = √Δt Z with a fresh independent standard normal Z for each non-overlapping step. Brownian motion has continuous paths; it is not a sequence of independent price levels.

The GBM stochastic differential equation is

$$
\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.
$$

Here μ is the **instantaneous price-return drift**, per year, under the chosen probability law. Sigma σ is the **diffusion coefficient**, in inverse square-root years: log-return standard deviation over Δt is σ√Δt. A quoted annualized σ of 20 % does not say the finite one-year simple return has exactly 20 % standard deviation.

Apply the [Itô rule taught in the prerequisite course](/stochastic-processes/ito-and-sdes) to f(S) = log S. Its derivatives are f′ = 1/S and f″ = −1/S², so the log drift is

$$
m=f'(S)\mu S+\tfrac12 f''(S)\sigma^2S^2
=\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2.
$$

Consequently the **exact transition** for this constant-coefficient model is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell&=m\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z,\\
S_{t+\Delta t}&=S_t e^{\ell},\qquad
R=e^{\ell}-1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The symbol ℓ is a **log return**; R is the corresponding **simple price return**. A log return 0.10 gives simple return e^0.10 − 1 ≈ 10.517092 %, not exactly 10 %. [Columbia’s Brownian simulation notes construct independent normal increments and multiplicative lognormal ratios](https://www.columbia.edu/~ks20/4404-Sigman/4404-Notes-sim-BM.pdf); their drift notation for the log process corresponds to our m.

### Work one adopted trading-day step

Take S = 100, μ = 0.10, σ = 0.20 and Δt = 1/252. This is an adopted model convention of 252 equal trading intervals per year, not a calendar law. With Z =  + 1:

- Annual log drift m = 0.10 − 0.20²/2 = **0.08**.
- Deterministic log component = 0.08/252 = **0.000317460**.
- Diffusion component = 0.20√(1/252) × 1 = **0.012598816**.
- Log return ℓ = **0.012916276**; price = 100 exp(ℓ) = **101.300005**.

For Z = −1, the log return is 0.000317460 − 0.012598816 = −0.012281355 and price is **98.779375**. Exponentiation, rather than a sign restriction on Z, keeps the mathematical price positive.

### When to use it

Use the exact transition when the constant-coefficient GBM law is the intended model. Match drift, volatility and time units before drawing shocks. Additive normal models can be useful for other quantities, but an unrestricted normal dollar shock can make a limited-liability stock price negative; GBM avoids that state by construction. Its lognormal shape is an assumption to assess, not a universal description of stock returns.

## 2. Compute the mean, median and mode of the model law

The arithmetic mean, the middle quantile and the density peak answer different questions. For positive σ and T, horizon log return is normal with mean mT and variance σ²T. Substituting those into the [lognormal moment formulas](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda3669.htm) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[S_T]&=S_0e^{\mu T},\\
\operatorname{median}(S_T)&=S_0e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T},\\
\operatorname{mode}(S_T)&=S_0e^{(\mu-3\sigma^2/2)T},\\
\operatorname{Var}(S_T)&=S_0^2e^{2\mu T}(e^{\sigma^2T}-1).
\end{aligned}
$$

The **median** has half the model probability below it and half above. The **mode** is the highest point of the continuous density; it is not an outcome with positive point probability. The **mean** averages values weighted by their probabilities. It matters for an expected payoff, while the median describes a quantile. Neither is automatically the right personal planning target.

The expected-minus-median one-year simple-return gap here is about **2.188385 percentage points**, distinct from the **2-point** instantaneous level/log drift gap.

The normal exponential moment explains the correction exactly:

$$
E[e^{mT+\sigma\sqrt T Z}]
=e^{mT}e^{\sigma^2T/2}=e^{\mu T}.
$$

The gap between the **instantaneous level drift μ** and **expected log-growth rate m** is exactly σ²/2 in this GBM model. That does not make half the variance an exact arithmetic-versus-geometric gap for every finite sequence of simple returns. A + 50 %/−50 % pair, for example, requires its own product; the [return-measurement lesson](/kelly-and-cagr/arithmetic-vs-geometric-mean) distinguishes those definitions.

### When to use it

At fixed μ in this model, increasing σ leaves the analytical mean unchanged and lowers the median; the variance increases. At σ = 0, all three price summaries become S₀e^(μT). At T = 0 they equal S₀. Positive-σ GBM never reaches zero in finite time, although it can come arbitrarily close; there is no atom of default loss at zero. A default model needs different structure.

A curve joining the median at successive dates is a **pointwise median curve**, not a selected “most likely path”. It helps summarize marginal laws without pretending one smooth line is the future.

## 3. Compare the exact transition with Euler on the same shocks

An **Euler–Maruyama step** approximates the SDE directly:

$$
\widetilde S_{t+\Delta t}
=\widetilde S_t(1+\mu\Delta t+\sigma\Delta W).
$$

It differs from the exact exponential transition. [Oxford’s SDE simulation notes separate Euler approximation, pathwise error and expected-payoff error](https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/gilesm/infomm/lec3-2x2.pdf). Comparing methods should use the **same Brownian increments**; otherwise a different draw can masquerade as a scheme error.

### One full-year step can lose positivity

For S₀ = 100, μ = 0.10, σ = 0.20, T = 1 and Z = 1:

- Exact price = 100 exp(0.08 + 0.20) = **132.312981**.
- Euler value = 100(1 + 0.10 + 0.20) = **130**.

For the specified shock Z = −6, exact price is 100 exp(0.08 − 1.20) = **32.627979**. Euler gives 100(1 + 0.10 − 1.20) = **−10**. That negative value is a numerical approximation’s failure to preserve this model’s state space, not a negative stock price under GBM. Silently clipping it to zero changes the scheme and its distribution again.

### Couple two half-steps to one full step

Use half-step standardized shocks + 1 and −1. Each Brownian increment is ±√0.5, so their sum is zero. The exact terminal price is 100 exp(0.08 × 1 + 0.20 × 0) = **108.328707**. Two Euler factors are 1.05 ± 0.20√0.5:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde S_1
&=100[(1.05+0.20\sqrt{0.5})
(1.05-0.20\sqrt{0.5})]\\
&=100(1.1025-0.02)=108.25.
\end{aligned}
$$

The corresponding one-step Euler value with the same total Brownian increment is **110**. Here splitting improves the terminal approximation, but an individual path’s error need not shrink monotonically under every refinement. Convergence statements concern a specified error criterion and assumptions.

### When to use it

For constant-coefficient GBM, the exponential method samples the correct joint law **at the chosen grid dates** without Euler time-step bias. A terminal-only payoff can use one exact terminal draw. A continuously monitored barrier or time integral needs information between dates: exact sampled endpoints alone do not settle whether a crossing occurred in between. Monitoring approximations, bridge techniques and contract observation dates need their own treatment.

A path is one realization of a model. A fan is a finite sample of them. An analytical distribution assigns probabilities to all possible outcomes. Drawing eight lines does not turn the first object into the third.

### A terminal realization is not the conditional mean

Our one-year μ = 10 %, σ = 20 % model has price 132.312981 for standardized terminal shock + 1 and 88.692044 for shock −1. Those are two stipulated scenarios. Averaging just those two prices gives **110.502513**, already different from the analytical mean **110.517092**; two symmetric normal locations are not an exact integration of the exponential payoff.

### When to use it

Use simulations to estimate a target quantity under the chosen law, and attach an error estimate appropriate to that estimator. More independent paths can reduce sampling error; the [convergence lesson](/monte-carlo-finance/convergence-and-variance-reduction) examines the required moment and estimator conditions. GBM’s finite moments make many such calculations tractable, but a small realized sample need not have its sample mean above its sample median just because the population does.

A finely sampled path can help with a grid-dependent payoff, but more dates on the same path do not become more independent paths. More simulated paths also do not verify the drift estimate, add missing jumps or turn model probabilities into observed market frequencies.

## 5. Choose the probability law and handle dividends explicitly

Use **P** for a stipulated physical law describing real-world uncertainty and **Q** for a pricing law in the specified no-arbitrage model. They can assign different probabilities and drifts to the same future paths. Q is not a claim that investors are personally indifferent to risk.

Now let S be **ex-dividend price**: dividends are separate cash payments, not part of this quoted price change. Assume constant continuous financing rate r and proportional cash-dividend yield q, with payment qS_t dt per share. Under the stated frictionless stock/cash replication assumptions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dS_t}{S_t}&=\mu_Pdt+\sigma dW_t^P,\\
\frac{dS_t}{S_t}&=(r-q)dt+\sigma dW_t^Q.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the Q log drift is r − q − σ²/2. Using r alone for ex-dividend price is the **q = 0 case**. The [SOA educational note specifies continuous proportional dividend cash and reinvested share growth](https://www.soa.org/globalassets/assets/files/edu/MFE-27-07.pdf); [CFA’s contingent-claim discussion places pricing inside its replication assumptions](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/valuation-contingent-claims).

### Reconcile the terminal price with dividend value

Take S₀ = 100, μ_P = 8 %, σ = 20 %, r = 3 %, q = 2 % and T = 1. Expected terminal ex-dividend price under P is 100 e^0.08 = **108.328707**. Under Q it is 100 e^0.01 = **101.005017**. Discounting the latter at r gives

$$
e^{-rT}E_Q[S_T]=100e^{-qT}=98.019867.
$$

That is below 100 because the terminal share alone excludes the earlier dividend cash. The Q expected present value of the continuous cash dividends is 100(1 − e^−0.02) = **1.980133**. Together **98.019867 + 1.980133 = 100**.

Alternatively reinvest every dividend into shares: the share count becomes e^(qT), and under Q the expected discounted terminal **total-return wealth** is 100. Under our different P drift it is 100 e^((0.08 + 0.02 − 0.03) × 1) = **107.250818**, not an arbitrage-free pricing identity.

State whether the task is a physical scenario, a Q pricing expectation or a total-return account. Specify funding, dividends, discounting and market assumptions before replacing a drift. If σ = 0, changing probabilities cannot change a deterministic path: incompatible physical total-return and financing rates do not become an arbitrage-free common model by relabeling P as Q. The visual explains that boundary instead of silently treating two incompatible deterministic laws as an equivalent measure change.

## 6. Estimate parameters without promising that a year reveals the drift

<MCQ
  client:visible
  question={"Under ideal constant Gaussian log increments with known sigma, does daily rather than monthly sampling within the same one-year horizon reduce the drift estimator’s sigma/sqrt(T) standard error?"}
  options={[
  {
    "text": "No; the fixed observation horizon still supplies one year of drift information",
    "correct": true
  },
  {
    "text": "Yes; dividing the year into twelve times more dates creates twelve years",
    "correct": false
  },
  {
    "text": "Yes; drift uncertainty vanishes whenever the path is observed daily",
    "correct": false
  }
]}
  explanation={"The ideal log-drift estimate is the endpoint log change divided by the total observed time. Its standard error depends on that horizon, not just the count of subdivisions."}

Model coefficients are inputs; fitted coefficients are uncertain estimates. For n equally spaced observed log returns ℓ_i at interval Δ, the Gaussian constant-parameter **maximum-likelihood estimates**, MLEs, are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat m&=\frac{\bar\ell}{\Delta},\\
\widehat\sigma^2&=\frac{1}{n\Delta}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\ell_i-\bar\ell)^2,\\
\widehat\mu&=\widehat m+\tfrac12\widehat\sigma^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The bar denotes the sample mean. The variance MLE uses divisor **n**, rather than the n − 1 used for the usual unbiased variance estimate under these IID assumptions. [MIT’s GBM lecture discusses this level/log parameter distinction and estimation frequency](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-642-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2024/1M4kbTRRqekKhDctRz40f6vyYXc-X6GX1_transcript.pdf).

### Fit four invented quarterly log returns

Use −0.04, 0.02, 0.08 and 0.10, at Δ = 0.25 years. Their mean is 0.16/4 = **0.04**. Deviations from that mean are −0.08, −0.02, 0.04 and 0.06; squared deviations sum to 0.0064 + 0.0004 + 0.0016 + 0.0036 = **0.012**.

Therefore m̂ = 0.04/0.25 = **0.16 per year**, σ̂² = 0.012/(4 × 0.25) = **0.012 per year**, σ̂ = **0.109544512 per square-root year** and μ̂ = 0.16 + 0.012/2 = **0.166 per year**.

A simulator expecting level drift μ should receive 0.166 for these fitted inputs. Feeding it the already-corrected log drift 0.16 and subtracting half the variance again would produce log drift 0.154: a second correction that the data did not request.

### Distinguish sampling frequency from observation horizon

With known σ in the ideal model, the log-drift estimator over observation horizon T_obs satisfies

$$
\widehat m=\frac{\log(S_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}}/S_0)}{T_{\mathrm{obs}}},
\qquad \operatorname{SE}(\widehat m)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{obs}}}}.
$$

This follows from log-return variance σ²T_obs; [Duke’s Brownian-with-drift notes derive the endpoint estimator and its σ²/T variance](https://www2.stat.duke.edu/courses/Spring12/sta357/lec/brmot.pdf). With known σ = 20 %, one observed year gives a drift standard error of **20 percentage points per year**. Twenty-five observed years give **4 points**, if the same constant-parameter law remains appropriate throughout. Daily rather than monthly observation within the same year does not shrink this ideal drift error; more independent simulated paths do not create more years of market evidence.

### When to use it

Fit a clearly specified series: split-adjusted price changes, ex-dividend prices and reinvested total returns are not interchangeable inputs. Check spacing, units, parameter stability and estimation error. The known-σ calculation above is a conditional information benchmark, not a confidence interval for an actual market forecast; estimated volatility and structural uncertainty add further questions.

Constant-σ GBM has continuous paths and independent Gaussian log increments. It does not generate jumps, changing volatility regimes or default-to-zero states by itself. A larger simulation only explores those same assumptions more precisely. Use sensitivity scenarios, diagnostic evidence and a model that represents the exposure when those omissions matter; no fixed number of observations guarantees the drift has become clear.
