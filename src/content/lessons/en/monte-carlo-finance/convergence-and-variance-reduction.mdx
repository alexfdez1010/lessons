Two simulation runs report 4.7321 and 4.7188. Their difference is evidence of sampling variability, not an interval guaranteed to contain the answer. To judge precision, identify the estimator, its independent sampling units and its cost. Then ask what it is converging to.

We start with a **mean of independent, identically distributed outputs** from one fixed model. Later, mirrored pairs, weighted samples and randomized low-discrepancy designs change the relevant sampling units. A path may contain dependent time steps; the assumption here concerns independent **whole-path outputs**, not independence of every date within a path.

## Standard error measures sampling noise in a specified mean

Let $Y_i$ be a discounted payoff or other per-path output, with mean $\mu$, finite variance $\sigma_Y^2$, and independent identical draws. The estimator and its sampling variance are

$$\hat\mu_M=\frac1M\sum_{i=1}^M Y_i,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_M)=\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{M}$$

When $\sigma_Y$ is unknown, estimate the **standard error (SE)** using the sample standard deviation of the same outputs:

$$s_Y^2=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_i(Y_i-\hat\mu_M)^2,\qquad\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}=\frac{s_Y}{\sqrt M}$$

Standard deviation describes individual outputs; standard error describes their **mean estimator**. If $s_Y=100$ outcome units and $M=10{,}000$, estimated SE is $100/100=1$. To reach half that SE at the same variance requires four times the count:

| Independent outputs | Estimated SE at unchanged per-output SD 100 |
|---:|---:|
| 10,000 | 1 |
| 40,000 | 0.5 |
| 160,000 | 0.25 |
| 1,000,000 | 0.1 |

This is a scale law for **sampling standard deviation**, not a promise that each new run’s realized absolute error decreases monotonically. A tenfold smaller SE requires roughly one hundred times the evaluations if cost per output and variance stay fixed.

### When to use it

Use this formula for the relevant independent mean outputs. With dependent draws, covariance terms enter the variance of the mean; counting correlated values as independent exaggerates information. With infinite output variance, the usual finite-variance SE and normal central-limit argument do not apply. The IID law of large numbers can require only a finite absolute mean, so consistency and the root-count SE claim are separate conditions. [Statlect derives the mean-estimation setup and its asymptotic error conditions](https://www.statlect.com/asymptotic-theory/Monte-Carlo-method).

## A confidence interval is conditional on the estimator and model

For an adequately large independent sample with an appropriate finite-variance central limit approximation, an approximate 95% interval for the model mean is

$$\hat\mu_M\pm1.96\frac{s_Y}{\sqrt M}$$

A simulated mean of EUR 12 million with SE EUR 0.5 million gives

$$12\pm1.96(0.5)=12\pm0.98$$

or **[EUR 11.02 million, EUR 12.98 million]**. In repeated uses of a well-calibrated procedure, approximately 95% of such intervals cover the fixed model mean. This is not a guarantee for a particular interval or a 95% range of individual future payoffs. A Student-t interval is exact for normal independent observations; otherwise its small-sample use also needs justification.

### A zero estimated SE can be misleading

Suppose a simulated event is rare and no event appears in 1,000 independent paths. The Bernoulli observations are all zero, so the sample SD and the plug-in normal interval are zero. That does not prove the event probability is zero. If its model probability were 0.001, the chance of no events would be

$$(1-0.001)^{1000}\approx0.367695$$

about **36.77%**. The example diagnoses a poor normal approximation for this rare-event sample, not the probability of a real financial loss.

| Quantity estimated | Is payoff SD divided by root count its automatic SE? |
|---|---|
| Mean of IID finite-variance path outputs | Yes, as a sample estimate under those assumptions |
| Mean of independent antithetic pair averages | Use SD of pair averages and number of pairs |
| VaR, a loss quantile | No; the quantile estimator needs its own uncertainty analysis |
| One deterministic Sobol sequence’s average | No IID point-based SE justification |

### When to use it

Choose and validate an interval for the actual statistic and sampling design. Predetermine a reporting budget, or use a sequential procedure with its own coverage justification; repeatedly stopping when an ordinary interval looks narrow does not automatically preserve its nominal coverage. A small sampling interval says little about whether the financial model, parameters or numerical approximation are adequate.

## Antithetic pairs help only when the paired payoffs offset

For $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$, both $U$ and $1-U$ have the same marginal distribution. Let $Y=f(U)$, $Y'=f(1-U)$, with common variance $v$ and payoff correlation $\rho$. One independent pair produces

$$A=\frac{Y+Y'}2,\qquad E[A]=E[Y],\qquad\operatorname{Var}(A)=\frac{v(1+\rho)}2$$

To compare **equal payoff-evaluation cost**, allocate $N=2m$ evaluations either to $N$ IID payoffs or to $m$ independent pair averages:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_{\mathrm{IID}})=\frac vN,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_{\mathrm{anti}})=\frac{v(1+\rho)}N$$

Negative payoff correlation helps, zero gives no equal-cost variance gain, and positive correlation hurts. Mirroring inputs does not guarantee the required sign after a nonlinear payoff. [MIT presents the independent pair average and its variance conditions](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-450-analytics-of-finance-fall-2010/4fa033082ff5ee58722a67fe81f0dce7_MIT15_450F10_lec03.pdf).

### A monotone example with checkable moments

For $f(U)=e^U$, the target is $\mu=e-1\approx1.71828183$. Direct integration gives

$$v=\frac{e^2-1}{2}-(e-1)^2\approx0.24203561$$

Since $e^Ue^{1-U}=e$,

$$\operatorname{Cov}(Y,Y')=e-(e-1)^2\approx-0.23421061$$

Thus the equal-cost variance ratio is $(v+\operatorname{Cov})/v\approx0.03232993$, not a universal one-half. At **1,000 evaluations**, IID population SE is about **0.01555749**; 500 independent mirrored pairs have SE about **0.00279732**. Calculate the sample SE from **500 pair averages**, not by pretending the 1,000 paired payoffs are independent.

### A symmetric payoff makes the same trick worse

Change to $f(U)=(U-1/2)^2$. Then $f(1-U)=f(U)$: the second evaluation duplicates the first payoff. Its mean is $1/12$ and variance is $1/180$. With 1,000 total evaluations,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_{\mathrm{IID}})=\frac{1}{180000},\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_{\mathrm{anti}})=\frac{1}{90000}$$

The antithetic estimator has **twice the variance** and $\sqrt2$ times the SE at this cost. Switch the visual to the symmetric payoff to see repeated estimates spread out. A particular run can still land closer by luck; the variance statement concerns repeated runs.

### When to use it

Test the covariance of the actual paired outputs and compare error against computational cost. A reflected financial path needs the same model law, including any transformed shocks. Random-number reuse may save some setup cost, but two payoff evaluations are not automatically free. Keep pair averages as the independent units for error estimation.

## A control variate needs a correct known mean and a coefficient

A control uses a related random output $X$ whose expectation $m_X$ is known **under the same model and probability measure**. With a coefficient $\beta$ fixed independently of production draws,

$$\hat\mu_{\mathrm{cv}}=\bar Y-\beta(\bar X-m_X)$$

It remains unbiased for $E[Y]$ when the known mean is correct. For IID pairs with finite second moments and $\operatorname{Var}(X)>0$,

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y-\beta X)=v_Y+\beta^2v_X-2\beta\operatorname{Cov}(Y,X)$$

$$\beta^*=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y,X)}{v_X},\qquad v_{\mathrm{cv}}^*=v_Y(1-\rho_{YX}^2)$$

The sign of $\beta$ matters: a negatively correlated control can help with a negative coefficient. High correlation alone does not validate an arbitrary coefficient. A zero-variance control contains no stochastic error to remove.

### Correct and incorrect controls for the same exponential target

Use $Y=e^U$, $X=U$, $m_X=1/2$, $v_X=1/12$. Integration gives $\operatorname{Cov}(e^U,U)=(3-e)/2\approx0.14085909$, so

$$\beta^*=6(3-e)\approx1.69030903$$

$$v_{\mathrm{cv}}^*\approx0.00394022,\qquad\mathrm{SE}_{N=1000}\approx0.00198500$$

The control U is already available as an input in this toy, so its extra evaluation cost is negligible here. That cost advantage is not universal for a second option payoff or an estimated control price.

A coefficient of 4 gives per-output variance about **0.44849625**, worse than the original 0.24203561. Using the optimal coefficient but a wrong assumed control mean of 0.6 creates bias

$$\beta^*(0.6-0.5)\approx0.16903090$$

without changing the sampling variance. The estimator now centres near **1.88731273**, rather than the target 1.71828183; a narrow sampling interval does not repair it.

### When to use it

Verify the control expectation under the production model, measure and discounting convention. A fixed pilot estimate of $\beta$ from an independent training sample preserves conditional unbiasedness when $m_X$ is exact, but the pilot costs compute and the coefficient may be inefficient. Fitting it on the production sample can introduce finite-sample bias and changes the inference problem. [MIT gives the fixed-control formula and an independent two-stage pilot approach](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-450-analytics-of-finance-fall-2010/4fa033082ff5ee58722a67fe81f0dce7_MIT15_450F10_lec03.pdf). Compare total cost and validated estimator variance, not just payoff correlation.

## Stratification uses known stratum weights

**Stratified sampling** divides the input distribution into regions with known probabilities and samples within each. For strata $h$ with probabilities $p_h$,

$$\hat\mu=\sum_h p_h\bar Y_h$$

For $U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $Y=U$, take one independent sample in each half. Each within-half variance is $(1/2)^2/12=1/48$. The equally weighted estimator has variance

$$\left(\frac12\right)^2\frac1{48}+\left(\frac12\right)^2\frac1{48}=\frac1{96}$$

Two IID draws have mean variance $(1/12)/2=1/24$. This particular stratified design cuts variance to one quarter at the same two evaluations.

| Region | Probability weight | One illustrated draw | Weighted contribution |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Lower half | 0.5 | 0.1 | 0.05 |
| Upper half | 0.5 | 0.8 | 0.40 |
| Sum | 1 | | Estimate 0.45 |

### When to use it

Use known probability weights and feasible conditional sampling. Unequal-probability strata must not be averaged equally merely because they receive the same sample count. The allocation of samples across strata affects variance and cost; a useful partition depends on where the target output varies.

## Importance sampling changes the law and reweights the output

**Importance sampling** draws from a proposal density $q$ instead of the target density $p$, then corrects the output:

$$E_p[f(X)]=E_q\!\left[f(X)\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right]$$

The proposal must cover every region where $f(x)p(x)$ contributes, the likelihood ratio must be computable, and finite-variance SE needs a finite second moment of the **weighted output**. A poor proposal can increase variance or make it infinite. [Owen derives these support and moment conditions](https://artowen.su.domains/mc/Ch-var-is.pdf).

As an invented arithmetic check, an event has target probability 0.01 but proposal probability 0.5. The weighted indicator is 0.02 on an event and zero otherwise:

| Under the proposal | Proposal probability | Indicator × likelihood ratio |
|---|---:|---:|
| Event | 0.5 | 1 × (0.01 / 0.5) = 0.02 |
| No event | 0.5 | 0 |

Its expectation is $0.5(0.02)=0.01$, and variance $0.5(0.02)^2-0.01^2=0.0001$, versus 0.0099 for the target Bernoulli indicator. Here the probabilities are supplied to check the weights; in a useful integration problem the event integral is unknown even though pointwise density ratios can be evaluated. Counting proposal events without weights would estimate 0.5 instead of 0.01.

### When to use it

Choose a proposal for the output being estimated and inspect weight tails and repeated-run stability. Oversampling a tail is not enough: the correction defines the target, and unstable weighted outputs invalidate an ordinary finite-variance interval. Importance sampling generally changes the variance constant; it does not automatically replace root-count convergence with a faster rate.

## Randomized QMC measures uncertainty across randomizations

**Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC)** uses structured low-discrepancy points rather than IID points. Suitable integrands and dimensions can permit better rates, but the benefit is conditional; discontinuity, effective dimension and construction choices matter. Sobol constructions also have sample-size rules, commonly powers of two. [SciPy describes these conditions and the distinction between deterministic and randomized QMC](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/stats.qmc.html).

A single deterministic sequence does not provide an IID sampling distribution by treating its points as independent. With **randomized QMC**, create independent scrambles, compute one mean $Z_r$ per scramble, and estimate uncertainty across those means:

$$\bar Z=\frac1R\sum_r Z_r,\qquad\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\bar Z)=\frac{s_Z}{\sqrt R}$$

For 16 independent scrambles, each with 1,024 evaluations, total cost is **16,384 evaluations**. Suppose their mean is 1.718 and their between-scramble SD is 0.008. SE is $0.008/4=0.002$. If an approximately normal replicate-mean analysis is justified, a t-based interval with 15 degrees of freedom uses about 2.131:

$$1.718\pm2.131(0.002)=[1.713738,1.722262]$$

| Measurement | Relevant count |
|---|---:|
| Total function evaluations | 16,384 |
| Independent randomized block estimates | 16 |
| Count in the between-block SE denominator | Square root of 16 |

### When to use it

Assess randomization and interval quality at the **replicate** level and respect the construction’s point counts. [SciPy’s QMC integration documentation uses independent scrambled estimates and a t-based interval across them](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.integrate.qmc_quad.html). QMC does not simply shrink the ordinary IID per-point sample variance; it changes the joint sampling design. A finite-size comparison should include both total cost and the resulting estimator error.

## More paths do not remove a fixed discretization or model error

There are several targets to distinguish: a numerical approximation’s mean, the intended mathematical model’s mean, and a financially appropriate model or benchmark. For a fixed comparison target $\theta$, finite mean squared error obeys

$$E[(\hat\mu-\theta)^2]=\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu)+(E[\hat\mu]-\theta)^2$$

If per-output SD is 100, $M=10{,}000$ gives SE 1. With a **hypothetical fixed bias of 3**, RMSE is $\sqrt{1+9}=\sqrt{10}\approx3.1623$. At one million samples, SE falls to 0.1 but RMSE is $\sqrt{0.01+9}\approx3.0017$. A financial model’s real discrepancy is generally unknown; the visual’s bias is supplied for this illustration, not estimated by the simulation.

### A time-step error has a different lever

Consider an invented GBM with $dS=0.10S\,dt+0.20S\,dW$, $S_0=100$, over one year. Its exact terminal **mean level**, not log-growth rate, is $100e^{0.10}\approx110.51709181$. With $L$ equal Euler–Maruyama steps and independent zero-mean shocks, the numerical terminal mean is

$$E[S_1^{\mathrm{Euler}}]=100\left(1+\frac{0.10}{L}\right)^L$$

| Euler steps per path | Numerical terminal mean | Difference from the exact GBM mean |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 110.000000 | −0.517092 |
| 2 | 110.250000 | −0.267092 |
| 4 | 110.381289 | −0.135803 |
| 12 | 110.471307 | −0.045785 |

More independent paths at **one fixed step** estimate 110 more precisely; refining the step changes the numerical target. GBM has an exact step available, so this Euler example diagnoses discretization rather than recommending it. [Higham distinguishes numerical SDE approximation and weak convergence](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/S0036144500378302).

### When to use it

Budget separately for sampling error, discretization, parameter uncertainty and model adequacy. In the ideal IID mean problem, finite sampling does not itself bias the sample mean; other estimators, such as sample quantiles or fitted-control procedures, can have finite-sample bias that changes with sample size. Therefore “more paths never reduce any bias” is too broad. The precise claim is that more paths **with the model, estimator target and discretization held fixed do not correct a fixed target discrepancy**. Refinement, validated estimators and alternative models address different errors.

## Rebuild the error report

Name the target and estimator, count independent groups and evaluations separately, and report a validated uncertainty measure. Compare variance-reduction methods at equal total cost and check their conditions. Then test what sampling cannot diagnose: numerical refinement, calibration uncertainty and the financial assumptions themselves.
