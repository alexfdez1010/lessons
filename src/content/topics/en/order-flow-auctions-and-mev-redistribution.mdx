Suppose a searcher will pay \$4 for the right to back-run an order. An auction can return part of that payment to the user, but the refund alone does not show whether execution improved: the filled price, fees and alternative routes also count.

This course compares redistribution mechanisms through who sees an order, who competes to fill it and who receives the resulting value. It builds on the MEV supply chain and atomic arbitrage:

- **The redistribution turn** — how mechanisms allocate transaction-created value among users, searchers, builders and proposers.
- **Order-flow auctions (OFAs)** — sell the right to back-run your transaction in a sealed auction, then allocate the winning bid according to the mechanism's refund rules. The "redistribute" verb made concrete, with the refund-split arithmetic worked out.
- **Intents & solvers** — stop signing *transactions* and start signing *outcomes*; let a competitive field of solvers bid to fill your intent best. UniswapX as a Dutch auction over your trade.
- **Batch auctions & coincidence of wants** — CoW Protocol's uniform clearing price, peer-to-peer matching that can avoid an AMM route, and the conditions under which batch settlement improves execution.
- **MEV-Share & SUAVE** — programmable privacy that reveals just enough of your order to be useful, and the decentralized-sequencing endgame that tries to make the whole auction layer credibly neutral.
- **Mechanism design & the hard tradeoffs** — price improvement versus solver centralization, censorship, trust assumptions, and how to tell a real redistribution mechanism from a rebranded toll booth.
