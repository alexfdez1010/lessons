If a combination of shares and cash reproduces an option's payoff in every state of a model, the two portfolios should have the same price within that model. Otherwise a trader could buy the cheaper one and sell the more expensive one. This replication argument is the starting point for no-arbitrage pricing.

The course builds from a two-state tree to Black–Scholes, then uses market prices to show where the model's assumptions become restrictive:

- **Why a payoff isn't a price** — why real-world expected payoffs need a treatment of risk before discounting.
- **No-arbitrage & replication** — rebuild the payoff from stock and cash, and the option must cost exactly what the recipe costs, or free money appears.
- **Risk-neutral valuation & the binomial tree** — a model simple enough to run with a pen, which converges to Black–Scholes under the appropriate assumptions.
- **The Black–Scholes formula** — where it comes from, what its assumptions buy and cost, and the meaning hiding in `d₁` and `d₂` including `N(d₂)` as a risk-neutral probability in the standard model.
- **The Greeks** — delta, gamma, theta, vega and rho: how a position breathes as the world moves, and how to **hedge** it.
- **Implied volatility, smile & skew** — run the formula *backwards*, find a different vol at every strike, and see why that curve admits Black–Scholes is only an approximation.

- **State prices, probabilities and uniqueness** — construct dated unit-state claims, normalize q without double discounting, finance every leg of replication, connect physical expectations through state discount factors and identify the information missing when traded payoffs do not span every state.

The European formula lesson matches compounding and dividend conventions, separates physical drift, pricing probability and spot delta, and calculates zero-time/zero-volatility boundaries explicitly. Its theta examples retain the rate/dividend assumptions rather than assigning one sign to every long option.
