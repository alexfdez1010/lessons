A model can pass a historical test and fail after deployment because the data changes, its signal becomes crowded, or another participant exploits its behaviour. These are different failure modes: **distribution shift** need not be an attack, while an **adversarial attack** deliberately targets the model.

Building on Machine Learning for Alpha and Deep Learning for Market Data, this course separates those mechanisms, tests their consequences and examines what each defence can actually guarantee.

- **Markets are adversarial** — the security mindset; how strategic responses can change the data, and how an explicit threat model defines the robustness claim being tested.
- **Adversarial examples on trading models** — the FGSM/PGD lineage of tiny crafted input perturbations that flip a classifier, reframed for market features: how a few basis points of engineered noise in your inputs can flip a position, and why high-dimensional linear-ish models are so fragile.
- **Data poisoning & model extraction** — an adversary who nudges the *training* data your model learns from (spoofing and layering to bait a signal), backdoor triggers, and the inference-time attacks — model extraction and membership inference — against a deployed strategy whose behavior leaks through its fills.
- **Distribution shift & concept drift** — the non-malicious half: covariate shift vs label shift vs concept drift, how a strategy can decay after deployment (changing conditions, adaptation and crowding), and the statistics of drift detection.
- **The robustness toolkit** — adversarial training, distributionally-robust optimization (the min-max objective), regularization and ensembling for stability, and conformal prediction and the assumptions or adaptations needed when the data distribution shifts.
- **Adversarial stress evaluation** — replacing the i.i.d. test set with a worst-case opponent; why robustness claims are easy to fake, how to red-team your own strategy, and the honest scorecard that survives a hostile referee.
