A merchant needs capital for a voyage that may fail. A city needs funds before taxes arrive. A lender needs a record of what is owed. Financial history connects such problems to credit, bookkeeping, shares, bonds and risk-sharing arrangements.

Building on the history of money, the course follows selected developments from ancient lending to modern funds. Each case asks what the arrangement allowed people to do and what obligations or risks it created; an early documented example is not always the first invention.

- **Ancient credit and the first banks** — interest before coins, the Code of Hammurabi's interest caps, Mesopotamian grain loans, temple banks, the Italian merchant banks that gave us *double-entry bookkeeping* and the *bill of exchange*.
- **The first bonds and public debt** — Venice's *prestiti*, the leap from "the king borrows personally" to "the *state* borrows," and the perpetual annuity that never repays its principal.
- **Shares and the first stock exchange** — the Dutch East India Company (VOC, 1602), the development of *permanent, tradable shares* and investor liability arrangements, and the Amsterdam exchange where they were first bought and sold.
- **Bubbles and the first derivatives** — tulip speculation and early derivatives, and Dojima’s organised rice-futures market, where you trade a thing before it exists.
- **Insurance, central banks, and modern products** — Lloyd’s coffeehouse and the development of marine insurance, the Bank of England (1694) and what a central bank is *for*, and the climb from mutual funds to index funds to ETFs.
