A model sees thousands of overlapping market windows. That does not make them thousands of independent examples: many contain the same observations, and the relationships can change over time. Adding parameters can improve representation while also increasing the opportunity to fit noise.

Building on Machine Learning for Alpha and Time-Series Finance, this course compares sequence architectures and their information windows. Each model is judged against simpler alternatives using evaluation that respects time, overlap and repeated experimentation.

- **Why deep learning struggles in finance** — distinguish estimator-specific information from raw row counts, examine representation and limited-signal estimation, and account for architecture selection before increasing capacity.
- **Recurrent models: RNN, LSTM & GRU** — reading a sequence one step at a time, the unrolling mental model, the **vanishing-gradient problem** that cripples plain RNNs, and how the **LSTM/GRU gating** highway lets information survive a long window.
- **Temporal convolutional networks** — treating a return series like a 1-D signal: **causal, dilated convolutions** that see far back with few parameters, their trade-offs against RNNs on financial sequences, and the receptive-field arithmetic.
- **Attention & transformers for markets** — self-attention as "who looks at whom," how direct connections across time differ from a recurrent hidden state, transformers on returns and on **limit-order-book microstructure** (the DeepLOB lineage), and the data-hunger that punishes them on small samples.
- **Embeddings for categorical & alternative data** — turning tickers, sectors, regimes, calendars and news into dense learned vectors, how **entity embeddings** compare with one-hot encodings, and the leakage traps that embeddings make especially easy to fall into.
- **Training and evaluating without fooling yourself** — explicit dependence and as-of label availability, regularisation and correlated ensembles, search-adjusted evidence, fair baseline comparisons, prediction loss versus net cash results and monitoring with uncertainty.
