A one-day 99% VaR of \$4.2 million is a loss threshold from a specified distribution: the model puts roughly 1% of outcomes beyond it. It does not say how severe those tail losses can be. Expected Shortfall asks a different question by averaging losses in the tail.

This course calculates both measures, compares the assumptions behind three estimation methods and checks the forecasts against observed exceptions:

- **What VaR actually says** — its horizon, probability level and loss threshold, and the tail severity it leaves unspecified.
- **Historical simulation** — replay the past to read the loss straight off the data.
- **Parametric / variance–covariance** — assume a bell curve and get VaR with algebra.
- **Monte Carlo** — simulate thousands of synthetic tomorrows, each with its own assumptions and failure modes.
- **Time scaling** — stretch VaR across horizons with the square-root-of-time rule, and spot when it breaks.
- **Expected Shortfall (CVaR)** — the tail-loss mean under a stated definition, answering *when you breach the line, how bad is it on average?*
- **Backtesting** — count the days reality punched through, run the **Kupiec test** and the **Basel traffic light**, and confront the **fat tails** that humbled VaR in 2008.
