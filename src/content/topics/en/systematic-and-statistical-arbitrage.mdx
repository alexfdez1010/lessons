Two related shares usually move together, but their estimated spread has widened. A pairs strategy buys one and sells the other, expecting convergence. It can still lose if the relationship changes, borrowing becomes expensive or both positions become hard to trade.

Statistical arbitrage uses estimated relationships rather than a guaranteed price identity. Building on time series, factors and execution, the course follows a signal into a portfolio and tests how costs and crowding can erase its expected edge:

- **The relative-value mindset** — what "arbitrage" really means on a spectrum from risk-free to purely statistical, why a *market-neutral* spread is a bet on a relationship rather than a direction, and how win rate, payoff size, dependence and costs determine whether repeated trades have value.
- **Pairs trading & cointegration** — the original stat-arb trade: why *correlation is not enough* and **cointegration** is the real prize, how to build a stationary spread, standardize it into a **z-score**, and trade the ±2σ bands with a half-life you can measure.
- **Mean reversion vs momentum** — the two great signal families, why they are mirror images, where each one works (and fails), the cross-sectional vs time-series distinction, and how their results depend on the regime and horizon.
- **Building a market-neutral book** — turning a raw signal into a tradable portfolio: **dollar-neutral** vs **beta-neutral** vs **factor-neutral**, hedging out the market and the style factors to target residual returns while retaining estimation and basis risk, and why neutrality is a choice with costs.
- **Signal combination & decay** — no single alpha is enough: the **information coefficient**, combining weak-but-independent signals into a strong one, and the brutal arithmetic of **alpha decay** versus **turnover cost** that sets your optimal rebalance speed.
- **Capacity, crowding & the quant quake** — why every stat-arb strategy has a hard size ceiling, what happens when everyone discovers the same trade, and a forensic walk through **August 2007**, when crowded, levered, market-neutral books all unwound at once and "impossible" 25-sigma days happened three days running.
